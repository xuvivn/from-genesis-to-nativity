# Book of Job

---

## Chapter I: The Upright Man of Uz and the Day Calamity Struck

**1.** In the land of **Uz** lived a man named **Job**, blameless and upright in character, revering justice and turning away from every evil. He was blessed with a warm family of seven sons and three daughters, and vast abundance surpassing all in the East—thousands of sheep, camels, yoke of oxen, and donkeys. Whenever his children gathered for a feast, early the next morning he faithfully offered prayers for each one, lest any should have faltered unwittingly in their heart.

**2.** Yet one day a storm of fierce trial crashed down upon his life. Within a single day, breathless lone survivors came running one after another with tidings of disaster: raiders slaughtered his field servants and carried off the oxen and donkeys; fire fell from the sky consuming both flocks and shepherds; northern war bands swept away all the camels; and last of all, a howling desert gale struck the house where his ten children were feasting, bringing down the roof so all perished together.

**3.** Hearing one shattering blow after another, Job rose, tore his mourning cloak, shaved his head, and fell to the ground uttering words of noble surrender: *"Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return; the One who gave and the One who has taken away—blessed be His name forever!"* In all this crushing sorrow, Job spoke no word of accusation or bitterness.

---

## Chapter II: The Trial of Sickness and the Long Debate in the Night

**1.** Nor did the trial end with outward loss: soon afterward, Job's entire body broke out in **agonizing boils from the soles of his feet to the crown of his head**. Forced to sit out among the ashes, he scraped his stinging sores with a broken piece of pottery. Even his own wife, seeing his hopeless agony, wept in pity and urged him to curse his fate and die; yet Job answered steadily: *"Shall we receive good gladly and refuse to endure adversity?"*

**2.** Hearing of their friend's ruin, three longtime companions—Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar—agreed to come and comfort him. Catching sight of Job from afar, so wasted and scarred they scarcely knew him, all three wept aloud and **sat silently on the ground beside him for seven days and seven nights**, speaking not a single word because they saw how immense was his grief.

**3.** When at last Job broke the silence to lament the night of his sorrow, a long and weary debate unfolded: his friends insisted stubbornly that anyone struck by such misery must surely be hiding some secret sin that brought down punishment. Yet Job held fiercely to the clear testimony of his conscience—neither denying his agony nor consenting to lie and confess false guilt merely to appease his friends, pleading instead to stand face to face with truth itself.

---

## Chapter III: The Unbroken Confession and the Voice from the Whirlwind

**1.** In the deepest solitude—doubted by his friends and worn near to death in body—from the abyss of Job's despair flashed a radiant spark of trust reaching beyond death itself: *"For I know that **my Redeemer lives**, and at the last He shall stand upon the earth! Though my flesh be wasted and torn away, yet with my very own eyes—and not another's—I shall behold Him!"*

**2.** At long last, out of the majestic **whirlwind**, the Most High answered—not with rebuke, but with sweeping questions inviting Job to gaze out across the wondrous cosmos: who laid the foundations of the earth while the morning stars sang together? Who shut the sea behind doors, setting bounds to halt its proud waves at the shore? Who gave wings to the soaring hawk, strength to the warhorse, and mystery to the snow, the rain clouds, and the constellations?

**3.** Standing before a grandeur far surpassing frail human understanding, Job bowed his head in deep humility: *"Before this, my ears had only heard reports of You, but now my own eyes have seen You!"* Then the Most High affirmed that Job's honest words spoke truer than his three friends' rigid arguments; and when Job prayed for peace upon the very friends who had misjudged him, his fortunes were restored twofold: kinsfolk and old friends gathered once more around his hearth, seven more sons and three daughters fairer than any in the land were born to him, and he lived in quiet peace for another hundred and forty years, beholding four generations of his descendants.
