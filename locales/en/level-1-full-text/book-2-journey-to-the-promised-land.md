# Book II: Journey to the Promised Land
*(Full Text Edition — Ages 9–10)*

---

## Part I: Abraham

### Chapter 1: King Nimrod

After the Great Flood, Noah's descendants began to multiply across the new world. In those days, everyone spoke the same tongue—the language of Noah. Back then, all who offered sacrifices and prayed beneath the rainbow of the covenant held language to be something sacred. Not a single soul in the new world dared to swear falsely or misuse words.

Once more, humankind grew numerous upon the earth. When Noah and all who had been saved aboard the Ark had passed away, the people began to wish for a king to rule over them.

At that time, there lived a mighty hunter, tall of stature and immensely strong. He wore a long robe stitched from the hides of twelve fierce wild beasts he himself had slain. That hunter's name was Nimrod.

Many said to one another:

"Nimrod deserves to be our king; he is tall and strong, and always defeats every rival in battle."

They thronged before Nimrod and cried out:

"Be our king! Give us your commands, and we will obey!"

When they all bowed low before him, Nimrod drew himself up to his full height and spoke:

"I will be your king, if you carry out everything I command."

His very first command was:

"Build me a palace and a throne set high above the ground."

Throughout the entire land, everyone set eagerly to work. They raised up a palace and a vast city surrounding it. The throne was carved from stone, resting upon four pillars aligned exactly with the four corners of the sky.

Yet after only seven days, Nimrod declared:

"This throne is far too low for me."

So the craftsmen set atop the stone throne a wooden throne carved with figures of many wild beasts. Nimrod sat upon that wooden throne and ruled.

After one month, he said again:

"This wood is too soft for me!"

The metalworkers promptly cast an iron throne, placed it atop the wooden one, and built stairs behind it for the king to climb. Nimrod sat upon the iron throne and ruled.

After half a year, he remarked:

"This throne is much too dark!"

So the coppersmiths cast him a throne of copper, rubbed its surface with fine sand until the metal glowed fiery red, and placed it atop the iron throne. Nimrod was pleased and sat upon it to rule for an entire year.

One day, irritated over some small matter, the king roared:

"This throne is far too red for me!"

At once the silversmiths forged a throne of silver and polished it until it gleamed like the moon in the night sky. Nimrod climbed up to the silver throne and ruled.

Two years later, he proclaimed:

"I prefer a throne of pure gold!"

The king's servants had to gather gold from far and near. Goldsmiths cast a throne that shone as brilliantly as the sun without even needing polish. Once again the builders had to raise the stairs higher for the king to climb up. Quietly they murmured to one another:

"Surely he is satisfied now! The throne cannot possibly be built any higher, or his head will bump right against the ceiling."

Nimrod sat upon that throne of pure gold and ruled. Anyone entering the royal court had to prostrate himself flat against the floor and shout at the top of his lungs just to reach the king's ears far above.

Three years later, Nimrod issued another command:

"Let the goldsmiths stud my golden throne with precious stones, so that it sparkles like the starry sky!"

By the time Nimrod sat atop a throne that could be made neither higher nor grander, other thoughts began to stir inside his head.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Rising rhythm of the seven-tiered throne:** Read each time marker clearly (*seven days, one month, half a year, one year, two years, three years*), steadily raising the impatience and arrogance in Nimrod's voice with each new material.
- **Quiet pause at the chapter's end:** Slow down on the final sentence—where the king sits atop his gem-crusted throne that can go no higher—to spark curiosity for the next chapter.

### Chapter 2: The Tower of Babel

In his sleep, King Nimrod often had strange dreams. Those dreams were sent by a fallen evil spirit—the dark spirit named Sandalphon. He crept close to Nimrod's ear and whispered:

"Build a tower high enough to touch the dome of heaven. Place my statue inside the tower, and I will shield your city from rains of fire and floodwaters. Fire will never harm the tower, and whenever the waters rise, you need only climb up the tower to escape."

The evil spirit whispered many other schemes into the king's ear as well.

Nimrod dreamed of climbing the tower with his warriors to attack the heavenly world. His soldiers loosed arrows up into the air, only to see those arrows fall straight back down, stained with blood. He also had one gentler dream: he saw a great beam of light shine down upon the tower and branch outward toward all four corners of the earth.

Dreaming such dreams, Nimrod thought to himself:

*"This tower will bring me power and glory even greater than my throne."*

He summoned all the master builders in the realm before him and commanded:

"Build me a tower reaching right up to the sky! I give you authority to drive the entire population to work: men, women, children, and the elderly alike must knead clay and bake bricks day and night. Whoever refuses to work shall be put to death!"

Royal officials proclaimed Nimrod's decree, calling the people to labor. Loudly they announced:

"Whoever helps build the tower will gain great reward, and one day may climb straight up into heaven itself!"

Day and night, fires blazed endlessly inside the brick kilns so the kneaded clay could be baked hard into bricks for the tower.

The people of the whole land grew obsessed with the thought of the tower. If someone accidentally dropped a brick and broke it in two, they wept bitterly over the loss. Yet if a worker slipped and fell from the tower to his death, they only laughed in mockery and tossed his body into the nearest brick kiln.

Nimrod commanded:

"In the heart of the tower, raise a stone pedestal. Set upon it the statue of the god Sandalphon. From far and near, everyone must gaze upon it and revere it!"

So the sculptors set to work carving from stone a colossal head to place atop the tower.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Sandalphon's whisper:** Keep your voice low, slightly raspy, and lingering as the dark spirit tempts Nimrod into building the tower.
- **Chilling contrast:** Read calmly and clearly the detail that people wept over a broken brick yet laughed at a worker falling to his death, letting that callousness speak straight to the listeners' hearts.

### Chapter 3: The Birth of Abram

Among Nimrod's soldiers and courtiers was an officer named Terah. His wife gave birth to a baby boy, and Terah named his son Abram. On the very night the child was born, the officer invited King Nimrod's magicians and astrologers to his house. He wanted them to read the stars in the sky at the hour of his boy's birth so he could foretell what the child's future life would hold.

At the moment the baby was born, the astrologers beheld across the night sky a great shooting star blaze brightly and shatter into countless smaller stars. The stargazers were filled with astonishment. They said to Terah:

"This boy will grow exceedingly mighty and become the forefather of a great nation!"

Returning to Nimrod's palace at midnight, they conferred together:

"We had better report this birth to King Nimrod. If we keep it secret and the king finds out, every one of us will surely lose his life."

The next day, those same counselors appeared before Nimrod's throne. They prostrated themselves, pressing their foreheads to the ground. Their spokesman declared:

"Long live the King forever! Tonight, Your Majesty, a son was born to your officer Terah. He invited us to his house. At the hour of the child's birth, we lifted our eyes to the night sky and beheld a shooting star blaze brilliantly and burst into many smaller stars. Terah's son is destined to become great and powerful—the father or king of a nation!"

Nimrod asked:

"Could he become a danger to me?"

The chief astrologer replied:

"Calamity may very well come upon you from this child. If it pleases the King, pay the father a sum of money, and we will slay the infant—before he has time to grow dangerous."

Nimrod summoned Terah and told him:

"I have heard that last night your wife bore a baby boy. At his birth, strange omens appeared in the sky. Hand the boy over to me, so that I may do away with him before misfortune falls upon me on his account. I will pay you handsomely in gold and silver."

Terah knew well that Nimrod never tolerated defiance. He answered:

"All that belongs to me, I place into the King's hands. Grant your servant only three days' grace, so that I may settle the King's command decently with my household."

The king agreed and gave Terah three days to surrender the boy.

As soon as he reached home, Terah told his wife and trusted household servants of Nimrod's command. All were plunged into fear and grief.

On the morning of the third day, Nimrod's soldiers pounded on Terah's door demanding the child. On that very night when Abram had been born, a servant woman in the household had also borne a son. Terah felt forced to hand the servant woman's baby boy over to the king's soldiers. Nimrod dashed the infant violently against his stone throne, killing the child on the spot.

Meanwhile, Terah had secretly led his wife and baby Abram, together with a nursemaid, to a rocky cave on the outskirts of the city. From time to time he brought food to this hiding place. To avoid raising suspicion, a few days later the mother returned home to Terah's house, leaving the nursemaid in the cave with the boy.

Yet the fact that Abram still lived did not escape the eyes of the dark spirit Sandalphon. He sent Nimrod a dream. In the dream, a young boy appeared before the king; right before the throne, the boy grew tall like a tree and toppled the seven-tiered throne crashing to the ground. At that very instant, a cold, sharp voice whispered:

"Nimrod, beware: the boy Abram is still alive. From him, danger threatens you!"

Nimrod started awake, that voice still ringing in his ears. He ordered his men to slay every newborn boy one month old or younger throughout the city. His henchmen swung their cruel, bloodstained swords and carried out the order.

Even so, Abram's hiding place stayed hidden from the dark spirit Sandalphon, for the dazzling radiance of Archangel Gabriel shone all around the child. Shielded by that light, Sandalphon could neither see him nor find him, though he scoured the entire land.

From time to time, Abram's mother visited the cave, bringing food and clothing. Still the dark spirit Sandalphon found no peace. He knew Abram lived, though he knew not where. Time and again he appeared in Nimrod's dreams and whispered:

"Abram still lives, and he will overthrow your throne unless you destroy him completely! Send your soldiers to hunt down his hiding place!"

Once again, Nimrod dispatched soldiers to search far and wide for Abram and bring him back alive before the king. In that way, this time he meant to be certain he killed the right child.

Outside the mouth of the cave, young Abram was playing with little sticks and pebbles. Suddenly he spotted a band of soldiers down in the valley climbing the footpath up the hill. All at once, a strong humming sound rang through the air, and Abram hurried back inside the cave.

Outside, a thick cloud of mist swept down and wrapped tightly around the soldiers, leaving them utterly lost. They scattered, crashed headlong into one another, and tripped over each other's feet, tumbling in heaps, until—panic-stricken by this eerie wonder—they turned and bolted straight back down into the valley. Only when every last soldier reached the plain below did the mist clear away, and the troop had to return empty-handed to Nimrod.

When Abram grew old enough to walk, the angel Gabriel took him by the hand and led him far away to old Shem—Noah's son—who was living a quiet, hidden life. There, Abram grew up under Shem's care and was nurtured in his wisdom. The angel gave Abram's mother a peaceful sign to let her know he had taken the boy into his loving protection. With his radiant power he comforted her, and she quietly told Terah the wondrous story of their son's rescue.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Clear shifts in mood:** Keep an anxious, heavy tone as Terah asks for three days and the babies are switched; then let the voice brighten gently as Gabriel's radiance wraps around the cave.
- **The cloud of mist:** Read vividly the humming sound in the air and the soldiers bumping into one another and scrambling pell-mell down the hill.
- **Warm ending:** Gently emphasize Gabriel's hand guiding Abram to elder Shem, carrying forward the living tradition of Noah.

### Chapter 4: Abram and Nimrod

Years passed, and Abram grew from a boy into a man. When elder Shem saw that the time had come, Abram returned to the city of Babel to seek out Nimrod. Like an unknown stranger, Abram walked into the palace courtyard. There, Nimrod had set up many stone idols, forcing the people to pray before them.

Abram thought to himself:

*"If I ask to be brought before the King, they will mock me and drive me away. Instead, if I knock over one of these wicked stone statues, it will cause a great uproar and they will drag me straight to Nimrod. Then I will be able to speak with him."*

Beside one of the statues, a stonecutter was carving symbols into stone. Seeing no one watching, Abram snatched up the hammer and knocked the statue's head clean off its body. He placed the hammer right into the statue's stone hand and cried out to the terrified crowd rushing up:

"If he is a god, let him strike me dead!"

Alarmed by the shouting, guards came running. They seized Abram and marched him into the palace to receive punishment from Nimrod. The guards reported the stranger's crime to the king and brought him at once before the royal presence.

In fury, Nimrod thundered:

"Who are you to dare do such a thing?"

The stranger answered:

"I am Abram, son of Terah!"

Nimrod turned pale and demanded:

"Why have you done harm to my gods? Have you gone mad, or have you been deceived?"

Abram asked in return:

"What is your god?"

Nimrod answered:

"Fire is my god!"

Abram said:

"Water quenches fire!"

Nimrod replied:

"We pray to water, too."

Abram said:

"The clouds carry water within them."

Nimrod argued back:

"We pray to the clouds, too."

Abram said:

"The wind scatters the clouds."

Nimrod answered:

"We pray to the wind, too."

Abram said:

"The earth is the home of the wind!"

"We pray to the earth, too!" Nimrod said. Then, flying into a rage, the king leaped to his feet and shouted, "How much longer will you mock me, a king? Yes, we pray to fire, and in the fire you shall die!"

He commanded his men to hurl Abram into a blazing brick kiln, and ordered:

"Stand guard beside the kiln until evening. With my own hands I will come and scatter his ashes to the four winds!"

Palace guards bound Abram's hands and led him out to the city gate, where the brick kilns were roaring with flame. At that hour, the angel Gabriel spoke to the Heavenly Hosts:

"I will go down to the earth and rescue Abram. I will cool the fire for him."

A vast crowd trailed behind as Nimrod's men marched the prisoner through the streets to the place of death by fire. Reaching a fiercely burning kiln, they lifted the iron lid with heavy chains, and Abram—still bound—was flung inside.

Without a moment's delay, the soldiers slammed the lid shut again. Outside, no one could see the miracle unfolding within: the cords binding Abram burned cleanly away, yet all around his body there settled a stream of air so cool that the flames could not touch him. That cool air formed a sheltering dome around him like an inverted bell, so not even a single hair on his head was singed. He knelt down amid the glowing red coals. In prayer, he gave thanks for the deliverance he had received and surrendered his heart to the Holy Will of God.

As evening drew near, Nimrod arrived to scatter Abram's ashes. His servants hooked the chains and hoisted the kiln lid open. The moment the lid was lifted, Abram—completely free of his bonds—stepped calmly out. King Nimrod's knees buckled beneath him, and he collapsed flat on his face to the ground in sheer terror.

From the watching crowd rose cries of amazement:

"Look at Abram! He is untouched and unharmed! He is a holy man!"

As the people fell to their knees before him, Abram spoke:

"It is not before me that you should bow down; kneel before the God of the world, who created us all. It is He who saved me from the flames."

Many listened to Abram's words and from that hour longed to live a better life.

After his wondrous escape, Abram returned to his father's house. From then on, Nimrod left him in peace. From time to time, Abram gathered those who wished to learn from him and taught them from the treasury of his wisdom. Before long, a band of faithful disciples changed their way of life, walked under his guidance, and treasured his words in their hearts.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Crisp rhythm of debate:** Set each exchange between Abram and Nimrod cleanly apart (*Fire – Water – Clouds – Wind – Earth*), contrasting Abram's calm poise with the king's rising anger.
- **A cool dome amid glowing embers:** Lower your voice softly and gently when describing the bell-like shelter of cool air guarding Abram as he kneels in prayer among the red-hot coals.

### Chapter 5: The Confusion of Languages

The Babylonians kept on building their lofty tower—stone piled upon stone, staircase joined to staircase, scaffolding stacked upon scaffolding—reaching ever higher into the sky.

One night, the Voice of the Lord spoke to Abram:

"This evening at sunset, go to the tower. Seventy angels are about to descend from heaven. They will confuse the speech of those building upon the tower, and humankind will scatter across the whole world."

Abram did exactly as he was told. As he drew near the tower, his spiritual eyes beheld radiant figures descending from high heaven. A mighty wind began to howl and roar around the tower's walls.

Suddenly, the workers could no longer understand one another at all. Whoever called for bricks was handed mortar. Whoever asked for a plank was given a stone. Whenever someone shouted, *"Haul it up!"* a whole load came crashing straight down to the ground. A dreadful uproar broke out; bricks and tiles struck workers on their feet, then rained onto their heads. The earth beneath the tower's foundations trembled, and stone walls cracked open with a deafening roar. Gale winds whipped empty pots, tools, and wooden planks flying through the air. A tangle of shouts and curses echoed up from below. Many workers lost their footing and plunged from the tower, dragging others down with them.

When the wind and the clamor died away, all those who spoke the same language naturally drew together into groups upon the wide open ground around the tower. Standing in their midst was Abram. The faithful disciples who had listened to his teachings found him there as well.

With his spiritual sight, Abram saw the seventy angels step beside the seventy families of humankind, each family speaking a language of its own. Abram alone beheld this with the eyes of his spirit, and he knew that from this hour the seventy language families would be led forth across all the earth. From them, seventy nations would be formed according to the Will of God.

The very last angel was Archangel Michael. He drew near to Abram and spoke to him:

"You and your company are granted to keep that ancient tongue. I will lead you out of Babylon into another land, and entrust to you and your people a calling for ages yet to come."

No one ever heard word of King Nimrod again. Perhaps a stone falling from the tower struck him dead. Whatever became of him, his reign had come to an end.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Climax on the chaotic worksite:** Gradually quicken your pace as workers hand out the wrong bricks, mortar, stones, and planks while storm winds hurl tools through the air—then fall suddenly quiet when the wind stops.
- **Mission at the close:** Read slowly and solemnly the words of Archangel Michael entrusting Abram with his calling, and close Nimrod's fate in a calm, steady tone.

### Chapter 6: Abram Journeys to Canaan

Abram and his company set out along the path the angel showed them. With him traveled his wife Sarah, his father Terah, and his nephew Lot, his brother's son. They journeyed across many regions where wild tribes dwelt until they reached the borders of peaceful Canaan. There, the people plowed fields and tended lovely gardens, and the trees hung heavy with sweet fruit. That land was called Haran, rich in fertile fields. Abram paused to dwell there. Later, he and Lot journeyed onward into Canaan. He built an altar so that through sacrifice he might always keep himself and those with him united with the sacred world.

One night, while sleeping in his tent, Abram suddenly awoke. Had someone just called his name? Yes—out of the deep night, the Voice of the Lord rang through his soul and spoke:

"Abram, do not be afraid! Step outside beneath the starry sky. See if you have strength enough to count the stars."

Standing at the tent door beneath thousands upon thousands of gleaming stars, Abram answered:

"O Lord, the sparkling stars are far too many to count!"

The Voice of the Lord replied:

"Look, Abram: just as orderly and countless as the stars in the sky above, so shall your people become, and the generations of your descendants shall be beyond number. As the father of a nation, from this day forward you shall be called Abraham!"

Abraham fell prostrate to the earth, reverently touching his face to the ground. When he lifted his eyes once more, a bright star streaked across the sky, and the Voice of the Lord grew still.

Dawn broke. Abraham carried stones to that very spot and raised an altar, for he held the place where God had spoken to him to be holy ground. From that day on, Abraham offered sacrifices and spoke prayers there. He gathered his household and people to this place to celebrate worship.

Years passed by. Abraham was blessed with large herds of cattle and flocks of sheep. His shepherds always kept their flocks within their own borders, never letting them graze upon another's land. Lot—whose lands bordered Abraham's—also owned sheep, cattle, tents, and servants. Yet Lot's herdsmen were lazy. They let their animals wander and graze wherever they pleased across the countryside. Soon complaints rose on every side against Lot and his household. The herdsmen began to quarrel bitterly with one another.

Abraham's shepherds shouted:

"Watch your herds more carefully, you careless lot!"

Lot's herdsmen shouted back:

"Grass grows green everywhere—and lounging beneath your shade trees is mighty pleasant, you foolish boundary-keepers!"

Since Lot did nothing to correct his workers, Abraham spoke gently with his nephew:

"We must not quarrel among our own family. Go down into the Jordan valley, my nephew. Down there you will find plenty of good land."

Lot agreed and went to settle near the city of Sodom.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A new name beneath the starry sky:** Lower your voice into a warm, spacious hush when reading the Lord's words in the deep night, helping children picture the vast sparkling sky and the shooting star.
- **Quarreling shepherds:** Bring a touch of cheeky stubbornness to the reply of Lot's herdsmen, then shift smoothly back to Abraham's calm, measured voice.

### Chapter 7: Abraham's Tree

Wherever Abraham went, he planted trees. He loved them dearly and often said:

"Trees grow upward from the earth and spread their branches wide above, just like rising sacrificial smoke. Trees are grateful to the light, and the wind carries the song of their leaves across the whole face of the earth."

One day, as he approached a tree beside his tent that he had planted many years earlier, he saw a figure like an angel moving among its leaves.

At that very moment, a traveler walked along the path leading right past the foot of the tree. To Abraham's amazement, several branches suddenly drew back as if to avoid coming too close to that man. He wondered to himself, *"What is there about this man that makes the tree shrink away from him?"* He invited the traveler to share a meal with him, and soon discovered that the guest eating at his side was an idol-worshiper from a foreign land.

Abraham began speaking with him and asked:

"Who made the sun that rises and sets? Who made the stars upon the dome of the sky? Who gave the flowers and grasses their wondrous colors and sweet fragrance? Who set the flocks of birds in the air and the schools of fish in the waters? Who shaped the very first human form and granted us the miracle of speech?"

He told the stranger about the living God, who reveals Himself in every work of creation throughout the world.

Listening, the stranger opened his heart to the thoughts and words of his host. Late that afternoon, as he left Abraham's tent and walked past the tree once more, the tree no longer shrank its branches back, and its leaves spread wide open, as if rejoicing over a soul that had grown brighter and clearer.

Another time, a woman with sorrowful eyes walked past the tree, and it let its branches droop all the way down to the ground. Abraham asked her what was troubling her. It turned out that her only possession—a donkey—had run away. Abraham sent his servants out to search; they found the long-eared donkey and brought it back. When the woman sat riding upon her donkey past the tree, the tree lifted its branches high into the air, and its leaves fluttered joyfully in the breeze.

Once, two servants came before Abraham, quarreling fiercely over a knife. Each insisted the knife belonged to him. Abraham said to the man holding the knife in his hand:

"Go and lay that knife down beneath the tree over there."

When he did so, Abraham saw that the tree's branches remained completely still. He had the knife brought back and handed it to the other servant, telling him to do the same. The instant this second man laid the knife beside the tree, the upper branches began to twist and writhe as though serpents were slithering through them.

Thus Abraham knew that the second servant had lied. When the man came back carrying the knife, Abraham snatched it out of his hand, seized hold of his tunic so fiercely that the cloth tore, and thundered at him:

"Drive falsehood out of your heart at once! Lies poison the very blood in your veins!"

Terrified beyond words, the servant dropped to his knees and begged Abraham for forgiveness. Gently, Abraham told him:

"From this hour on, never tell a lie again! An honest heart is worth more than a knife made of gold!"

Another time, a stranger came to visit Abraham. He rode upon a scrawny, bony camel. As he passed the tree, Abraham saw many of its leaves curl inward and shrivel up like dry claws. *"Aha,"* thought Abraham, *"that man is a miser."* He did not invite the stranger inside his tent. Nevertheless, he gave the camel a brimming basket full of food.

In those days, Abraham had a servant who tended the herds. The fellow loved sitting idle and watching others work, wanting nothing more than to loaf. Whenever he strolled past the tree, a single leaf detached itself, drifted slowly down to the ground, and withered away at once. *"Aha,"* thought Abraham, *"any task at all is too heavy for this lazy fellow!"*

For a long while no rain fell, and the trees and plants began to droop and wilt. Abraham called the lazy servant to him and said:

"I have fine work for you: look how miserably the trees and flowers are suffering from thirst. Take a carrying pole and tie two leather water-skins to it. Fetch water from the well. When you have watered the thirsty plants so they stand tall and turn lush green again, you will make me very happy indeed."

The servant did as he was told. He watered up and down the whole garden, then lay on the ground watching to see whether the plants showed any sign of change. After a little while, one tree and then another slowly lifted its leaves and straightened its stem, and by afternoon many clusters looked far greener. The very next day, the servant grew far more eager and kept hauling water without stopping. Seeing that he himself could revive drooping, wilted trees made him grow truly fond of the work. And when he poured generous water around that special tree as well, it stopped shedding any more leaves.

Abraham praised the water-carrier and told him that trees are grateful whenever someone cares for them. In that way, Abraham always strove to awaken the very best inside every person, and the lazy servant became a devoted gardener.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Cadence for the five short episodes:** Keep a warm voice, pausing briefly between the five scenes around the tree (*the idol-worshiper – the poor woman who lost her donkey – the knife – the miser – the gardener*).
- **Vocal contrast:** Let your voice boom strongly as Abraham rebukes the liar, then soften immediately for *"an honest heart is worth more than a knife made of gold"*; speak kindly and encouragingly as he gives the lazy servant the task of hauling water to save the trees.

### Chapter 8: The Visit of the Three Guests

One day, Abraham stepped outside his tent to warm himself in the sunshine. Far off, he saw three men drawing near, clad in robes of many colors like the rainbow. He wondered to himself, *"What might these noble travelers wish?"* They were three angels come down from heaven. They bore a message for Abraham and had clothed themselves in forms of air and light.

Abraham went out to welcome them. The closer they came, the stranger and more wondrous his heart felt. Their great beauty radiated deep into his soul. He bowed low in reverence and spoke:

"Welcome to my home, my lords! Pray stop and rest as guests beneath my roof!"

The three bowed their heads slightly in thanks for his warm hospitality. Abraham noticed that as the three men walked across the earth, their footsteps made not a single sound. Their voices rang like music. And as Abraham walked beside them past the tree, the tree bowed its branches all the way to the ground in greeting.

The four of them sat down beside the tree to share a meal. Sarah brought out bread, milk, and delicious dishes she had cooked. The guests ate, yet neither the bread nor the food grew any less. Suddenly, the heavenly guest seated in the middle lifted his hand and spoke solemnly:

"Abraham, a little more than a year from now, your wife Sarah shall bear you a son, who will bring you abundant joy."

Inside the tent, Sarah overheard those words and burst out laughing. She thought to herself, *"I am already an old woman. Even in my youth I had no child, so surely at this advanced age I can never bear one now."*

One of the guests asked Abraham:

"Why did Sarah laugh? Is anything too hard for God?"

Having delivered their message, the three men rose to continue on their journey. As they took their leave, one of them spoke of what lay ahead:

"We are traveling onward to the city of Sodom. Many there live in wickedness and godlessness. Their evil deeds rise all the way to heaven like a foul stench. They mock and offend the Word of the Lord. Alas! Judgment may soon fall upon Sodom!"

Abraham grew deeply anxious at those words. He thought to himself, *"Lot lives there too, and surely my nephew is a righteous man."*

A little over a year later, the words of the three guests came true. Sarah gave birth to a baby boy. Abraham named him Isaac—which means *"laughter"*—to remember Sarah's reaction to the prophecy of his birth. Far and wide the news spread from ear to ear that a miracle had happened so Abraham's line might continue and flourish.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Ethereal beauty of the three guests:** Read softly and brightly when describing their rainbow-colored garments, soundless footsteps, musical voices, and the tree bowing in welcome.
- **Shifting mood:** Let a gentle touch of humor slip in as Sarah laughs inside the tent, then drop into anxious solemnity as the angel mentions Sodom's impending judgment.

### Chapter 9: The Destruction of Sodom

What had been happening in Sodom? Evil spirits—who sow dark thoughts in human hearts and stir up insolence and cruelty—had gathered there. Strangers passing through the city were robbed. If merchants traveled from afar to sell their wares, the townsfolk simply grabbed whatever they wanted without paying a single coin. If a merchant tried to stop them, he was jeered at, beaten, or pelted with a hail of stones until he fled the city, counting himself lucky just to escape alive. If a poor beggar came asking for food, they flung filth at him and left him to starve to death. There was no telling all the wicked deeds done in Sodom. Lot had lived for many years with his family in this city, and they too suffered sorely at the hands of Sodom's people.

God had made known to Abraham that His wrath was about to fall upon the city. One night, Abraham cried out to the Lord:

"O Lord, I am worried for my nephew Lot and for anyone there who is still upright and pure of heart. Would You truly destroy them along with the wicked? Perhaps there are still fifty righteous people in the city!"

The Lord answered:

"If fifty righteous people are still found there, I will not destroy the city."

Abraham feared there might not be as many as fifty righteous souls, so he kept pleading down step by step until the Lord spoke to him:

"And if ten righteous people are still found there, I will not destroy them either."

Before long, God sent two angels in human form to see whether even ten righteous people remained in Sodom. As the two approached the city gate, Lot happened to be resting upon the wall. Seeing two men of noble bearing, Lot thought to himself, *"What evil will happen to these strangers inside the city?"* He stepped forward and spoke to them:

"My honored guests, evening is falling fast. Pray spend tonight resting in a room inside my house. At early dawn you may continue on your way."

Already several townsfolk had begun gathering around Lot and the strangers, hatching wicked designs. The two men accepted Lot's invitation. They followed him through the city and entered his home.

Daylight faded into night. The two angels saw the light of righteousness shining nowhere else in the entire city except from Lot's house. Not ten upright souls could be found anywhere in Sodom.

Suddenly, Lot heard loud shouting outside his door. A mob of men had gathered in the street, bellowing:

"Hand those two strangers out to us! We want to have our sport with them!"

Bravely Lot stepped out into the street, trying to drive the savage mob away. Instead they roared louder and threatened to seize him. The door opened. Quickly the two strangers pulled Lot back inside, slammed the door shut, and barred it fast. Out in the street, the ruffians howled like wild beasts, fetching clubs and heavy stones to batter down the door.

Then something wondrous happened! The two angels sent forth an unseen beam of power straight through the wooden door. Before the eyes of every man pounding at the door, all turned pitch black as ink. Just as though struck blind, they groped helplessly around and wept aloud in panic. Soon the courtyard outside Lot's house grew quiet. Meanwhile, other mobs still rampaged wildly throughout the city, committing all manner of evil.

Only then did Lot realize he had sheltered heavenly guests beneath his roof. Just before dawn flushed pink across the sky, the two urged Lot:

"Take your family and flee from this house and this city at once! Before the sun rises, this place will be destroyed!"

Seeing Lot still hesitate, the two angels grasped him by the hand and pulled him away. His wife and two daughters ran behind them. One of the angels warned them sternly:

"No matter what happens behind you, never, ever look back! If you do, terrible doom will overtake you!"

Before long, a thunderous roar tore open the sky. The earth quaked violently; crimson sheets of flame leaped skyward; scorching fire and brimstone rained down from above. Fire from beneath the earth, and fire from the heavens! Sodom and the neighboring city of Gomorrah collapsed into smoking ash and ruin.

At the fiercest moment of booming explosions and blinding flashes, Lot's wife could not hold back her curiosity. She stopped and glanced back behind her, while Lot, his two daughters, and the angels hurried onward. By the time they reached a rocky cave where they could shelter in safety, Lot's wife was no longer with them. She had frozen motionless on the very spot where death overtook her. So the angel of God reported to Lot.

Having rescued Lot and his two daughters, the angels departed. The survivors settled in to spend the night inside the cave after all the terrors of that day.

Though far away, Abraham felt the thunderous rumble and earth-tremor rolling from the direction of Sodom. Early the next morning, he set out to see what had happened. Standing atop a hill overlooking the plain where Sodom and Gomorrah had once stood, he saw nothing left but ash-colored mounds and heaps of rubble. Thick smoke and rising steam still coiled up into the sky. Later, when Lot came to join him, Lot recounted to Abraham everything that had taken place. In years to come, Moses wrote all of these events down.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Abraham's pleading:** Read slowly, earnestly, yet reverently as Abraham pleads with the Lord step by step from *fifty* down to *ten* righteous souls.
- **The night of chaos in Sodom:** Pick up the pace moderately as the mob batters the door and is suddenly struck blind; keep a brisk, urgent tone as the angels hurry Lot's family out before sunrise.
- **Epilogue on the hillside of ashes:** Drop into a calm, reflective hush as Abraham gazes out at gray smoke curling above Sodom and Gomorrah.

## Part II: Isaac

### Chapter 10: The Boy Isaac

Once, young Isaac stood watching his mother Sarah knead dough for bread. He was still a little boy, though he had already lost his baby teeth. Mother Sarah scooped a little dough into a small bowl for him so he could shape a loaf of his very own.

A little while later, she lifted the heavy dough-trough in her strong arms and carried it outdoors. The sunshine was dazzling; cradling his bowl of dough, Isaac trotted along right behind his mother. He stood watching as she set the dough-trough down upon a flat stone and whispered softly. Isaac asked:

"Mother, what are you saying?"

Mother Sarah answered:

"I am letting the sunshine fall upon the bread dough. I am thanking the sun for helping all the tiny grains of wheat grow and warming them until they ripen golden, so they can give our family bread to eat."

Isaac cheered:

"I want to do just what you did, Mother!"

Right away, he plopped down upon the ground beside his mother, cupped both hands snugly around his ball of dough, squinted up at the sun, and piped up at the top of his joyful, chirping voice:

> *"O golden sun shining high and clear,
> Warming the sweet grain far and near,
> Giving me fragrant bread to eat—
> Thank you, warm sun, for your blessing sweet!"*

Mother Sarah gazed down happily at her little boy and said:

"Now let's carry our dough inside so it can rest. When we bake it, the bread is sure to taste wonderful."

Another time, when Isaac was alone inside the tent, the sacred Sabbath lamp was burning upon the small stone table. He stared intently at the tiny flame, watching it sway ever so gently. With his right hand he fanned a breeze, and the flame leaned over to one side. He fanned with his left hand, and the flame leaned over to the other. Finding this game utterly delightful, Isaac kept making the little flame dance without pause, fanning faster and faster, first left, then right. Still not satisfied, he puckered his lips and blew out a stout puff of breath. Not knowing how to bend before breezes coming from every side at once, the little flame flickered out.

At that exact moment, Mother Sarah stepped into the room and saw a thin wisp of smoke curling up from the dead lamp. Sorrowfully she cried out:

"What have you done? Our dear, gentle Sabbath light has gone out and died!"

Startled, Isaac replied:

"Mother, I only made it dance! It was so happy, and now it has danced all the way up to heaven. Look at the trail of smoke! Why must it stay burning down here forever?"

His mother smiled and explained:

"Yes, indeed—long ago the tiny flame of our soul danced joyfully in heaven. Yet now, we still wish to cherish all the bright joys of life upon the earth."

She took a wooden splinter, lit a fresh flame from the glowing embers inside the hearth, and relit the lamp, saying:

"When the Sabbath lamp shines brightly, our hearts turn toward the light of our soul within—the light that longs to linger and shine for a while upon the earth!"

Isaac understood what she meant, and for the rest of his life he never blew out the Sabbath light again.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Clear and warm:** Keep a lively, cheerful rhythm as Isaac sits beside his mother in the sunshine; recite the little verse brightly and crisply.
- **Slow down toward the close:** Quicken the rhythm slightly as Isaac fans the dancing flame, then soften into a tender, gentle tone for Mother Sarah's words about the light of the soul.

### Chapter 11: The Sacrifice of Isaac

An ancient legend tells that from time to time the wicked spirit Satan had to appear before God. Whenever he came, the Evil One always tried to excuse the deeds he did among humankind. He spoke ill of human beings and accused them. One day, as Satan sneered bitterly that mortals scarcely ever remembered their Creator, the Lord said to him:

"Do you not know Abraham, the father of Isaac? He is faithful and knows no deceit. His heart shines clear and bright. He would willingly offer up even his beloved son Isaac as a sacrifice to Me."

Hearing this, Satan shouted:

"No human being is that faithful! Just try asking it of your Abraham. You will see at once—he will refuse You!"

Not long afterward, Abraham suddenly woke in the middle of the night. Amid a dazzling radiance, the Voice of the Lord spoke to him:

"Abraham, take your son Isaac, the child whom you love. Go with him to Mount Moriah. Offer him up to Me there as a sacrifice!"

Abraham stood stunned in horror. The words seemed to freeze upon his tongue. At last he managed to stammer out:

"Must it... truly be so?"

Yet the Lord hid His voice and spoke not another word. Abraham could not fall back asleep. When dawn began to break, he thought to himself, *"Whatever God asks of me, I will obey!"* He gave orders to his faithful servant Eliezer:

"Load a bundle of firewood onto the donkey's back. You will come with Isaac and me to Mount Moriah. I wish to offer a sacrifice there."

To Sarah he said:

"Bring out Isaac's finest festive garment for him to wear. We are going up Mount Moriah to offer a sacrifice."

Sarah brought forth a wonderfully woven new robe and dressed her beloved son in it. She walked with them part of the way. Her heart felt strangely heavy with foreboding, though she knew not why. As she hugged and kissed Isaac goodbye, she burst into bitter tears. A serving girl walking beside her led her gently back to the tent.

Satan had been watching Abraham's obedience to the Lord very closely. On the evening of the first day, he disguised himself as an elderly traveler, drew near Abraham, and said:

"I can read in your thoughts that you intend to give your only son over to death. He is the delight of your eyes and the hope of your future. Surely that could never have been the voice of God telling you, 'Go and kill your own son!'"

With those words, the old man walked away, leaving Abraham to trudge onward through sorrow and doubt.

On the third day, the travelers paused to rest beside the road. While Abraham and Eliezer dozed in the midday heat, a handsome young man suddenly appeared, walked straight over to Isaac, and said:

"Poor boy! I saw you in a dream—and my dreams always come true. Your father is planning to do a dreadful thing to you. He is going to draw his sacrificial knife to offer you up and kill you. Watch out today! Run away from your father right now!"

Having spoken, the young man vanished from sight.

Isaac thought to himself, *"How strange! Could my father truly do such a thing to me?"*

When Abraham and Eliezer woke up, they left the resting place and pressed onward. Soon Mount Moriah rose before their eyes. Father and son walked ahead while Eliezer led the donkey behind. Abraham asked:

"Do you see that shining bright cloud atop the mountain peak ahead?"

"Yes, Father, I see it," Isaac answered.

"Within that cloud shines the light of God. Now let us two climb up the mountain and offer the sacrifice there."

Isaac turned back to ask Eliezer:

"Eliezer, do you see that cloud upon the mountain ahead—do you see how dazzlingly bright it shines?"

Eliezer answered:

"I see no cloud at all. Have your eyes grown dim, young Master Isaac?"

Only then did Isaac understand that the sacred light appeared to father and son alone as a spiritual vision, and that his own eyes were clearer than Eliezer's. Before him, the cloud radiated mighty splendor upon the mountaintop.

Abraham told Eliezer to stay behind at the foot of the mountain. He hoisted firewood onto his own shoulders and loaded wood upon Isaac as well, while carrying the vessel of fire and the sacrificial knife in his hand. Together father and son climbed up the slope. The luminous cloud withdrew slightly from the summit. Reaching the top, they set down their loads, split the wood, and stacked each piece carefully to prepare the altar. Isaac asked:

"Father, we have the wood and the fire, but where is the animal for the offering?"

Abraham laid both hands upon his son's shoulders:

"Isaac, my son, the Lord God has chosen you yourself to be the sacrificial lamb. Very soon you will step up into His heavenly realm. Will you join your father in fulfilling the Lord's command and serving Him?"

Because an angel of the Lord had poured strength into Isaac's heart, the boy answered:

"Father, I am ready. Do everything just as God has commanded you."

For the last time, Abraham clasped his son tightly in his arms and asked:

"Have you any last farewell message you wish to leave with me?"

Isaac replied:

"Father, please carry my greetings home to Mother, and wrap a little of the ashes from this sacrifice inside a cloth to give to her. She can set it beside the Sabbath lamp to remember me."

With those words, Isaac stepped onto the pile of wood, folded his hands, and closed his eyes. Abraham gripped the sacrificial knife and raised it high.

At that very instant, an angel of the Lord swooped down with mighty force, caught firm hold of Abraham's raised arm, and cried aloud:

"Abraham, do not slay the boy! God has beheld your obedience and faithfulness. Isaac shall live!"

The knife clattered down onto the ground. Abraham threw his arms around Isaac and pressed him tight against his chest. Within the radiance streaming from the cloud above, father and son wept in each other's arms for a long while.

When at last they drew apart, Isaac opened his eyes. His gaze came to rest upon a dense thicket nearby. He cried out:

"Father! Look over there in the bushes—there is a ram caught by its horns!"

"Yes indeed!" said Abraham. "That is our lamb for the sacrifice, sent by the angel of the Lord."

They freed the ram from the thorny branches and offered it up as a burnt offering.

As father and son knelt beside the flames, the angel of the Lord drew near once more and spoke:

"Abraham, I bring you the blessings of God. They shall pour down richly upon your son."

Both father and son felt the warm, luminous cloud soaking gently through body and soul. Thus God renewed His covenant with Abraham once again. Meanwhile, far down in the dark abyss, Satan growled in fury and slunk out of sight.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Solemn yet gentle atmosphere:** Keep your voice calm and steady during Satan's two temptations so they do not feel overly frightening; highlight the spiritual wonder when father and son alone behold the shining cloud.
- **A deeply moving pause:** Pause for a beat right before Abraham raises the knife, then let your voice ring clear and bright as the angel shouts, *"Abraham, do not slay the boy!"*—and read warmly as father and son embrace beneath the radiant cloud.

### Chapter 12: Isaac and Rebekah

Mother Sarah passed away after Isaac had grown into a full-grown man. Abraham grieved deeply for her. He chose a rocky cave as his wife's resting place.

While Sarah was alive, a radiant halo of light had always hovered around their home. Upon every batch of dough she kneaded there rested a blessing that gave her baked loaves an extraordinary fragrance and sweetness. When she died, that halo vanished. The sacred Sabbath lamp went out between one Sabbath and the next, and their bread tasted ordinary like all other bread.

Now Abraham decided to seek a wife for Isaac—one worthy to become the mother of a nation. He told his faithful servant Eliezer:

"You are about to set out on a long journey for me. Take camels and two servants and travel to the land of Haran in Syria, where my younger brother Nahor lives among our kin. The angel of the Lord will journey alongside you. If you meet a noble maiden there, ask her to return with you. She shall become the wife of Isaac, son of Abraham. The Lord will make everything prosper!"

*(In ancient times, choosing a husband or wife for one's children was usually arranged by the parents.)*

Eliezer traveled steadily for seventeen days until he reached the home of Nahor's kin. Afternoon was waning as he halted with his camels in an open pasture beside a well. At that hour, women from the region were coming out to draw water. Eliezer thought long and hard, *"If only I might receive a sign showing which maiden is the bride chosen by God for Isaac."* At last a wonderful idea came to him:

"I will sit right here by this well. If a young woman not only gives me water to drink, but draws water for all my camels too, then by God's Will may she be the true bride!"

Eliezer walked over to the well. Scarcely had he sat down when a lovely young woman of graceful figure and light, nimble step approached. Balanced upon her head was an empty water jar. Swiftly she climbed the stone steps up to the well's rim. As she came back down carrying her jar brimming with water, Eliezer stepped near and asked:

"May I please have a sip of water from your jar?"

At first the girl opened her eyes wide, surprised that a stranger would address her so directly. Then she carefully lowered the water jar from her head and let him drink. When he finished, she asked warmly:

"Would you like me to draw water for all your thirsty beasts to drink as well?"

"If you would, I would be most grateful!" Eliezer replied.

She poured the rest of the water from her jar into the trough for the animals. While the camels drank noisily in great gulps, she cheerfully drew jar after jar until they had drunk their fill.

Watching her, Eliezer thought to himself, *"It is she! O Lord, it truly is she!"* Once the camels had drunk enough, he drew a golden bracelet from his pouch. Just as the girl lifted her full jar back onto her head to leave, Eliezer slipped the golden bracelet onto her free wrist and asked:

"Tell me, please—whose daughter are you? And what is your name?"

Gazing in surprise at the golden bracelet, she replied:

"My name is Rebekah. My father is Bethuel, the son of Nahor."

Hearing that, Eliezer knew at once that her grandfather was Nahor, Abraham's brother. He asked further:

"Could we ask to lodge for the night at your father's house?"

"Certainly you may. Wait right here, and I will send someone out to welcome you," she replied, and hurried lightly homeward.

Back home, Rebekah told her father about meeting the stranger seeking lodging for the night and showed him the golden bracelet. Her father Bethuel immediately sent Rebekah's brother Laban out to the well to invite Eliezer, his servants, and the camels back to rest at their house.

Before everyone sat down to eat, Eliezer asked leave to speak with both Laban and Bethuel:

"Before we eat, allow me to deliver the message from my master, Abraham."

He explained clearly how he had been sent to seek a bride from Nahor's household for Isaac, Abraham's son:

"Look—every sign points directly to Rebekah. She was the very first maiden I met, and she drew water both for me and for all of Abraham's camels."

Hearing this, Bethuel and Laban answered together:

"This meeting is surely the work of the Lord God! Let us rejoice at so fortunate a meeting."

After asking Rebekah and seeing that she gladly consented, Bethuel said:

"Faithful Eliezer, take Rebekah with you. Bring our daughter home to Isaac in the house of Abraham!"

Eliezer promptly unpacked many costly gifts from the camels' packs and presented them to Bethuel's household. Everyone ate and drank merrily as at a betrothal feast. The camels too were well cared for. Late into the night, Eliezer and his servants finally went to rest.

Three days later, the baggage was packed and ready; Rebekah's parents blessed their daughter—blessing her body, her soul, and her journey—and bade her farewell. Eliezer chose especially for Rebekah a gentle camel with silvery-gray fur.

Weeks later, as the camel caravan returned to the land of Canaan, Isaac happened to be strolling through the fields one afternoon. Spotting a line of camels swaying across the ridge of a hill, he counted them, recognized Eliezer's company returning, and walked out to meet them.

As Rebekah's camel began descending the slope, her clear eyes caught sight of a young man walking toward them. Around the young man's head shone a dazzling light. She asked Eliezer:

"Kind sir, who is that stranger walking across the field toward us?"

Only then did Eliezer, whose eyes had grown dim with old age, make out Isaac approaching. Joyfully he cried out:

"That is young Isaac, my master!"

Rebekah's heart fluttered with excitement. Her cheeks flushed rosy red, and she hastily drew her veil across her face. Eliezer leaped down from his camel and hurried forward to greet Isaac. He threw his arms around his master, pointed toward the bright silver-gray camel, and said:

"The maiden riding upon that camel is Rebekah, daughter of Bethuel."

Isaac bowed low before her as though greeting a king's daughter. Gently she lifted the corner of her veil and smiled down at him. Leaning close to Isaac's ear, Eliezer whispered:

"God has granted us a truly lovely blessing!"

Isaac took the bridle of the silvery-gray camel and led her back to Abraham's tent. When Rebekah prepared to dismount, Isaac held out both his strong hands to support her; her feet rested right upon Isaac's hands before ever touching the soil of Canaan. Stepping inside to greet Abraham, Rebekah raised her veil; the old patriarch gazed upon her beauty and praised her warmly. Abraham placed Rebekah's hand into Isaac's hand and blessed their marriage.

From that day on, Rebekah relit the Sabbath lamp in the home, and the lamp burned steadily all week long just as in Sarah's day. The halo of peace returned, and within the very dough Rebekah kneaded there rested once more the blessing of old.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Lively scene beside the well:** Highlight Rebekah's graceful movement, the noisy slurping of the thirsty camels, and the gleam of the golden bracelet.
- **Tender reunion:** Read softly and reverently where Rebekah sees the light shining around Isaac's head, he bows as if before a princess, and lifts her feet onto his hands before they touch Canaan's ground.

## Part III: Jacob

### Chapter 13: Jacob and Esau

One day, Rebekah said to Isaac:

"Rejoice, my husband, for the Lord God has heard our prayers. I am carrying a child beneath my heart!"

Overjoyed, Isaac answered:

"Then the lineage of our father Abraham's nation has already begun inside you. May you be blessed, Rebekah!"

Not long afterward, Rebekah said again:

"Isaac, rejoice! I am carrying two children inside my womb. At times it feels as though they are wrestling with one another right inside me!"

Isaac laughed, thinking Rebekah must have had a dream like that. Yet when the day of birth arrived, Rebekah gave birth to a baby boy covered all over with hair. They named him Esau, meaning *"covered with hair."*

As the first baby was born, the second child's hand reached straight out and gripped his brother's heel tightly. Truly, Rebekah had borne twins! The second boy had smooth skin and slender, graceful limbs. In Abraham's tongue, the word for heel was *akob*, so they named the second child Jacob, meaning *"the heel-grasper."*

Esau, the elder twin, grew into a strong yet rather rough boy. He caught wild animals with his bare hands, killed them, and roasted their meat to eat. Before long, Esau learned to draw a bow, shoot arrows, and hurl a spear in the hunt. He often brought home wild game he had hunted to share with his father Isaac, yet he never shared even a morsel with Jacob.

Jacob, by contrast, had a gentle spirit, a noble bearing, and a handsome face. He helped his mother with household tasks, often had strange dreams, and loved telling her what he saw in his sleep. One day Rebekah said to Isaac:

"Esau has keen eyes to explore the outer world. Jacob, however, possesses an inner sight, and the images in his dreams hold deep wisdom."

Esau was father Isaac's favorite, while Jacob was mother Rebekah's favorite. Thus both brothers grew up warmly cherished.

Grandfather Abraham watched his two grandsons grow taller day by day with deep delight. When the twin boys were fifteen years old, Abraham passed away, and his soul entered God's kingdom. Isaac laid his father's body to rest inside the rocky cave where Sarah lay buried. Great throngs of people gathered to honor Abraham—the Righteous One.

The priestly robe that Abraham had once received from Shem, the son of Noah, now passed down to Isaac. Whenever Isaac offered a solemn sacrifice, he wore this robe. Reverently he called aloud the names of the priests who had gone before him: *Abraham, Shem, Noah, Enoch, Seth*. They were the stepping-stones leading back toward the lost Garden of Paradise.

One day, Esau came home from the fields exhausted and famished. Usually he roasted game he had hunted on a wooden skewer to eat, yet that day he had caught nothing at all. Inside the tent, Jacob had just finished cooking a pot of lentil stew. And Esau loved lentil stew above all things! The moment he neared the tent door, the rich aroma of lentils drifted straight into his nostrils, making his aching hunger sharpen his craving all the more. Striding up, he demanded of Jacob:

"Give me some of your lentil stew to eat right now!"

Jacob asked:

"And what will you give me in exchange?"

"Ask for anything you want," Esau answered, so badly did he crave the lentils.

Jacob said:

"Then sell me your birthright as the firstborn son."

Esau answered:

"What good is being the firstborn to me? Everyone has to die someday anyway. Hand over that plate of lentils, and the birthright is yours!"

Jacob insisted:

"Swear to me that this is true and shall stay so forever."

At once Esau swore the oath, and Jacob gave his brother the dish of lentils along with bread and grape wine. Esau gobbled it down hungrily with great gusto and walked away, giving not another thought to what had just happened.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrast between the brothers:** Use a hearty, slightly hurried voice for Esau; keep a gentle, thoughtful tone for Jacob, teller of dreams.
- **Reverence then drama:** Speak the chain of patriarchal names (*Abraham, Shem, Noah, Enoch, Seth*) with quiet solemnity, then shift to a crisp, decisive back-and-forth beside the pot of lentil stew.

### Chapter 14: Isaac's Blessing

Esau married two wives from the Hittite tribe, among whom idol worship was widespread. Both women often offered sacrifices to idol statues according to their foreign customs. Esau cared nothing about that, nor did he feel much reverence for God. This caused Isaac and Rebekah deep sorrow.

In his old age, Isaac's eyes grew steadily dimmer until he went completely blind. Yet inward, his mind grew ever clearer and richer. He pondered deeply on the earth from its earliest beginnings and the unfolding story of humankind down to his own days. Knowing his life had not much longer to run, he summoned Esau and said:

"My son, according to custom, the firstborn son receives the blessing from the father of the family. Take your weapons and hunt some wild game. Cook a delicious meal for us and bring it to me; before I die, I will bestow upon you the family blessing."

Esau set off at once to hunt. Rebekah overheard every word Isaac spoke and clearly understood her husband's purpose. She hurried to Jacob and told him:

"Your father means to give the firstborn's blessing to your brother. That must never happen! Esau already promised to yield that right to you. Your father just sent him out to hunt game; once he cooks a meal for the two of them to share, your father will bless Esau. Listen closely to your mother, Jacob, and do as I say: go out to the flock and bring back two young goats. I will cook a delicious meal. You shall carry that dish in to your father, so that he gives you the family blessing—for you already bought it from your brother with the lentil stew."

Jacob answered:

"Dear Mother, that will never succeed! Look: my brother Esau is covered all over with hair, while my arms are smooth. The moment Father touches me, he will think I am mocking him and bring a curse down upon me instead!"

Rebekah replied:

"I will wrap the skins of the two young goats around your arms and shoulders, and you shall wear Esau's festive robe. That way, you will receive the blessing that rightfully belongs to you."

Jacob followed his mother's counsel. The kid meat was cooked tender, and strips of goatskin were tied securely over his skin. Dressed in Esau's festive robe, Jacob carried the meal inside to his father. Isaac was resting upon a couch, and Jacob spoke:

"Father, I have done what you asked. I have brought the meat dish to offer you. Now please grant me your blessing!"

Isaac answered:

"Come closer to me so I may feel you. Today your voice sounds strangely like Jacob's."

Running his hands over the arms wrapped in soft goatskin, Isaac thought to himself, *"Surely these are Esau's hands—and the scent of this robe is truly Esau's own scent."* He said, "Give me the food." After eating, Isaac said:

"Come here, my firstborn son—draw as close beside my bed as you can, so I may bless you."

Jacob knelt down before his father, and Isaac spoke his blessing:

> *"May God grant you the dew of high heaven
> And the rich abundance of mother earth!
> May all peoples serve you.
> Whoever curses you shall himself be cursed,
> And whoever blesses you shall himself be blessed."*

Scarcely had Jacob stepped outside and changed his clothes when Esau returned from the hunt with wild game. He cooked a savory meal and carried it inside to his father. In his deep, gruff voice, Esau spoke:

"Father, please sit up! I have brought venison to offer you, and when you have eaten, let your hand bless me."

Isaac cried out in shock and dismay:

"Then he who brought the meal in before you must have been Jacob! I have already bestowed the firstborn's blessing upon him. Forever shall he be blessed, for a sacred blessing is like a solemn oath—it can never be taken back."

Hearing this, Esau wept aloud in bitter grief:

"My brother has deceived me twice! First he took away my birthright, and now he has stolen my blessing too!"

Esau burned with wrath so fierce that a dark thought stirred inside him. A voice whispered to him, *"Soon enough your father will die, and then you can kill your brother Jacob!"* Esau spoke similar words to his wives. A serving girl overheard him and hurried to warn Rebekah of Esau's wicked plot.

Rebekah called Jacob to her and told him of his brother's scheme:

"Your brother wants revenge and means to kill you. Listen to your mother: set out at once. Flee to your Uncle Laban's house in the land of Haran. Stay there until your brother's vengeful anger cools down. As soon as it is safe for you to return, I will send word to you."

Knowing well how fierce his brother's temper could be, Jacob obeyed his mother. To Isaac, Rebekah said:

"You and I have both been deeply grieved by Esau's Hittite wives. Jacob ought to return to my homeland to find a wife. I am sending him to my brother Laban's house. That way, he will not quarrel any further with Esau over your blessing either."

Isaac saw the wisdom in her words. He called Jacob to him and gave him a blessing, sending him off upon his journey in peace.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Depth of the blind father:** Read slowly and warmly where Isaac's eyes grow dim yet his inner mind grows clear, contrasting with Rebekah's anxious haste.
- **The irrevocable blessing:** Speak the five lines of blessing solemnly and resonantly, like a sacred vow; then shift to Esau's choked sobs of fury and the tender urgency of a mother hurrying her son to safety.

### Chapter 15: Jacob's Flight

Rebekah gave Jacob two keepsakes to carry with him: the walking staff that had accompanied Grandfather Abraham and then Isaac upon their long journeys, along with a flask of sacred oil as a welcoming gift for Uncle Laban.

When Esau heard that Jacob was about to set off far away, he thought to himself, *"I will ambush him along the road, right at the fork turning toward Haran, and kill him there. Once he lies buried beneath the sand, no one will ever know."*

Early the next morning, while darkness still hung over the earth, Esau picked up his bow and arrows and left the house. To his wives he said:

"I am going hunting."

An hour later, at daybreak, Jacob set out on his long journey. After walking a stretch, he strayed from the main trail and wandered by winding paths. The staff guided him in a wide curve well clear of Esau's hiding place. On and on he walked. The sun climbed high. Not until nearly sunset did he find the highway again.

Esau lay waiting in ambush hour after hour for his brother, yet Jacob never passed that way. The sun rose and moved across the sky. He waited until late afternoon before trudging home, only to learn that Jacob had departed at daybreak. His two wives laughed and teased him:

"You went hunting all day long today, and couldn't even bring home a single pheasant!"

Jacob traveled onward for many days. One evening he reached a hill—none other than Mount Moriah, where Abraham had once brought Isaac to offer him to God—though Jacob had no idea where he had arrived. The sun sank below the horizon. Noticing several stones piled together, left behind from Abraham's old altar, he chose one flat stone as a pillow to rest his head for the night.

As soon as his soul rose upward in sleep, a wondrous dream unfolded and turned into living truth. High above him, the starry heaven swung wide open. A radiant stairway woven out of beams of light stretched down from the heights above to the earth below. At its lofty crest, the stairway melted into streams of light, while its foot touched the very spot where Jacob's head lay resting.

Then he beheld angels descending beside him along that heavenly ladder. Looking upward, they gazed upon the holy countenance of God; looking downward, they studied the form and face of the human sleeper. Jacob heard the angels exclaim:

"Look! Within the human being shines a small image of God!"

Throngs of angels moved up and down, and Jacob breathed in the sweet fragrance of heaven around them. From high above, a voice resounded and spoke to Jacob:

"I am the Lord God, the God of Abraham and of your father Isaac. This very ground on which you lie, I will give to you and to your descendants. Your children shall grow as countless as the stars shining across the sky. I will be with you along every road you walk, and I will guard and shield you. One day you shall return safely to this land."

Jacob watched the ladder of light dissolve gently back into the air and heard heavenly music fill the night. The angels hovered softly and rose out of sight into the heights. Only brilliant starlight remained when Jacob awoke from his vision.

His heart swelled with solemn awe and wonder. He told himself, *"This place is Beth-El, the House of God. This is the gate, the very threshold of heaven that I just beheld."*

When morning brightened, he gathered the scattered stones and stacked them one atop another, crowning the pillar with the very stone that had pillowed his head. Upon it he poured three drops of sacred oil *"in the name of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob,"* and swore a solemn vow:

"O my God, who revealed Yourself to me in this place, I vow to remain forever faithful to You!"

With that, he gripped his walking staff tightly and pressed onward toward Haran in the land of Mesopotamia.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Lighthearted then majestic:** Keep a gentle touch of humor as the staff guides Jacob in a wide circle past Esau's trap and his two wives tease him; then drop into a deep, hushed wonder, letting the sound open wide as the starry sky parts over Mount Moriah.
- **The angels' astonished cry:** Speak slowly and brightly the words, *"Look! Within the human being shines a small image of God!"* and hold a reverent cadence as Jacob crowns the stone pillar and lets three drops of sacred oil fall.

### Chapter 16: Jacob in a Foreign Land

After a long journey, Jacob reached the land of his Uncle Laban. He came upon a well covered tightly by a huge flat stone. Nearby, several shepherds were camping with their flocks of sheep. Only when all the shepherds from the surrounding pastures gathered together beside the well was their combined strength great enough to lift that heavy stone.

Jacob asked the shepherds:

"What is the name of this land?"

They answered:

"This is Haran."

"Do you know a man named Laban? How is he faring?"

They replied:

"He is doing very well. Look, his daughter Rachel is coming right now. She plans to water her sheep here too."

Jacob asked:

"Why haven't you opened the well yet so the sheep can drink?"

"Because there are not enough of us here to lift the stone."

"Let me help you!" said Jacob.

They only stared at him doubtfully. Then he stepped up to the well and, all by himself, shoved the enormous stone aside. The shepherds could scarcely believe their eyes.

Jacob walked over to Rachel. Filled with joy at meeting Uncle Laban's family so soon, he took her hand warmly and asked:

"May I draw water for your flock of sheep to drink? I am the son of Rebekah, your father's sister."

Rachel thanked him for watering her sheep, then ran swiftly home on light feet to tell her father the news.

Before long, Laban came galloping up on a camel. Right away he recognized his sister's features in Jacob's face. Jacob told his uncle about Rebekah and presented the flask of sacred oil she had sent. Laban said:

"Yes, truly—you are Jacob, my sister's son! Welcome to my house, and stay as long as you wish."

Then Laban threw his arms around Jacob and kissed him in welcome.

From that day onward, Jacob worked as a shepherd for Uncle Laban and was allowed to raise a flock of his very own. According to the customs of that region in those days, a man was permitted to take two wives. Jacob married the elder sister Leah first, and afterward married the younger sister Rachel—both of them daughters of Laban. During fourteen years spent in Uncle Laban's household, Jacob became the father of eleven sons, named Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun, and Joseph.

Year after year, Jacob waited longingly for word from his mother Rebekah, yet always in vain. Not a single message ever arrived. Could it be that Esau's anger had still not cooled?

Meanwhile, Laban and his sons grew envious watching Jacob's flocks and herds flourish far beyond their own—seeing him grow prosperous with manservants and maidservants, and many camels and donkeys.

One night, a sacred voice spoke to Jacob:

"Return to the land of your father; I will be with you."

Accustomed as he was to leading his flocks from pasture to pasture, obeying the Lord's command to embark on a long journey felt neither strange nor hard to Jacob. Gathering his herds, pack animals, servants, and large household, Jacob turned homeward along the very same roads he had traveled all alone years before. Above all else, his heart rejoiced at seeing his parents again, though he felt a flicker of unease at the thought of meeting Esau face to face.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Strength beside the well:** Emphasize the shepherds' astonishment as Jacob pushes the great stone aside single-handedly, then soften into warm joy as he clasps Rachel's hand and introduces himself as kin.
- **Homesickness across the years:** Read the names of the eleven sons in a steady, even cadence, then let your voice fall wistfully on the quiet question wondering why no word came from Mother Rebekah after fourteen years away.

### Chapter 17: The Land of Canaan — Reconciliation

Jacob's journey with his sprawling household and vast herds moved slowly. Because of this, faster-traveling caravans carried word ahead to Isaac that his son Jacob was returning to the land of Canaan.

Esau heard the news as well. His old anger had not yet fully died away, and he wondered whether Jacob was returning to rule over him. As soon as he learned that Jacob was drawing near, Esau rallied all his servants, friends, and kinsmen. Leading a large band armed with heavy clubs and a few swords, he marched out to intercept his younger brother, declaring:

"If he wants violence, he will find violence. If he wants peace, we will speak together."

Catching sight of Esau's company approaching, Jacob placed ten of his sons together with Leah at the front of the caravan, keeping Rachel and little Joseph behind. As the two groups drew close together, Jacob bowed low to the ground before Esau three times as a sign of peace.

Before that humble gesture, the last embers of anger melted away from Esau's heart. He hurried forward to Jacob and threw his arms around him in a warm embrace. Esau asked:

"Who are all these people traveling with you?"

Jacob answered:

"They are my children and their mothers."

To seal their reconciliation, Jacob gave his brother a generous gift of livestock of every kind. Esau soon turned back home to Hebron.

Jacob, however, traveled slowly, pitching camp for days at a time along the way. On the journey home, he longed to revisit Mount Moriah—where the light of God and the host of angels had once appeared to him. He wished to offer a sacrifice at Beth-El in thanksgiving for every blessing he had received.

Reaching the foot of Mount Moriah, Jacob pitched his tents. Together with his eleven sons, he climbed the mountain; Rachel rode a donkey alongside them, for beneath her heart she carried their youngest, unborn child. Reaching the summit, the sons did not touch the old stone altar Jacob had once built—for he had told them all about the heavenly ladder at this holy place—but instead joined their father in raising a brand-new altar.

As the sacrificial flames leaped skyward, Jacob knelt down with his sons, having placed a large stone for Rachel to sit upon. Silently Jacob prayed, *"May this twelfth child inside her womb grow healthy and strong."*

Suddenly, in the midst of his prayer, he heard a sacred voice speak:

"Until today your name has been Jacob. As the forefather of your people, from this hour onward you shall be called Israel. Kings and prophets shall spring forth from your house. The land of Abraham and Isaac shall belong to you and to your nation!"

As soon as those words ended, the voice grew silent once more.

Jacob came down from the mountain with Rachel and his eleven sons. The whole company traveled on toward the region of Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Before they reached the town, Rachel gave birth to their twelfth son; they named the baby Benjamin. Sadly, the birth proved far too difficult; Rachel's soul departed from her body, and she passed away. In deep grief, Jacob built a stone tomb for her near Bethlehem, each of his sons gathering stones according to his strength. To this very day, Rachel's Tomb can still be seen near Bethlehem.

Almost at that very same time, Jacob's mother Rebekah also passed away; she never got to see her beloved son again while alive upon the earth.

When Jacob drew near to Hebron, aged Isaac rejoiced that he would soon embrace his son once more. Jacob introduced each of his twelve sons one by one to his blind father. Old Isaac touched their skin, stroked their hair, felt the stature and hands of each grandson, called each one by name, and blessed them. When he had finished, he cried aloud:

"Praise be to God! I have been granted to bless the twelve tribes of Israel!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Melting away an old grudge:** Speak Esau's declaration firmly and crisply, then slow into a deeply warm cadence as Jacob bows three times and anger dissolves into a tearful embrace.
- **Solemnity followed by gentle sorrow:** Let God's voice renaming Jacob as Israel ring bright and resonant atop Mount Moriah, then soften with tender compassion at Rachel's passing near Bethlehem before blooming into quiet, fulfilled joy in blind old Isaac's embrace.

## Part IV: Joseph

### Chapter 18: Joseph Is Sold

Among his twelve sons, Jacob loved his second-youngest son, Joseph, best of all. Joseph delighted in staying close to his father, waiting upon him at meals and walking beside him across the fields while the older brothers stayed out with the herds. With the eyes of a prophet, Jacob also saw a great light shining within Joseph's soul. He thought quietly to himself: *"This child will one day stand at the head of all."*

Once, while walking alone in the fields, Joseph heard a sheep bleating piteously. Several of his brothers were there, skinning the poor creature alive to roast and eat its meat. Worse still, those heartless brothers laughed and jeered as they did it. The moment they spotted Joseph approaching, they hastily killed the animal and invited him to roast and eat it with them. Joseph cried out loudly:

"You have tortured an innocent creature! I will never eat with you the meat of a sheep tormented like that!"

He turned and went straight home, telling his father through streaming tears what he had just witnessed.

Father Jacob raised his sons with a firm hand, and at once he sternly rebuked their wrongdoing:

"Take care! Never again dare to torture a creature in so shameful a way!"

From that time onward, his brothers harbored hatred in their hearts against Joseph.

When Joseph became a youth of seventeen, his old tunic had grown too small. His brothers wore sleeveless woolen tunics to watch over the flocks of sheep. Yet Father Jacob had a special long-sleeved coat sewn just for Joseph out of silk in many radiant colors. He had thought it through carefully: *Joseph is the most careful of my sons in everything he does, and the handsomest among all twelve brothers. Therefore he deserves to wear the finest coat.* And because Joseph did only light tasks around the house, waiting upon his father and serving as his messenger, he could always keep his new robe spotless.

From time to time, Joseph had wondrous dreams. At breakfast, he often told them to his father. Jacob marveled deeply at the wise and lovely pictures shining in his son's dreams. Once, out in the fields with his brothers, Joseph told them a dream that had come to him the night before:

"All twelve of us brothers were out in a wheat field together, binding golden sheaves of grain. Suddenly, the sheaf I had laid upon the ground stood straight up on end, its ears of wheat reaching high toward heaven. All of your sheaves lifted themselves up too, skipping over to form a circle around my sheaf. I thought to myself, 'Now comes a harvest dance of the sheaves!' Yet no, nothing of the sort happened. Your sheaves stood still inside the ring and bowed down low before my sheaf. At that moment I awoke."

The brothers whispered among themselves about Joseph's dream and said:

"Do you truly want so badly to be our king and master, ordering us about?"

And they resented him even more fiercely than before.

Another time, Joseph called all twelve brothers together to get ready for the wheat harvest. When everyone was assembled, Joseph said:

"Last night I had yet another strange dream. The sun, the moon, and eleven stars all bowed low before me."

At this, Father Jacob rebuked him right in front of his brothers:

"Do you mean to say that your father, your mother, and all your brothers must fall prostrate at your feet?"

Joseph answered:

"Father, I did not make up this dream myself. It came to me on its own. What power do human beings have over the dreams that visit them?"

Even so, his brothers felt deeply irritated with him because of the things he dreamed.

Benjamin, the youngest brother, had only just turned ten and was still a child. He was the one brother whom Joseph truly loved. Joseph often played with him and told little Benjamin countless dreams and lovely stories that bloomed inside his soul. Like Joseph, Benjamin stayed mostly at home rather than going out to the pastures to herd the livestock with the older brothers.

* * *

One day, Jacob said to Joseph:

"For many days now your brothers have been out in the distant pastures. At night they sleep beside the herds, for wild beasts are always a danger. For several days I have heard no word of them. Go out to visit your brothers and bring back word to let me know that everything out there is well."

Joseph walked steadily for many hours before he finally caught sight of the herds and his brothers in the distance. Seeing him walking alone toward them in his coat of many colors, the brothers said to one another:

"Look over there—here comes the dreamer! He wants to spy on us again so he can run home and tell everything to our father. It is high time this fellow vanished for good. We get nothing out of him but trouble!"

One proposed killing Joseph and burying his body in the sand. Reuben, the eldest brother, did not agree:

"Shed no blood!"

Another brother suggested:

"We can throw him down into that dry well over there. Down inside, he will die on his own without any hand of ours laying a blow on him."

Reuben thought to himself: *When night falls, I will pull Joseph out of the well and save his life.* All of them agreed to that plan.

When Joseph arrived, he saw nothing but dark scowls on his brothers' faces. Not a single one answered his greeting. Growing afraid, Joseph tried to ask in a friendly voice:

"The sun is so hot that I am terribly thirsty. Surely you have milk or water in your leather skins? Please let me have a little."

One of them replied:

"We will give you plenty to drink!"

Several of them lunged at Joseph, seized him fast, and tore the coat of many colors right off his back. They lifted him off his feet, carried him to the dry well, and dropped him straight down into the shaft.

Joseph was so stunned that he could not utter a single sound. Only when they carried him to the rim of the well did he cry out:

"Brothers, dear brothers, please let me live!"

Down at the bottom of the deep well the earth was still slightly damp and muddy, so when he fell, Joseph did not break a single bone. Still, his whole body ached and throbbed. As his eyes slowly adjusted to the darkness, he heard a faint rustling noise. He made out a snake slithering out from the wall of the well, flickering its tongue to test the air. Joseph kept himself completely motionless. Satisfied, the snake glided quietly back into its crevice—the very hole from which his sudden fall had startled it out.

Up above at the mouth of the well, the brothers began roasting the meat of a young goat they had just killed. Reuben slipped away from them and went to sit on a nearby hilltop to keep watch over the flocks. He planned to steal back secretly at nightfall and rescue Joseph.

After the brothers had eaten and lay resting, one of them suddenly cried out:

"Look over there—camels! A caravan! Those are merchants on their way to Egypt. They will soon pass right by us here."

Judah said:

"What good does it do us to leave Joseph to die? We can sell him to those traders yonder. That way his blood will not stain our hands."

All the others agreed to this plan.

Moments later, the caravan halted beside the brothers' camp. They brought out goat's milk and offered it to the merchants to drink. Judah said:

"We have a young lad we would like to sell."

A merchant asked:

"Where is he, then? I don't see anyone else here."

Judah answered:

"We had to punish him, so we dropped him down into the well."

The brothers lowered a rope into the well, and Judah shouted down:

"Catch hold of the rope, Joseph!"

They hauled him upward. To the astonishment of the merchants, a handsome young man, stripped bare, rose up from the bottom of the well. He stood before them with his head bowed low.

The leader of the merchant caravan saw at a glance that this was an unusually handsome, clever youth from a noble house. He said:

"Since he has no clothes at all, I will give you twenty pieces of silver for the boy."

Only then did Joseph understand what they were bargaining over. He begged his brothers:

"Please let me stay in our own homeland! Do not sell me away into a strange, distant land!"

All his pleading was useless. His brothers refused even to listen.

They did not bargain long with the trader; Judah took the twenty silver coins. One of the camel drivers hastily wrapped dirty rags around Joseph's waist, shoved a staff into his hand, and barked:

"Move along, boy! Drive the camels forward!"

When Joseph glanced back one last time, he counted only nine of his brothers. *Where is Reuben?* He halted for a brief moment, scanning their faces. A leather whip whistled through the air and lashed across his bare back. A harsh voice snarled:

"Get moving, boy! Don't stand there gawking!"

Through his pain, Joseph clenched his teeth tight and drove the camels forward toward the blazing desert.

* * *

The brothers pitched a tent near the mouth of the well to sleep through the night. In the middle of the night, when all were fast asleep, Reuben came down from the hill to pull Joseph up out of the pit. Around his waist he had coiled a sturdy rope several times over. Reaching the well, he unwound the rope, leaned out over the rim, and called softly:

"Joseph, can you hear me?"

Hearing no answer, he called a little louder:

"Joseph, it is your brother Reuben! I want to pull you out of there!"

Still silence. Reuben thought to himself: *He has fallen asleep. If I toss a few small pebbles down there, that will surely wake him.* Under the pale moonlight, he scooped up a handful of tiny pebbles and dropped them into the shaft. Nothing stirred. He lowered the rope deep down and swung it around in wide circles:

"Joseph, grab the rope!"

Only silence answered him.

"He is no longer down there," Reuben whispered. "Where have they taken him?"

He stepped inside the tent where his younger brothers lay sleeping and poked Simeon in the ribs to wake him. Simeon sat straight up. Reuben demanded loudly:

"Where is Joseph? Did you kill him?"

The noise woke all the brothers at once. Simeon hurried to reassure him:

"No, we never touched a hair on him. We sold him to the merchants of a caravan passing through on their way to Egypt. That way we got rid of him, and our hands were not stained with his blood."

Reuben still could not believe it:

"Show me the money!"

Simeon pressed two silver coins into his hand:

"We got twenty pieces of silver—two coins for each of us. Here is your share."

Reuben walked outside the tent to look at the coins by moonlight. It was Egyptian silver indeed. Reuben's heart thudded hard. Slowly, he trudged back through the night to the rim of the well. He sat down upon the stone ledge circling the shaft and asked himself: *What on earth can I tell our father? Father loved Joseph so dearly!* Taking the two silver coins, he hurled them straight down to the bottom of the well. He blamed himself bitterly:

"I ought to have had the courage to stand up and defend Joseph!"

Yet what was done could never be undone. In the dim gray light of dawn, he walked back into his brothers' tent.

Meanwhile, the brothers had made a solemn pact among themselves that no one would ever reveal a single word about selling Joseph. One of them even thought of soaking his torn coat in the blood of a young goat. They decided to bring it back to their father so he would believe a wild beast had killed Joseph.

When Reuben returned, they forced him to join their vow of secrecy. In the end, he saw no choice left except to become part of their plot.

The brothers drew lots to see who would carry the bloodstained coat home to their father. The lot fell to Judah and Asher. When they came before their father, Asher held out the garment while Judah stammered:

"Look, Father, this coat... we found it far out in the fields, all torn to shreds. And there is blood on it too. Could this be Joseph's coat?"

Father Jacob took the tattered scraps of cloth into his hands and cried out in anguish:

"Woe is me! It truly is Joseph's coat! A wild beast has torn him to pieces! O God in heaven, woe is me! Joseph—the joy of my eyes, the gladness of my heart—is dead!"

Weeping aloud, he tore the clothes he was wearing, beyond all comfort.

From that day onward, Jacob wore the dark clothes of mourning. His older sons kept out of their father's sight, their hearts weighed down by guilty consciences. There was no one left to tell young Benjamin lovely stories, and the boy grieved bitterly over the loss of his beloved elder brother.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Creating emotional contrast:** Keep your voice warm and bright when telling of Jacob's fatherly love and Joseph's bond with little Benjamin; shift to a cold, tense tone in the scene where the coat is torn away and Joseph is dropped into the well.
- **Hush in the deep well and moonlit night:** Lower your voice softly and slow down when the snake flickers its tongue in the darkness, then lift the pleading cry when Joseph is sold and the crack of the whip echoes across the desert; read with quiet sorrow where Reuben flings his two silver coins down the well.
- **The cry of grief at the chapter's end:** Let Jacob's heart-wrenching lament swell as he holds the bloodstained scraps of cloth, then soften gently into Benjamin's lonely sorrow.

### Chapter 19: Joseph in Egypt

When the merchant caravan brought Joseph into Egypt, they laid out their wares for sale in the marketplace of Pharaoh's capital city. They had brought sweet balm and precious spices to trade. Two of the merchants led Joseph over to the slave market to sell him as a house servant. Passing by a well, they ordered him to wash away the desert dust and sand of the long journey. Then they gave him a spotless white loincloth and bade him wrap it neatly around his waist. They also took him to a barber to have his hair trimmed and combed, knowing full well that the cleaner and neater a slave looked, the higher the price he would fetch.

When the traders brought him into the slave market, they saw at once that Joseph was the handsomest youth offered for sale that day. They set his price at two hundred pieces of silver. Only a very wealthy buyer could afford such a sum. Drawn by his striking bearing and noble, eye-catching face, many people crowded round to look at Joseph. Yet the moment they heard the steep price, their eagerness to buy him faded away.

Suddenly a stately nobleman of great authority strode up, flanked by two armed guards. Upon his head he wore a helmet exquisitely inlaid with gold, and a costly cloak hung from his shoulders. It was Potiphar, commander of Pharaoh's royal guard. His gaze swept across the slaves waiting to be sold. His eyes stopped on Joseph, and he studied him for a long while. *Where does this bright, handsome young man come from? Having him as a house servant would adorn my household,* Potiphar thought to himself. *He was certainly never born a slave!* He asked one of the merchants:

"Where did you get hold of this young fellow?"

Delighted to be addressed by so high and noble a lord, the trader hastened to answer:

"Most honored sir, he comes from the land of Canaan. He is a Hebrew. His skin bears no slave's brand. He is new to slavery and has never yet served any master. He was cast out by a noble family and sold away."

Potiphar pondered what he had just heard:

"From Canaan? That is far outside Egypt. What is your price for him?"

Seeing how pleased the high official was, the trader answered:

"Two hundred and fifty pieces of silver, my lord!"

Potiphar weighed the matter and replied:

"I will pay two hundred pieces and no more. That is all I have brought with me. Yes or no?"

The merchant knew well enough that a high minister would not stand there haggling. He nodded:

"Very well, my lord. For two hundred pieces of silver, the slave belongs to you."

Potiphar stepped up in front of Joseph and asked:

"How much of our Egyptian tongue do you understand?"

Joseph bowed respectfully according to custom and answered haltingly:

"Egyptian... not speak very well yet—learn much, much!"

Potiphar burst out laughing with amusement at the way the boy stumbled over every word. Reaching into his belt, he drew out gold coins to pay the full purchase price. His two soldiers placed Joseph between them and escorted him through the city to an imposing stone mansion. They handed him over to the head steward who oversaw the household. The steward took an instant liking to Joseph and began teaching him how to serve at table.

During the long caravan journey, Joseph had picked up only a handful of Egyptian words from the traders. Now he was eager to learn, and day by day he spoke with growing confidence.

Potiphar, the commander of the guard, had a lovely wife whose nature had been spoiled by too much indulgence. She took a fancy to the handsome young slave. Whenever he poured wine into her goblet, she would lightly stroke his hand. One day, she told the head steward:

"I want Joseph to serve as my personal attendant. Every morning he must bring my breakfast. Whenever I receive guests, I want him to wait upon us and serve refreshments."

Because he enjoyed his mistress's favor, Joseph became the envy of every other servant in the household. Lady Potiphar gave him an embroidered silk tunic that made him look more handsome than ever. Joseph realized he had become his mistress's favorite, yet he always carried out his duties respectfully and never took advantage of her fondness.

One evening, Lady Potiphar invited several distinguished noble ladies to visit. Before her guests arrived, she instructed the hairdresser:

"Make sure Joseph is groomed to perfection. I want my friends to see that my personal servant is the handsomest young slave in all the city."

She even had a brand-new silk tunic sewn for him, embroidered with golden thread. Once the ladies were gathered in her parlor, a slave played the lyre and sang for them. Lady Potiphar ordered platters of oranges and fruit knives brought out for her guests. At the soft chime of a bell, Joseph stepped in to clear away each lady's plate.

When he entered the room with quiet grace and poise, the noblewomen stared at him as though bewitched. One lady, who could scarcely take her eyes off the servants, gazed so intently at Joseph that she accidentally cut her own finger. The plate slipped from her hand and shattered on the floor.

Quick as thought, Joseph fetched fine linen to bind her injured finger and spoke gentle, soothing words. Gracefully, he gathered the broken shards and orange peels into a basket, smiled, and thanked each lady politely. The moment he stepped out, the guests showered praise upon the courteous young servant. Lady Potiphar swelled with pride at their words. Any one of those fine ladies would gladly have paid two hundred pieces of silver to buy Joseph away from her.

Later that evening, while Joseph was tidying the parlor where the guests had dined, Lady Potiphar walked over to his side and murmured:

"You did wonderfully today, my dear."

She threw her arms around Joseph and kissed him. Terrified, he wrenched himself free and fled from her. She only laughed aloud at the sight of him running away. At that moment Joseph realized Lady Potiphar had fallen hopelessly in love with him.

All that night he tossed and turned, scarcely able to sleep. At last he drifted into a dream. Father Jacob appeared before him dressed in garments of mourning, weeping bitterly. Joseph longed to wipe away his father's tears, yet he could not lift his arms at all. Looking round, he saw that Lady Potiphar was pinning both of his hands behind his back. He woke with a start and knew at once: *I must stay on my guard whenever I am near my mistress.* Time and again he had noticed that inside her head, truth and falsehood were always tangled together.

* * *

One day, Master Potiphar happened to walk past Joseph's small chamber. He heard a soft murmuring inside. Potiphar paused to listen, then parted the curtain and stepped in. He demanded sharply:

"What are you muttering quietly to yourself here? Are you practicing sorcery?"

Joseph answered respectfully:

"Master, every day I pray to God just as my father taught me back in the land of Canaan. I pray that I may always remain worthy of my lord Potiphar's kindness and trust."

Seeing that Joseph was devout at heart and wholly sincere, his master told him:

"Come with me to the head steward. From this day onward, you shall carry the keys of the house at your belt."

Thus, young though he still was, Joseph became assistant to the chief steward—deputy steward of the entire household. Now he held authority over all the other slaves and directed their daily work.

Joseph tried to keep as far away from Lady Potiphar as he possibly could. Yet one day, she sent word summoning him to her private chamber. He had just been preparing to go out on an errand and already had his red cloak draped over his shoulders. He thought to himself: *She probably wants to send me into the city to buy supplies.* With that thought in mind, he stepped into her room.

Scarcely had he crossed the threshold than she flung her arms around him, pulled him close, and tried to kiss him. Joseph summoned all his strength and pushed her away to break free. His red cloak ripped apart, and she fell backward onto her couch with the torn garment still clutched tight in her hands. Furious with rage, Lady Potiphar shrieked at the top of her lungs:

"Help! Help me! The servant Joseph attacked me!"

She scratched her own neck with her fingernails until blood trickled down and screamed:

"He tried to strangle me!"

The servants came rushing in. Lady Potiphar sent them to summon her husband home, and when he arrived she thrust Joseph's torn cloak and the bleeding scratches on her throat before his eyes. She shed crocodile tears. Potiphar believed every single word his wife spoke. Boiling with fury at his unfaithful servant, he immediately ordered two guards to track Joseph down in the city and fling him straight into the royal dungeon, with nothing to eat or drink but bread and water.

Joseph was seized right in the middle of the marketplace. The guards bound both his hands behind his back and marched him off to prison. Thus, though completely innocent, Joseph landed inside a dark dungeon cell. His fine silk tunic was exchanged for filthy prison rags.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Lively pace in the Egyptian market:** Keep your voice bright and lively at the slave market, especially reading Joseph's first halting Egyptian words with charming warmth to bring out a gentle smile.
- **Tonal shift at the warning dream:** Tell the ladies' orange-and-lyre banquet with gracious poise, then lower your voice into an anxious, somber tone when Joseph dreams of Father Jacob weeping while his mistress pins his hands behind his back.
- **Climax of false accusation and quiet sorrow:** Pick up a sharp, decisive pace as the red cloak tears and the false screams ring out, then slow down gently with deep compassion as Joseph is led in bonds from the marketplace into the dungeon.

### Chapter 20: Joseph in the Dungeon

Throughout his first night in the dungeon, Joseph had time to think over everything that had happened that day. Despair flooded his soul. Quietly he prayed to God:

"O Lord, You lifted me up. O Lord, You have let me fall. Only You know all the paths of my life. I put my trust in You!"

Before long, the jailer saw that Joseph was no wrongdoer. He put him in charge of handing out bread and water to the prisoners. The jailer was well pleased to have someone take over that chore for him.

One day, guards led in two rather unusual prisoners. One wore a baker's clothes, with flour still clinging to his hair. The other wore fine garments and had once been an official in the royal palace. Both looked deeply downcast. Joseph struck up a conversation with the two men.

The baker told his tale:

"I used to be the royal baker in Pharaoh's palace. Somehow a tiny pebble got caught inside the dough. As bad luck would have it, Pharaoh bit down straight onto the very bite of bread with that little stone in it! He flew into a terrible rage and ordered me thrown into prison."

The other man told his story next:

"And I am Pharaoh's cupbearer. I always filled His Majesty's goblet at every meal. Just as I was pouring wine into Pharaoh's cup, at that exact instant a fly plunged straight into the pitcher and floated along with the stream of wine right into His Majesty's drinking goblet! He commanded that I too be locked away in this dungeon. I am innocent! How could I tell the fly not to dive into the wine pitcher just then?"

The next morning, when Joseph brought water and bread to the two men, each sat huddled in a corner with a gloomy face. Joseph asked:

"Why do both of you look so discouraged today? Surely there is still hope that Pharaoh will forgive you."

Hearing this, the cupbearer replied:

"I keep thinking about a strange dream that came to me last night. Its images are still clear right before my eyes. If only there were someone who could interpret this dream for me!"

Joseph answered:

"Only God can interpret dreams. Still, tell me what you saw in your dream."

The cupbearer began to tell it:

"I saw a grapevine standing before me. Three green shoots sprouted from that vine. No sooner had the three shoots grown up than they burst into blossom, and the blossoms turned into clusters of grapes right before my eyes. In one hand I held Pharaoh's goblet, and with the other I plucked the grapes and squeezed them into the cup. The goblet filled to the brim with grape juice. Then I lifted it and presented it to Pharaoh."

Joseph said to him:

"I believe I can interpret this dream for you. The three vine shoots are three days. Within three days, Pharaoh will send for you. Once more you will fill the king's goblet. Just as in former days, you will serve again as his cupbearer."

Hearing such a hopeful interpretation, the cupbearer's whole face brightened at once. Joseph continued speaking to him:

"Cupbearer, I beg of you, when all goes well with you once more, remember me and do me a kindness. Speak a good word for me before Pharaoh. Help me get out of this dark dungeon, for I was thrown in here just as innocently as you were."

Joseph told the cupbearer everything that had happened to him in Potiphar's house.

All this while, the chief baker had listened intently to how favorably Joseph had interpreted the cupbearer's dream. So he said to Joseph:

"I would very much like to tell you the dream that came to me last night as well: I was balancing three baskets full of bread upon my head. Inside the topmost basket were the finest round loaves and sweet pastries baked for Pharaoh. Birds of the sky swooped down and pecked away all the baked bread out of all three baskets. Tell me, what does that mean?"

Joseph remained silent for a long while. He hesitated, reluctant to break the meaning of this dream to the baker. At last he spoke softly:

"Master baker, I would rather not tell you what this dream means."

Yet the baker would not let him rest:

"Whatever it is—even if it brings bad news—please tell me! I can see you know how to interpret my dream too."

Gently, Joseph began:

"The three baskets are three days. Within the next three days, by Pharaoh's command, you will be hanged, and your body hung upon a stake. The birds of the air will swoop down and pick at your flesh."

The poor baker sank into bitter despair. Meanwhile, inside the cupbearer's heart a tiny spark of hope flickered to life.

Three days later was Pharaoh's birthday. The king gave a splendid banquet for all his courtiers and ordered the release of the cupbearer, who had faithfully waited upon him. The king restored him to his former post. Toward the baker, however, Pharaoh's anger had not softened. Pharaoh had him led out to be hanged, all because he had failed to take proper care while kneading the bread dough.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Prayer inside the dungeon:** Open slowly, deeply, and steadily with Joseph's prayer on his first night so children feel the strength of faith amid hardship.
- **A light touch of humor before the dreams:** Read the stories of the pebble in the dough and the fly diving into the wine jug with a slightly aggrieved, wry tone to keep the rhythm natural.
- **Contrast between the two dreams:** Sound bright and relieved when interpreting the three vine shoots; hesitate tenderly and sadly when Joseph must tell the truth about the three bread baskets, then close solemnly on Pharaoh's birthday.

### Chapter 21: Pharaoh's Dream

Many months passed, and Joseph bore his imprisonment with patience. He never heard a word from the cupbearer again, for amid his newly restored splendor the man had forgotten Joseph completely. Day after day, Joseph brought bread and water to his fellow prisoners. From them he learned many different languages and gradually made peace with his lot. More than a full year went by like this.

One night, Pharaoh dreamed two exceedingly strange dreams. Waking in the morning, the king felt restless and troubled. He summoned his soothsayers to interpret his dreams for him. At the appointed hour, the wise men assembled in full number, and Pharaoh recounted his first dream:

"I was standing by the bank of the River Nile when seven plump, handsome cows climbed up out of the Nile waters. They grazed among the tall reeds along the riverbank. Right behind them, seven gaunt, ugly cows also stepped up out of the water. They strode onto the meadow and swallowed all seven sleek, handsome cows whole! I know this dream is of utmost importance. Tell me its meaning!"

The soothsayers guessed one way and then another, yet could not hit upon a single reasonable explanation. Then Pharaoh told them his second dream:

"I saw seven sturdy ears of grain, plump and bursting with kernels, growing upon a single stalk. Then I saw another stalk sprout seven thin, drooping ears. They swallowed up all seven fine ears of grain, and then everything crumbled to dust!"

Once again, the soothsayers puzzled over every possible clue, wrinkling their brows and sweating with anxiety. Still they could not offer one true answer. Disheartened, the king sent them all away.

When he heard of this, the cupbearer suddenly remembered the dream interpreter back in the dungeon. Could he still be there? He told Pharaoh how long ago a young Hebrew in prison had interpreted dreams for both him and the baker, and how everything had come to pass down to the smallest hair, just as the young man said. Hearing this, Pharaoh immediately commanded that the young dream interpreter be brought out of the dungeon and led before him.

Two royal palace guards went down into the prison with the cupbearer. Among all the inmates, he quickly found Joseph. Catching sight of the cupbearer, Joseph looked at him questioningly. He thought to himself: *"Can it be that he means to hand me back to Potiphar?"* Yet the cupbearer proved friendly, stepped up to embrace Joseph, and said:

"Look—now I have remembered you at last."

The cupbearer showed the jailer a papyrus scroll stamped with Pharaoh's seal. Seeing the royal seal, the jailer turned Joseph over to them. Still afraid he was about to be returned to Potiphar, Joseph asked:

"Master Cupbearer, where are you taking me?"

The cupbearer replied:

"You are going to interpret two dreams for Pharaoh. None of his wise men can solve them. How accurately you interpreted my dream and the baker's long ago—everything happened just as you spoke. I have told Pharaoh all about it."

Joseph asked:

"What did he dream?"

The cupbearer answered:

"Only Pharaoh and his wise men know. Pharaoh's dream is kept strictly secret. He himself will tell it to you."

Even then, Joseph did not know whether to be glad or whether he was walking into some fresh misfortune.

In the servants' quarters of the palace, Joseph was bathed, shaved clean, anointed with fragrant oil, and dressed in an elegant robe. Everything changed so swiftly that he could scarcely grasp what was happening to him. The cupbearer led him into Pharaoh's inner palace. Along the way, he coached Joseph on how a subject must conduct himself in Pharaoh's presence.

They entered a small throne room. Pharaoh sat upon a golden chair, surrounded by his counselors. Joseph threw himself upon the floor, touching his forehead to the ground three times. Then he rose to his feet and looked straight into Pharaoh's eyes. The king looked the young man over from head to foot and was well pleased with what he saw.

"Step closer!" he said. "I hear you can interpret dreams. Is that true?"

Joseph answered:

"Most High Majesty, when the Spirit of God shines within me, I can interpret them."

"Then hear my dream!" Pharaoh recounted the portent of the seven fat cows and the seven gaunt, ugly cows. "Then I had another dream. Seven ears of grain grew out of a single stalk, full and fair. After them sprouted seven blighted ears, scorched by the east wind. Those seven withered ears swallowed up the seven fine ears. I told my soothsayers, yet none of them could explain it. Speak, Hebrew—what do you say?"

Joseph closed his eyes. He stayed silent for a moment, listening intently deep within his own heart. In the tense quiet, Pharaoh and his wise men waited breathlessly. Suddenly, Joseph opened his eyes and began to speak:

"Noble Majesty! Both dreams hold one and the same meaning. God wishes to grant Pharaoh a sign of things soon to come. The seven fine cows and the seven full ears of grain mean that the next seven years will be blessed with rich, abundant harvests. Following close behind them will come seven years of famine. Every green pasture will wither dry. Most of the cattle will perish. Seed sown in the earth will be parched dry by scorching heat and drought. There will be no bread left to bake. That Noble Pharaoh dreamed two such similar dreams at once means God has firmly decreed that this shall surely happen. Therefore, Pharaoh ought to govern wisely throughout the seven plentiful years ahead. Appoint overseers to manage the harvest and order many granaries built. In this way you will save your people through the seven years of famine."

Joseph said no more, folded his arms across his chest, and bowed his head.

His words struck with tremendous force. Pharaoh stood stunned by the truth as though struck by a bolt of lightning. Not for a single instant did he doubt that the Hebrew had spoken the whole truth. His counselors nodded in agreement, and an excited murmur rippled through the hall.

As soon as he recovered from his astonishment, Pharaoh rose to his feet. Everyone fell silent. He spoke to Joseph:

"Hebrew, the voice of God has spoken through you. Because you are so favored by God and so wise, you shall likewise be held in utmost honor by me. I appoint you ruler over my entire court and all my royal estates. As the voice of wisdom, you shall govern the people beside me. Only as to the throne itself am I higher than you. Throughout the next seven years, see to it that abundant stores of grain are gathered and safely kept. As many carpenters as you need, all are placed under your command. Every man must obey your orders. Throughout all of Egypt, no one stands above you except myself, Pharaoh."

With that, he drew the golden signet ring from his own finger, slipped it onto Joseph's hand, and proclaimed:

"With this ring of royal authority, I bestow upon you an Egyptian name. From this very hour, you shall be called Zaphnat Paneah, the Bread-Giver."

Then he ordered a golden chain and every badge of honor placed around Joseph's neck.

Joseph scarcely knew what was happening to him. Was he dreaming, or was he wide awake? He knelt down before Pharaoh, overwhelmed by the king's words and favor. The Supreme King motioned for him to draw closer and invited him to sit right beside him on the throne.

At a wave of Pharaoh's hand, attendants brought forth a splendid robe and draped it over Joseph's shoulders. Afterward he was given a palace of his own, complete with household servants, chariots, and prized horses.

On an appointed day, Pharaoh appeared before his people by riding through the capital city in the royal chariot. Seated right beside him was Joseph-Paneah. Musical instruments rang out thunderously. Heralds shouted aloud:

"Pharaoh and Grand Vizier Zaphnat Paneah, the Bread-Giver, are approaching! Pay them honor!"

The crowds fell to the ground in homage and cheered in welcome, each in their own way. Pharaoh had ordered the miracle of the dream interpretation proclaimed far and wide. Amid the rejoicing throng, Joseph suddenly caught sight of a lone woman standing apart and staring straight at him. It was Potiphar's wife. For one fleeting moment a thought crossed his mind: *"Now you could punish her for all her lies!"* Then that thought gave way to another: *"Yet it was her own passion that sent me to prison. Otherwise I would never have met Pharaoh's cupbearer. She too helped shape my destiny. I forgive her!"*

Not long afterward, Joseph set out on a journey across the entire kingdom with a train of chariots and officials. He wanted to inspect each region with his own eyes, learn what each land produced and how rich its harvests were, and have everything carefully recorded. Wherever he went, he ordered granaries built and had every surplus bushel of grain stored inside them. Just as he had prophesied, the first year's harvest proved so plentiful that the carpenters could not build new storehouses fast enough, so they stored huge quantities of grain inside dry rock caves as well. Year after year rolled by in this way. Pharaoh gave Joseph the noble daughter of a priest in marriage. She bore him two sons, whom they named Manasseh and Ephraim. So Paneah lived, pouring himself into great labors and ceaseless activity amid dazzling splendor. Yet pride and arrogance never once showed in him, for he had tasted the sorrow of this world down to its deepest depths.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From dungeon to throne room:** Keep a calm, unhurried tone while Joseph is in prison, quicken the pace as the soothsayers furrow their brows in defeat, and pause deeply before the quiet moment when Joseph closes his eyes to interpret the dreams.
- **The moment of forgiveness in the crowd:** Pause a beat when Joseph recognizes Potiphar's wife, then speak his silent thought *"I forgive her!"* very softly so children feel the greatness of his mercy.

### Chapter 22: The Years of Famine

When the seven years of plenty came to an end, the hard years arrived just as Joseph had foretold. Green fields withered and died from the drought. Not only Egypt suffered crop failure, but all the neighboring lands as well. Scarcity spread all the way to Canaan, where Father Jacob and Joseph's brothers lived. People from many surrounding nations flocked into Egypt to buy grain from Paneah's storehouses. With every passing year of famine, respect for him deepened in the hearts of the Egyptian people. Everywhere people repeated the saying:

"We all thank Bread-Giver Paneah for saving our lives. Without his granaries, every single one of us would have starved to death."

In the land of Canaan, not a pound of grain remained. Only now and then would a merchant caravan returning from Egypt pass through, selling off a little of the grain it carried at a steep price. Father Jacob told his sons:

"Hunger has fallen upon our land too. Already I have had to slaughter more than half of our herds. Word has it that grain is being sold in Egypt. Lead several camels down there and find a way to buy wheat for our household!"

The ten older brothers decided to set out. Their father wanted to keep young Benjamin safely at home beside him.

After many weeks on the road, the brothers reached Egypt. They were directed to the granaries right in Pharaoh's capital city. That very day, Paneah was overseeing the grain sales in person. Although roughly twelve years had gone by, the moment he laid eyes on the ten men asking to buy grain, he knew his brothers instantly. They, on the other hand, had no way of recognizing Joseph-Paneah in his fine Egyptian ornaments, beard, and Egyptian hairstyle.

Joseph wanted to test his brothers' hearts, so he did not speak to them in their native tongue. He called an interpreter to speak with them instead. Stepping close, he treated them with cold severity:

"Where do you come from, you strangers?"

Startled and confused to see an Egyptian noble surrounded by attendants, Reuben, the eldest brother, bowed low and answered:

"Noble sir, we have come from the land of Canaan, humbly begging your permission to buy grain."

Paneah replied coldly:

"Are you not all spies? You only want to spy out this land and our storehouses of grain!"

Astonished, Reuben answered:

"My lord, that is not true. We are all sons of one and the same father—twelve brothers in all—and we are honest men."

Paneah refused to relent and pressed them further:

"Is your father still alive? And where are the two missing brothers?"

Reuben replied:

"The youngest is at home with our father, and the other—is no more."

At that, Paneah declared:

"By the life of Pharaoh himself I swear you shall not leave this place until one of you brings that youngest brother to stand before me. The rest of you shall stay here as prisoners. Thus we shall put your truthfulness to the test."

Paneah ordered them all placed under arrest and handed their camels over to a servant's care. The ten brothers stood utterly bewildered and stunned.

Then Reuben said to his younger brothers:

"See—the sin we committed against Joseph is catching up with us now. You sold him into Egypt, and now Egypt strikes back at us, bringing misery down upon us all."

For three days and three nights, the ten men scarcely slept a wink for fear and remorse.

On the third day, Paneah came to them and spoke:

"I have decided to handle this differently. Only one of you will remain here in captivity. The rest of you may carry grain back home. Afterward, all of you must return here and bring your youngest brother with you. Talk it over and decide which one you will leave behind."

Paneah waited just outside the doorway. Through the curtain hanging across the entrance, he could hear and understand every word they whispered to one another in Hebrew.

Reuben said:

"Did I not tell you never to sin against Joseph? Now his blood is demanding justice upon our heads!"

And Simeon remembered:

"How pitifully he begged us that day not to sell him into Egypt. Our ears were deaf as stone. Now God's justice is punishing us. I will stay behind as a prisoner in Egypt until you bring Benjamin back."

Paneah had Simeon bound right before his brothers' eyes, then sent the other nine on their way. He had ordered their sacks filled to the brim with grain and their purchase money laid right inside the mouth of each sack. He also saw to it that they received good provisions for their journey. Then they were permitted to lead their camels homeward.

After traveling for several days, they reached to pay an innkeeper for lodging with a bit of grain from a sack. Opening one of the sacks, they saw the grain silver lying right on top of the wheat. At first they gasped in amazement, then terror gripped their hearts. Every brother checked his own sack and found his money resting right at the mouth. Surely that Egyptian lord was plotting some snare against them. Could he mean to force all eleven brothers into slavery? Hearts heavy with dread, they pressed on along the road. Reaching home to Father Jacob, they told him everything that had happened on their journey and how they had been forced to leave Simeon behind.

Their father lamented bitterly:

"I have already lost Joseph! Simeon is lost as well, and now must I send Benjamin away too and lose every last one of my sons?"

Reuben pleaded:

"If we fail to bring Benjamin back to you, you may put my two sons to death!"

Judah vowed:

"Father, I pledge myself as surety that Benjamin will surely return!"

Still Father Jacob could not bring himself to let his sons go back to Egypt.

Some time later, the Egyptian grain had all been eaten, while famine still pressed cruelly across the whole region. With a heavy heart, Jacob finally allowed his sons to set out for Egypt with Benjamin. They carried double the sum of silver, in case they were called to account for the money returned the first time. The closer they drew to Egypt, the more anxious and uneasy the brothers felt.

Word soon reached Paneah that they had arrived in the capital: *"The Hebrews have come back."* He immediately ordered that the men from Canaan be brought to his own mansion. Simeon was brought to the house as well. Frightened, the brothers assumed this was over the silver. One of them whispered:

"They have set a trap to seize us all as slaves!"

When Paneah appeared before them, they all threw themselves down upon the floor and bowed low. He greeted them kindly and bade them rise. Looking into the innocent face of his beloved young brother Benjamin, tears welled up in his eyes. So deeply stirred was his soul that he hurried into an adjoining room to weep. After washing his face clean, he returned and ordered his palace servants to prepare a banquet in the great hall for all his honored guests. For the very first time, all twelve brothers sat together around one table—yet eleven of them still had no inkling that their host was their own brother Joseph…

Once more, Joseph wished to test his brothers' hearts. When the time came for them to set out for home, Paneah instructed his servant:

"Fill the Hebrews' sacks full of grain. Slip the silver they paid right back inside their sacks. And into the youngest brother's sack, place my own silver cup."

Scarcely had the brothers ridden out of the city when a troop of horsemen overtook them and forced them to halt. The captain shouted aloud:

"One of you has stolen the silver cup belonging to our noble master Paneah!"

Reuben answered:

"Such a thing is impossible. There must be some mistake. Whoever stole it deserves to die!"

When the grain sacks were unfastened, the silver cup lay right inside Benjamin's sack. Panic and horror seized them all. The captain declared:

"This fellow—your youngest brother—must come back with me. He shall become my master's slave and remain in Egypt forever. The rest of you are free to go!"

Without Benjamin, not one of them wished—or dared—to go home to their father. Every single one turned around and marched back to Paneah's mansion. Once again they fell flat on the ground before the high minister. Judah stepped forward:

"Noble Prince, we do not know how the cup found its way into the sack. I pledged my very head to our aged father for Benjamin, promising to bring him safely back. Take me as your slave in his place, I beg you. If Benjamin does not return home, our old father will never survive it—he will go down into the dark underworld of the dead. Our father himself told us so."

Joseph-Paneah could restrain himself no longer. He ordered all the Egyptian guards to leave the hall so he might stay alone with the Hebrews. Walking over to Benjamin, he clasped his brother's hand and spoke in the purest Hebrew:

"I am Joseph, your lost brother!"

At those words, a shudder ran down the limbs of the older brothers, freezing them stiff with terror. Benjamin alone felt no fear at all. He flung his arms around his long-lost, beloved brother, and Joseph could hide his tears no longer.

He spoke to his brothers:

"Come closer to me, brothers, and do not be afraid. Yes, you sold me into Egypt; yet it was God's Will that brought this about. Through it I have been able to keep countless people alive who otherwise would have starved to death. The famine has already lasted two years, and five more years lie ahead. Look—God has placed me here as friend and co-ruler at Pharaoh's side. Hurry straight back to our father at once. Carry my greetings to Father, and tell him to move down here to Egypt with all your wives, your children, and the whole household. I will give you land and homes and feed every one of you through the five years of famine still to come."

The brothers could scarcely believe their ears or take in what had just unfolded. Joseph stepped up to each brother in turn, embraced him, and gave him a warm kiss of brotherly reconciliation. Their hearts swelled with repentance and gratitude, and their tears washed away the last traces of fear.

News that Paneah's brothers had arrived raced through the capital like wildfire. Pharaoh himself summoned them into his presence and rejoiced to hear of the wondrous weaving of destiny's paths. He ordered horses and wagons prepared to bring Joseph-Paneah's father and the wives and children of all his brothers down to Egypt. Joseph presented them with gifts of fine garments and ample food for the road.

This time, it was no small string of camels heading back to Canaan, but a great long caravan. As the eldest son, once they neared home, Reuben was chosen to gallop ahead and prepare their father's heart. As he approached the cluster of tents, his two young sons were the first to recognize the rider and ran rushing out to meet him, with the women and servants following behind. Reuben greeted everyone, then went straight to Father Jacob's tent.

Hearing the commotion outside, Jacob stepped to the doorway wearing his dark mourning clothes. Seeing Reuben alone, terror gripped his heart. Yet his eldest son threw his arms around him and cried joyfully:

"Father, everyone is coming home—all of them! We are bringing both Simeon and Benjamin back too. I rode ahead to bring you the glad news."

Hearing this, Jacob's face lit up. Lifting his eyes toward heaven, he exclaimed:

"Thank You, O God!"

A little later, Reuben sat alone with Father Jacob inside the tent. He recounted everything they had lived through on their journey to Egypt, especially all that had passed with Pharaoh and the Bread-Giver Paneah. He described the splendid feast with Paneah:

"And suddenly, Father, he sent every servant from the room. He began speaking to us in Hebrew. He said: 'Do you not know me? I am Joseph, your twelfth brother. It was by God's Will that I was brought to Egypt!'"

As Reuben spoke these words, Jacob raised both hands, his eyes opening wide. Slowly he covered his eyes with both palms. A strangely deep peace settled over him, so still that Reuben feared his father's soul was slipping from his body. Yet then Jacob opened his arms wide. His eyes sparkled as he gazed far into the distance toward the radiant light of the heavenly ladder. And softly across his lips came the words:

"Joseph is alive! He is alive!"

"Yes, Father, he is alive!" Then Reuben fell silent for a long while.

His father's eyes turned toward him. He asked:

"How did Joseph ever reach Egypt? Why did he not come home sooner?"

Reuben dropped to his knees at his father's feet, clasped his knees, and burst into tears. When he found his voice again, he confessed:

"Father, forgive us brothers for our sin!"

Then he told the whole story of that day when Joseph was cast into the pit—how he, Reuben, had made the mistake of waiting until night to pull his brother up with a rope, and how the brothers had sold Joseph to a caravan traveling toward Egypt. Reuben repeated word for word what Joseph had told them at the banquet table:

"You sold me into Egypt; yet it was God's Will that brought this about. Through it I have been able to keep countless people alive who otherwise would have starved to death. The famine has already lasted two years, and five more years lie ahead. Look—God has placed me here as friend and co-ruler at Pharaoh's side. Hurry straight back to our father at once. Carry my greetings to Father, and tell him to move down here to Egypt with all your wives, your children, and the whole household. I will give you land and homes and feed every one of you through the five years of famine still to come."

Scarcely had Reuben finished speaking when joyful shouts rang out outside. The other brothers had arrived with the caravan, bringing grain and gifts from Joseph. Reuben signaled to his brothers that Father Jacob already knew everything. One by one, as each stepped inside to greet their father, he clasped Jacob's knees and begged forgiveness. That very same day, Father Jacob put on a robe of pure white wool.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Building tension through dialogue:** Keep a stern, cold edge in Paneah's voice when questioning his brothers through the interpreter; drop to remorseful whispers behind the curtain and a choked, desperate plea when Judah offers himself in Benjamin's place.
- **The breathless moment of reunion:** Warm your voice noticeably when Joseph speaks Hebrew to Benjamin; pause softly at the detail of Jacob beholding the light of the heavenly ladder and laying aside his mourning clothes for a robe of white wool.

### Chapter 23: The Long Journey to Egypt

That night, a sacred voice spoke to Jacob:

"Do not be afraid to move down to Egypt. I will be with you, and there I will make of you and your sons a mighty nation. The day will come when I Myself will lead you back again to the land of Canaan."

So Jacob and his eleven sons, along with a long, winding train of wagons and camels, left their homeland behind and set out on the road. The long journey was filled with hardship and toil, yet a kindly star traveled alongside them. As soon as the caravan entered Egypt, swift riders galloped ahead to bring Joseph-Paneah word of their arrival. He had his swiftest horses harnessed to his chariot and drove out to greet the caravan. The moment he saw his father, he threw himself into his arms and wept openly without a trace of shame before all the crowds watching round about.

Pharaoh granted Joseph's brothers the land of Goshen for their new home. He invited their aged father into his presence and assured him of the friendship of the Egyptian people. Father Jacob lived seventeen more years in Egypt. He lived to see the branches of his twelve sons grow into a small nation. He gave that people the name Israel—the very name God had revealed to him upon Mount Moriah.

Joseph had to give his father his solemn promise that after his death, his body would be carried back to Canaan to be buried in Sarah's tomb. And everything took place just as he wished.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A peaceful, warm rhythm:** Keep a gentle, serene cadence following God's reassuring words in the night and the image of *"a kindly star"* guiding the caravan.
- **Solemn pause at the close:** Emphasize the name **Israel** and the seventeen quiet years in Goshen as a calm resting point before the storm of the next chapter.

### Chapter 24: A New Pharaoh

Throughout Joseph's lifetime, the people of his brothers—the Israelites—enjoyed a prosperous, peaceful life. They built houses, plowed fields, and tended herds of livestock. Their numbers grew steadily larger. The brothers had children, their children had children and grandchildren, and the household servants they had brought from Canaan multiplied no less.

When Joseph passed away and his soul went to join his fathers and forefathers in Heaven, his body was embalmed according to Egyptian custom. A stately tomb was built for him beside the River Nile. Mourning draped the entire kingdom as his coffin was carried into the burial vault.

Time rolled on, generation after generation. At length a new Pharaoh ascended the throne, a king who knew nothing more about Joseph than the fact that the Israelites had grown into a powerful people. The Egyptians grew fearful that one day the Israelites might rise up and seize control over all Egypt.

The royal counselors conferred with Pharaoh:

"Look—the Israelites have grown nearly as strong as the Egyptians themselves. We have trained many of them as warriors in our army. They are brave and daring fighters. We must devise a plan to weaken this nation, lest one day they rise up against us."

Pharaoh suggested:

"Let massive defensive walls be built around both our great cities, Pithom and Raamses. This huge undertaking will exhaust them utterly. Have work proclaimed throughout the land of Goshen. At first pay generous wages, then gradually introduce harsh taskmasters. Once we hold these Israelite workers firmly in our grasp, we will cut their pay smaller and smaller until at last they receive only food to eat as their wages. Then they shall become our slaves. We shall compel them to labor."

Pharaoh's plan was approved, and thousands of Israelites were conscripted as city builders. Night and day the brick kilns burned without rest. Giant stones for the walls had to be hauled from great distances. Wages dwindled away until nothing at all remained. From then on, merciless taskmasters and stern overseers drove the people relentlessly. Beatings with the whip became a daily routine, and death awaited anyone who dared resist.

Pharaoh's counselors hatched an even more cruel scheme to weaken the people: the Hebrew midwives—the women who helped mothers give birth—received strict orders to kill every newborn boy and dispose of his body. Only baby girls were allowed to live. Yet whenever an opportunity arose, the midwives strove to spare the newborn boys' lives.

Around this same time, Pharaoh dreamed an extraordinary dream. In his dream, the king saw a tall old man holding in one hand a balance scale with two pans at either end. With the other hand, he gathered all the nobles of Egypt and placed them upon one pan. Upon the opposite pan he set a single little lamb—and yet that little lamb outweighed the entire powerful nobility of the kingdom.

Pharaoh summoned his sorcerer Balaam to interpret the dream. Balaam declared:

"The little lamb is the emblem of the people of Israel. Your Majesty cannot weaken them by fire, for God rescued Abraham from the brick kiln. Nor can Your Majesty wipe them out by the sword, for Isaac was saved from the knife. However, Your Majesty can weaken them through water. From this day onward, command that every newborn infant be flung into the waters of the River Nile."

So spoke the sorcerer Balaam. Pharaoh promptly ordered his soldiers to carry out this plan. Every baby boy they discovered—little ones who could not even walk yet—was hurled into the River Nile. Bitter sorrow fell upon the Israelite mothers across the entire land of Goshen.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From peace to heavy oppression:** Begin with the calm, serene cadence of Joseph's days, then let your voice turn colder, heavier, and slower through the scheme at Pithom and Raamses and the brick kilns burning night and day.
- **The dream of the scales and sorrowful close:** Bring out the stark contrast between *"the entire powerful nobility of Egypt"* and *"a single little lamb"*; emphasize Balaam's threefold reasoning of fire (*Abraham*), sword (*Isaac*), and water (*the River Nile*), then drop gently into sadness on the closing line.

## Part V: Moses

### Chapter 25: Birth and Childhood

In the house of Amram the Hebrew there were already two children: little Aaron and his older sister, Miriam. One day, right after the law condemning newborn boys to death was proclaimed, Amram's wife gave birth to another son. As she held the infant in her arms to nurse him, the mother suddenly beheld a soft, gentle radiance glowing all around the child's body. She resolved to do everything in her power to save her son.

For three whole months, the mother kept the baby hidden away, until Pharaoh's soldiers launched a fresh sweep to hunt down and kill infants. Then she thought of a plan. Out of river reeds she wove a small chest, caulking every seam tightly with pine pitch and tar so no water could leak in. Placing her baby inside, she carried the little ark out and hid it along the edge of the River Nile, while young Miriam stood watch a short distance away.

That day the sun beat down like blazing fire. Princess Bithia, Pharaoh's daughter, walked down with her maidens to the Nile to bathe and cool off. At a quiet inlet overgrown with reeds, Bithia had scarcely stepped into the water when she spotted a small chest bobbing gently among the rushes right nearby. She sent one of her maidens to fetch it for her. As soon as she lifted the lid, the little boy inside burst out crying.

Bithia leaned low over the chest, and at once the baby stopped weeping, smiled brightly, and stretched both tiny hands up toward the princess.

"Oh, goodness," she cried out, "this must surely be a Hebrew baby—one of the children doomed to be slain."

Yet she gathered the infant straight into her arms and loved him tenderly. Without anyone noticing, little Miriam stepped quietly up beside Bithia and asked:

"Princess, shall I go call a Hebrew woman to nurse the baby for you?"

"I would like that very much," the princess answered. "What a dear little boy he is!"

Miriam ran straight home and brought back her own mother. When the two returned, the princess sat among her companions. She played fondly with the infant and declared:

"This little boy shall be my living plaything! I want to keep him! His name shall be Moses." (In the Egyptian language, that name means *"drawn out of the water"*.)

Turning to the Hebrew woman, she instructed her:

"This child stands under my protection. Take him home with you. Today you shall receive a written decree guaranteeing his life. Now and then, carry the little boy to the palace so I can play with him and look upon him to my heart's content. To pay you for caring for him, I will give you one piece of silver every day."

Everything happened just as Bithia had promised, and her royal father indulged his daughter's every wish. Miriam and her mother felt joy beyond words. Moses had been saved from death, and those writs of protection bore Pharaoh's own royal seal.

When the little boy turned two years old, Bithia announced:

"From now on, I want him with me in the palace all the time."

Miriam and her mother were still permitted to visit him at the palace from time to time and speak to him in Hebrew, while Bithia talked with him in Egyptian. She still had no idea that Miriam was Moses's real sister, or that his nursemaid was in truth his very own mother.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From suspense to gentle warmth:** Keep your voice low and urgent while the mother hides her baby for three months and sets the reed chest upon the Nile; soften into a serene, radiant tone when Princess Bithia lifts the lid and sees baby Moses smile.
- **Miriam's quick wit:** Read Miriam's offer in a clear, natural, clever young voice, letting the quiet triumph and joy of mother and daughter shine through.

### Chapter 26: The Burning Coals

One day when Moses was three years old, Pharaoh sat dining at the royal banquet table. To the king's right sat the queen, and to his left sat Princess Bithia holding little Moses on her lap. High nobles of the court were invited to the feast, and as usual, the sorcerer Balaam was present as well.

Right in the middle of the banquet, quick as a single breath, little Moses leaned forward, reached out, snatched the golden crown straight off Pharaoh's head, and set it upon his own.

The nobles gathered around the table froze in horrified astonishment. Bithia hurried to lift the crown back onto her father's head. Pharaoh's face darkened like a storm cloud, and the king asked:

"What is to be done with a Hebrew child who dares do such a thing?"

Balaam, who had despised Moses bitterly from the very beginning, spoke up at once:

"Your Majesty, do not think this is mere child's play. This little Moses already understands everything full well. When he grows up, he will scheme to seize rule over all the people of Egypt! Your servant counsels Your Majesty to spill his blood upon the ground this very day!"

Seated at the banquet table that day was a high judge of the realm. An angel of God planted a wise thought within his heart. He spoke up:

"Your Majesty, the boy is still very young. First let us test whether he did this out of understanding or not. Command a servant to bring in two bowls. Let the first bowl hold precious jewels, and the second bowl hold glowing red coals of fire. Let the boy choose. If he reaches out for the jewels, let that be his death sentence, for it will prove he knows what treasures are. Yet if he touches the burning hot coals, that shows he is still innocent and knows nothing—there is no need to kill him, since what he did was completely thoughtless."

Pharaoh agreed to follow this advice. When the two bowls were carried in, Moses stretched his hand straight toward the bowl of glittering jewels. Yet an unseen angel gently nudged his hand aside so it dipped straight into the bowl of blazing coals.

Touching the scorching coals, Moses burst into tears. A patch of skin on his fingers was burned raw. Bithia hurried to rub soothing fragrant oil onto the burn and comfort him.

From then on, Pharaoh felt sure the little boy had only reached mischievously for the shiny thing perched upon his head. Moses's life was saved, though Balaam grumbled sourly in displeasure. From that day forward, the sorcerer never stopped scheming to destroy young Moses.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Climax at the banquet table:** Pick up the pace quickly as Moses snatches the crown, drop into a harsh, spiteful edge when Balaam demands the boy's death, then shift to a calm, wise tone for the high judge.
- **The moment of the test:** Read slowly with gentle suspense as the child's hand reaches toward the jewels and suddenly turns into the burning coals; keep a tender, aching warmth when Bithia rubs oil on his burned fingers.

### Chapter 27: Moses and the Egyptian

From childhood on, Moses was dressed in rich brocade just like Pharaoh's own royal sons and studied beside them under wise teachers. Pharaoh grew very fond of him, for with his sharp mind and courteous bearing, Moses always set a fine example for the princes. For this reason, he stood high in Pharaoh's favor.

In the year Moses turned fourteen, he went to visit Miriam and his parents. As the whole family sat together, his older sister said to him:

"Until today our family has kept a secret from you. Now you are old enough to know the truth. Moses, you are my own real brother; Mother here is your real mother; Amram is your father; and your older brother is named Aaron."

Then Miriam told him the story of the first months after he was born and described the bitter grief of the Hebrew mothers who saw their babies killed. For the very first time, Moses heard of his ancestor Joseph, of Jacob and Isaac, and of old Abraham setting out from the land of Ur and the city of Babylon.

Moses listened in deep wonder. He said:

"Now at last I understand why I have always loved coming back to this house, and why I feel everyone here is closer to my heart than Pharaoh or even Bithia. The blood inside me is speaking to me: I am an Israelite!"

Toward Pharaoh and Bithia, Moses remained deeply grateful; Bithia especially felt like an older sister to him. Yet from that time onward, he went often to visit his parents and sister Miriam. His brother Aaron was forced to perform crushing slave labor for the Egyptians. Moses watched his own people being cruelly beaten and worked without a single day of rest. He made up his mind to find a way to change their miserable lot.

Once he had grown into a mature, thoughtful young man, Moses appeared at court and spoke to Pharaoh:

"Honored Lord, you have always shown me kindness. Today may I ask you to grant me one special gift?"

Pharaoh replied:

"Tell me what weighs upon your heart."

Moses answered:

"Could it be possible, through Your Majesty's mercy and favor, for my Hebrew people to be granted one day of rest every week to keep a holy feast? After that day, they will return to their labor with fresh strength."

After thinking for a moment, Pharaoh replied:

"I grant your request."

Thus, through Moses, the Israelites were permitted to observe the weekly Sabbath day of rest. Yet Balaam fumed with anger at this concession.

One day, when Moses traveled again to the land of Goshen to see his family, his path led past Israelites toiling nearly naked beneath the blazing sun. Suddenly he heard cries of anguish coming from a lonely spot, where an Egyptian taskmaster was swinging his whip mercilessly until an Israelite streamed with blood.

Fury blazed up inside Moses's heart. He charged straight over, wrenched the whip from the taskmaster's grip, and swung the handle down with all his strength upon the man's skull. The deadly blow felled the taskmaster onto the sand, dead on the spot. The bleeding Hebrew hurried to help Moses bury the corpse quickly beneath the sand.

Yet the deed did not go unseen by other eyes. Someone carried word straight to Pharaoh. Balaam immediately declared:

"Does Your Majesty see? Now he has begun killing Egyptians! Before long it will be Pharaoh's own turn!"

Terrified, the king issued a secret order to put Moses to death.

Princess Bithia learned of the accusation. That night when Moses returned, she crept quietly into his room carrying a lamp and warned him that his life hung by a thread:

"Leave at once—flee from this palace and this city for a while. I hope Pharaoh's anger will eventually cool. When it does, I will send word to you through Miriam. For now, nowhere here is safe for you any longer."

God's angel planted so deep a dread inside Moses's heart that he departed that very night, leaving the land of Egypt behind. From that time forward, Pharaoh's hand pressed down even more ruthlessly upon the Israelites, and the king even stripped away their Sabbath rest.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Discovering his true roots:** Read sincerely and warmly where Miriam reveals the truth and young Moses declares proudly, *"I am an Israelite!"*
- **A sudden turn into danger:** Quicken your cadence through the scene of the taskmaster's whip in Goshen, then drop into a hushed, urgent whisper like Bithia speaking by lamplight at midnight.

### Chapter 28: Moses in Midian

Moses wandered for a long, weary time before finally arriving in the land of Midian. An angel of God let him know he might stay safely in this land in peace.

One afternoon, he came to a well and sat down nearby to rest. Just then, several young women of Midian brought their flocks of sheep to the well for water. A band of shepherds came up and began chasing the girls away from the well. Moses had always carried a keen sense of justice within his heart; he sternly rebuked the shepherds and helped the young women draw water for their sheep. Seeing how tall and powerfully built the stranger was, the shepherds did not dare start a fight.

Among the shepherdesses were seven sisters—the seven daughters of the priest Jethro. Returning home, they told their father:

"Today a man dressed in Egyptian clothing protected us from the shepherds, which is why we came home so early."

Jethro asked:

"Why did you not invite that man here as our guest?"

At once he sent someone out to invite the stranger home.

Moses felt so warm and comfortable under Jethro's roof that he decided to stay. The next day, he helped tend the flocks and lead them to water. He learned much from the priest's deep wisdom. Jethro came to trust Moses and gave his daughter Zipporah to him in marriage. She bore him two sons, Gershom and Eliezer.

One day Moses led the flocks out to graze in the desert of Sin. Late in the afternoon, a little kid goat strayed away toward Mount Horeb—a peak the people of that region called *"the Mountain of God."*

While searching for the little goat, Moses caught sight of a thornbush in the distance that seemed to be on fire. Who could have built a fire out in such a lonely place? Hurrying closer, he could not understand why not a wisp of smoke rose into the air, and why the bush burned brightly without ever being consumed by the flames. As he drew nearer still, the fire leaped higher and shone even brighter. A sacred voice called out from the midst of the flame:

"Moses, Moses!"

He answered:

"Here I am."

The voice spoke again:

"Draw nearer. The ground where you stand is holy ground. Take the sandals off your feet."

Moses obeyed the command and planted his shepherd's staff into the earth. The sacred voice continued:

"You have been chosen to deliver your downtrodden people from the power of the Egyptians. Go back to Egypt. Appear before Pharaoh. I will be with you."

Moses answered:

"Yet what if Pharaoh and the people refuse to believe me? What shall I do then?"

The Lord God replied:

"I will grant you power, signs, and wonders to perform. They will believe you. Go together with the elders of your people to meet Pharaoh. Demand that he let the people of Israel go. I Am Who I Am, the God of your forefathers and of the people of Israel. Before the people, you may call My name Yahweh."

Once more, Moses turned pale and pleaded:

"O Lord, I have never been strong with words. My tongue always grows heavy whenever I try to speak."

The Lord spoke:

"Take your brother Aaron along with you. He is already on the road coming to meet you. His speech is strong and clear. I will place words into the mouths of both you and your brother. I give My blessing to your staff, and by its power you shall perform many wonders!"

The flame withdrew from the thornbush, a radiant light soaring straight up to the stars. Moses remained kneeling there. He felt that sacred fire warming all through his body, burning steadily inside his heart. It was the flame of courage kindled within him. Gripping his staff tightly, he bowed his head very low in reverence before the spot where the Lord had spoken to him out of the fire. As he slipped his sandals back onto his feet, the little stray goat came bounding joyfully toward him. Moses scooped up the little goat and carried it back to the flock.

Because Jethro was a priest, Moses told him everything about the burning bush. He also told Zipporah of the mission that called him back to Egypt. She declared that she and their two sons would go with him to Egypt. Zipporah rode upon a donkey, Moses carried his blessed staff, and the two little boys walked chattering cheerfully at their side.

During the night, the Lord God appeared to Moses's brother Aaron and told him:

"Go out into the wilderness to meet Moses. He is returning to Egypt."

Guided by God's angel, the two brothers met midway along the trail. Moses recounted everything that had happened and explained what they must do in God's name to free the people of Israel and lead them out of Egypt.

From Aaron, Moses learned that while he had been away, the old Pharaoh had died, and an even harsher king now ruled the land. Both brothers knew that a hard battle lay waiting ahead of them.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From peaceful pastures to holy awe:** Begin warmly and gently at the well and in Jethro's household, then slow down into deep stillness as the smokeless fire blazes around the thornbush on Mount Horeb.
- **The revelation of Yahweh:** Speak *"I Am Who I Am..."* with quiet majesty, conveying how the flame of courage enters Moses's heart before closing with the little family setting out on the road and the news of the new Pharaoh.

### Chapter 29: The Battle with Pharaoh

Moses and Aaron called together the elders of the people of Israel and told them of the mission God had entrusted to them: to lead the entire people out of the land of Egypt. The elders and everyone gathered there rejoiced with all their hearts at these glad tidings.

The next morning, the two brothers entered the royal palace to confront Pharaoh. Moses carried the staff that God had blessed. Guarding the palace gates lay two chained lions. Moses waved his staff above their heads; at once both lions grew completely gentle and meek. He unfastened their chains, and the two lions trotted obediently behind him just like a pair of house dogs. The palace guards stared in sheer terror and scattered to either side. So the two brothers marched boldly right through the palace straight into Pharaoh's presence.

Pharaoh himself was terrified at the sight of these two men walking in with a pair of lions. The brothers' faces shone with fearless courage. What strange power had allowed them to tame those fierce beasts so easily? The king's bodyguards gripped their spears tightly, ready to hurl them if the lions sprang to attack. Pharaoh roared:

"What do you want here? Who are you? What have you done to my lions?"

Moses replied calmly:

"We are messengers of the God of Israel. God commands you: 'Let My people go, that they may offer sacrifice to Me in the wilderness!'"

Pharaoh answered:

"I do not know who your God is. I see plainly enough that both of you are Hebrews. Get out of here, both of you, and haul stones and make bricks like all the rest! As for whether I will let your people go sacrifice for three days or not, I shall ask the advice of my wise men."

Pharaoh dared not touch a single hair on the two brothers' heads, seeing they carried an extraordinary power that had mastered even lions. Not wishing to provoke the king further, Moses chained the two lions back up as before. Yet he held out little hope that the advice of Pharaoh's wise men would bring any good news for the people of Israel.

As soon as Moses and Aaron departed, Pharaoh summoned his sorcerer Balaam to court and told him the whole tale of the two Israelites and the two lions. Balaam replied:

"Those two fellows are merely sorcerers too. Yet my magic is stronger and will defeat them. You may be sure those two will come back again."

Three days went by, and word came down: the taskmasters had orders to drive the Israelites to even harsher labor. Moses and Aaron marched back before Pharaoh once again—this time without the lions. Pharaoh instantly sent for Balaam and challenged the brothers:

"Show me a sign of your magic!"

Aaron was holding the staff God had blessed, and he cast it down straight in front of Pharaoh. Touching the ground, the staff turned into a serpent. Balaam flung down his own staff as well, and his turned into a serpent too. Yet Aaron's serpent swallowed Balaam's serpent whole and changed back into a staff again. Balaam's eyes bulged wide with fright, and he hissed:

"Confound it—their power is mighty indeed!"

Once more, Moses demanded that Pharaoh release his people to offer sacrifice to God in the desert. Once more, Pharaoh stalled, asking for more time to think.

When night fell, the Lord God spoke to Moses:

"Early tomorrow morning, Pharaoh will go down to the bank of the River Nile. Go meet him there too. If he still refuses to let the people go, strike the water with your staff, and the river shall turn into blood."

The next morning, Moses and Aaron stood waiting to meet Pharaoh by the River Nile. Pharaoh wished nothing more than to order his soldiers to cut both men down right there on the spot, yet he dared not do it after seeing their signs with his own eyes. Even so, he hardened his face and refused to give an inch. Moses spoke aloud:

"The Lord, the God of Israel, has sent me to you. Let His people go to offer sacrifice in the wilderness! If you refuse, I will strike the waters of the Nile with this staff, and the river shall turn into blood. Every fish in the river will die, and the water will no longer be fit to drink."

Pharaoh stubbornly ignored him. Moses handed the staff to Aaron, and Aaron brought it crashing down upon the surface of the water. Instantly the river turned vivid red like blood and gave off a sickening stench. The Egyptians had to dig pits everywhere in the ground to find water to drink. This plague lasted seven full days.

Then the Lord God spoke to Moses again:

"Go before Pharaoh. If he will not let My people go, I will bring a plague of frogs over his entire country."

The king remained cold as stone and wholly unmoved. Aaron stretched out his staff over every river and canal in the kingdom. Before long, vast swarms of frogs crawled up out of the water, hopped thickly through every street, and crept into even the innermost chambers of every house. Yet not one single frog ever set foot in the Israelite quarter—and even Princess Bithia's palace was spared from the calamity. That was the second plague.

Pharaoh hastily summoned Moses and Aaron and cried:

"Take these frogs away! Rid this land of such a terrible plague! Your people may go sacrifice in the wilderness."

At once the frogs dropped dead right where they sat—inside every house, along every street, and across every public square. People had to sweep them up into piles that reeked of horrible decay.

Yet scarcely had the frogs been cleared away when Pharaoh broke his word and refused to let the Israelites go offer sacrifice.

Then the Lord God told Moses:

"Strike the dust of the earth with your staff, and countless swarms of gnats will rise up to torment the Egyptians."

Everything happened at once just as He had spoken, bringing the third plague down upon the land of Egypt—yet Pharaoh stayed stubborn still.

And the Lord God unleashed plague after plague upon them: dense swarms of biting flies covering every place; a deadly pestilence sweeping across the pastures so livestock collapsed by the score; painful, festering boils breaking out all over the Egyptians; violent hailstorms battering the earth; vast clouds of locusts descending to strip every field bare; and finally an impenetrable, pitch-black darkness blanketing the entire realm.

Furious, Pharaoh roared into Moses's face:

"If you ever dare show your face before me one more time, I will have you put to death!"

Moses answered calmly:

"I shall never appear before your face again!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Calm authority taming the lions:** Read slowly and steadily where Moses waves his staff over the two gate-guarding lions, highlighting the contrast between the brothers' fearless, radiant faces and the panic of the guards and Pharaoh.
- **Mounting pace through the nine plagues:** Keep a brisk, crisp rhythm through the serpent contest with Balaam, slow slightly for the blood-red Nile and the plague of frogs (giving a gentle lift to the detail that Princess Bithia was spared), then quicken steadily through the rapid succession of plagues from gnats to pitch-black darkness.

### Chapter 30: The Exodus

The Lord God spoke to Moses:

"I will bring one final plague down upon the Egyptians. After that, they will surely let you go. On the night of the tenth day of this month, in every Egyptian household, the firstborn son shall die—even Pharaoh's own crown prince. When the tenth day arrives, let every Israelite family choose a young lamb. Slaughter it and eat it as a farewell meal. Brush the lamb's blood onto both sides of your house doorway. When the Angel of Death sweeps through the whole land, he will pass over every home marked with that blood."

Everything came to pass exactly as He had instructed. At the stroke of midnight, all the firstborn sons of the Egyptians drew their final breath. Heartrending cries of grief rose up everywhere. In every single Egyptian home there lay a dead child to mourn.

Yet before the houses of the Hebrews, the Angel of Death passed silently by. For this reason, the lamb supper celebrated in remembrance of that night is called the Passover meal—the farewell supper of Israel in the land of Egypt—and this great event bears the name Pesach, or Passover.

That very night, Pharaoh's messengers came racing breathlessly to Moses and Aaron with a decisive command:

"Lead all your people out of this country! Leave Egypt forever!"

The Egyptians urged the Israelites anxiously to hurry up and be gone from their sight.

All the people had already made ready for departure. Donkeys and camels stood laden with provisions and leather waterskins; the herds of livestock were gathered together. It was a vast, mighty throng. Their road led across the Sea of Reeds onward toward the far-off land of Canaan. Moses arranged the people in marching order by the twelve tribes of the twelve ancestral brothers: the tribe of Reuben led the vanguard, while the tribe of Benjamin closed the rear of the endless column. Pure joy shone in every heart. All trusted wholly that God's hand, guiding them through Moses and Aaron, was leading them safely forward.

From the burial vault along the River Nile, Moses had men carry out the coffin holding the bones of their forefather Joseph, to be laid to rest one day in their homeland.

The endless column marched past towns and villages until it reached a stretch of desert. As evening shadows fell, a strange glowing cloud appeared before the eyes of Israel, shaped like a pillar or a giant shining staff of cloud. Moses ordered the whole host to halt and declared clearly:

"Let us prepare to offer a sacrifice of thanksgiving! Look—a sign from our God has appeared in the sky above! That sign will guide our way through all the days ahead."

At once the people pitched camp. Aaron prepared the offering, Moses lifted his voice in prayer, and the multitude joined together in songs of thanksgiving. When dawn broke, that radiant glow changed into a white Pillar of Cloud moving forward to guide the people. When night fell dark, a bright glow like a blazing fire kindled within that very cloud.

When Pharaoh realized that the whole Israelite nation had truly vanished, and that every building work and task in the cities had come to a standstill, the king grew sorry he had ordered them away. Wicked Balaam promptly offered advice:

"Send out a mighty army to pursue them and force them back to work once they have finished offering sacrifice in the desert."

Pharaoh immediately ordered all his commanders to lead his entire elite force—foot soldiers, horsemen, and war chariots—pursuing the Israelites day and night to force them back.

The Israelites had reached the very shore of the Red Sea when Egyptian messengers galloping swift as the wind caught up with them. They demanded to see Moses and Aaron and delivered an ultimatum:

"In Pharaoh's name, we command you to return to Egypt. His Majesty permitted you to go into the desert to sacrifice for three days only. Behind us approaches a mighty army. Unless you obey of your own accord, blood will flow like rivers! Beware if you dare defy our command!"

Moses answered in a ringing voice:

"Go back and tell Pharaoh and his generals that God has commanded us to lead the people of Israel back to their ancient homeland. They will never come back!"

Furious, the messengers wheeled their warhorses round and galloped swiftly back to report to the main army. Hearing the grim news, many Israelites fell into panic and wept and wailed, while those who had been trained as warriors shouted:

"When they come, we will fight back!"

Yet Moses received an altogether different command from God. He stretched his staff out over the sea, making a sign, and suddenly a mighty wind sprang up howling fiercely, driving the water backward across a shallow reach of the sea.

At that same moment, the guiding Pillar of Cloud swept downward and settled squarely between the people of Israel and the Egyptian army. The Egyptian horsemen riding at the front suddenly found themselves lost inside a dense, blinding fog, unable to take a single step farther. Meanwhile, the Israelites stepped firmly along the dry pathway opened straight through the sea while the strong wind held two walls of water standing on either side. By the time the mist began to clear, the very last of the Israelites were stepping safely up onto the far shore. The Egyptians stared after them in astonishment.

The Egyptian commander bellowed:

"Forward—chase them down!"

Horses reared up pulling war chariots charging ahead, and the whole roaring army surged down onto the seabed path between the two walls of waves. Yet scarcely had the entire Egyptian host poured into the middle of the sea path than the waters began crashing back down upon them. Horses' hooves and chariot wheels sank deep into the churning, boggy mud. Soldiers were swept away by the rushing flood and drowned every last one. Pharaoh's entire mighty army lay buried beneath the deep sea.

Standing on the far bank, the Israelites watched in awe as that great army met its destruction. Everyone recognized God's mighty hand in this deliverance, and countless voices burst into songs of praise. Moses's sister Miriam lifted her voice in a long song:

> *Lift up your voices, strike the timbrel loud—*
> *Pharaoh's great host has sunk beneath the waves.*
> *Lift up your voices, strike the timbrel loud—*
> *Deep in the Red Sea lie entombed*
> *Both warhorse and its rider bold.*
> *That proud, fierce host sank in the watery depths,*
> *While waves beneath our feet drew swiftly back,*
> *Opening a broad path straight before our eyes.*
> *Then veiled in cloud, our passing footprints hid*
> *At the raised sign of Moses's staff.*
> *O sacred Pillar of Fire from God above,*
> *You lead us onward with Your shining light,*
> *Our faithful companion all along the road*
> *Homeward toward our distant homeland.*
> *Lift up your voices, strike the timbrel loud—*
> *Pharaoh's fierce foes are drowned beneath the sea.*
> *Lift up your voices, strike the timbrel loud—*
> *Deep in the Red Sea they have sunk.*
> *With song and harp and brimming thanks we sing:*
> *May God be praised forever through this hymn!*

As Miriam sang, throngs of women formed dancing lines behind her, joyfully shaking tambourines in time. Again and again all day long, they made music and sang aloud:

> *Sing unto the Lord,*
> *The Most High, glorious and great,*
> *For horse and rider both*
> *He has cast down into the sea!*

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From mourning to freedom:** Keep a solemn, hushed awe on the first Passover night, then brighten your voice joyfully as the twelve tribes march in ordered ranks carrying Joseph's coffin under the shelter of the Pillar of Cloud and Pillar of Fire.
- **Climax at the Red Sea and Miriam's song:** Build urgency as the Egyptian host charges into the seabed and the waves crash back down; then keep a crisp, ringing drumbeat cadence for the two songs of praise led by Miriam and the dancing women.

### Chapter 31: Marah and Sin

Leaving the Sea of Reeds behind, the long, winding column of travelers marched toward the desert of Shur. For three whole days, both people and livestock walked on and on without finding a single drop of water. At last they reached a place called Marah, which means *"bitter wells."* And truly, the water there was both salty and terribly bitter—no one could swallow a sip of it. The people began to grumble and blame Moses. As they made camp at Marah, they squeezed the very last drops out of their leather waterskins.

Moses cried out to God for help, and the Lord showed him a special kind of wood. He was to throw that piece of wood into the well. Wonder of wonders, the water immediately lost all its bitterness and turned sweet and good to drink!

By the time the travelers set foot in the desert of Sin, all the food they had brought along was completely gone. They had to slaughter some of their livestock just to have enough to eat day by day. Once again, the people broke out in bitter complaints and reproaches.

Then the Lord stretched out His hand to rescue them with two miracles one right after the other: that very evening, an enormous flock of birds—all quails—swooped down and settled thickly right in the middle of the Israelite camp. The people caught them easily and roasted them until everyone had eaten their fill.

The next morning, just as the mist melted away beneath the rising sun, the whole desert floor was suddenly covered with tiny, fine grains that looked just like white hoarfrost spread over the earth. Moses lifted his voice and told the people:

"This is the bread of the desert—the manna that God has given us. Before the sun climbs high in the sky, each person may gather only enough to keep away hunger for one single day. Every morning, fresh manna will fall for everyone to collect."

That manna had a wonderfully sweet, delicious taste. Even so, a few greedy people scooped great handfuls into jars and pots to save up for the next day. That evening, when they opened their jars to look, the manna had turned rotten and gave off a terrible stench! From that day onward, everyone strictly obeyed Moses, and nobody dared gather more into their jars than they needed for a single day.

Not long afterward, the travelers entered a dry, rocky stretch of desert. Once more they ran completely out of water, and angry grumbling swept through the crowd again. God spoke to Moses:

"Go over there and strike that great boulder at the foot of the mountain with your staff!"

Moses did as He commanded. The boulder cracked wide open in two, and a stream of cool, fresh water gushed rushing out.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Vivid realism of the desert journey:** Keep a slightly weary, parched tone as the people grumble and squeeze the last drops from their waterskins, then brighten with relief and wonder when the water turns sweet, the quails swoop into camp, and the ground gleams white with manna.
- **A lesson in daily trust:** Speak Moses's instruction about gathering only enough for one day clearly and firmly, with a touch of startled caution when the hoarded jars of manna spoil by evening.

### Chapter 32: The Amalekites

Among the Israelites at that time was a courageous warrior named Joshua. He had studied the art of war among the Egyptians and had once served as a captain in their army. Moses took counsel with him:

"We are soon to pass through the territory of the Amalekites. The Amalekites are a fierce, warlike tribe, and they will certainly never let us cross their land in peace. Joshua, you are our sword of defense if we are attacked. Prepare carefully so that every man strong enough to carry a weapon stands ready whenever the Amalekites come against us."

At once Joshua sent scouts ahead to watch for any danger lurking along the way. One day, several scouts came running back breathlessly with urgent news:

"Only an hour's march ahead of us, the Amalekites are lying in secret ambush waiting for us—a vast army, hidden very cunningly!"

Joshua immediately had the trumpets blown as the signal to muster the warriors for battle. The rest of the people drew back and set up camp upon a hill.

Together with Aaron and a servant, Moses climbed to the highest summit of the hill so he could watch the course of the battle. Reaching the crest, he saw the Amalekite forces marching steadily closer. At once he gripped the sacred staff in both hands, raised it high toward the heavens, and prayed earnestly to God for help.

Amazingly, as long as Moses kept the staff held high in his two arms, Joshua and the Israelite warriors held the upper hand on the battlefield. Yet the moment Moses's arms grew tired and dropped slightly to rest, the Amalekites began pushing forward and gaining ground.

Moses called loudly to Aaron and the servant:

"Hold up my two arms!"

So Aaron and the servant stood on either side of Moses, each lifting and supporting one of his arms so they stayed raised high without dropping. Thanks to this, Joshua won a sweeping victory. Not until the sun sank into the shadows of evening were the Amalekites utterly defeated, and only then could the three men on the hilltop finally lower the staff and rest their aching arms.

Many brave warriors had fallen in that battle. With deep reverence, the Israelites laid the fallen to rest right in the heart of the desert, their hearts filled with gratitude for those who had willingly given their lives to shield their people. After that battle, the nation of Israel was able to continue its journey in peace, free from any obstacle.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Battle spirit and quiet faith:** Deliver Moses's charge to Joshua and the call of the war horns with crisp, resolute energy; then slow into a steady, weighty rhythm as Moses keeps his arms lifted high from morning until dusk.
- **Close in solemn gratitude:** Lower your voice to a warm, reverent hush at the desert burial of the fallen warriors so that 9–10-year-old listeners feel the quiet depth of sacrifice.

### Chapter 33: At Mount Sinai

After the battle with the Amalekites, the people of Israel journeyed across the desert for three months until they arrived at the foot of Mount Sinai. The guiding cloud wrapped itself around the mountaintop, so everyone understood: *We are going to stay here for a while.*

The Lord spoke to Moses:

"Climb up the mountain to meet Me. You shall receive the law for the people."

Moses gave his command:

"O people of Israel, as long as I remain upon the mountain, no one—whether human or beast—is permitted to draw near the foot of the mountain!"

He stationed guards to enforce the order and had a boundary fence built so everyone could see the limit clearly. He instructed the people for three whole days to keep far from every evil deed and wicked word:

"Be as reverent as when offering a sacrifice to God, for the Lord desires to speak with us out of the midst of thunder and flashes of lightning!"

Aaron, Joshua, and seventy elders were allowed to climb partway up the mountain with Moses, so they might be near during the hour of revelation. Yet where the wall of cloud began, they could go no farther; Moses alone climbed upward into the heights.

The cloud closed completely around him. He stepped into a cleft in the rocky mountainside. As soon as he entered, his body sank into a deep sleep. Two angels of God, Michael and Gabriel, lifted his soul high into the air. Because Moses's soul had drunk of the sacred fire at the burning bush, he stayed wide awake as he was carried up into the lofty heavens. Before his eyes, all the splendor of the spiritual world opened wide.

First, he was led to a towering gateway. Standing guard there was the stern angel Kamuel, keeper of heaven's gate, who permits only ripened souls to enter the realm of the seven heavens. Because God had called Moses by name, he was granted passage. The noblest light shone across that celestial realm, glowing with the colors of the heavenly temple: violet-blue, royal purple, crimson, and dazzling white.

Far down at the foot of the mountain, the higher Moses rose, the brighter the lightning flared and the fiercer the thunder crashed. Everyone fell flat upon the trembling earth. Many fainted from the terrifying roar, yet not a single person or animal uttered a cry. Every bird vanished from the sky and tucked itself deep inside its nest.

High above, Moses was guided step by step through the realms of heaven.

In the first heaven, like an angel himself, Moses looked down upon all the sorrows and joys of humankind. He heard prayers and songs of thanksgiving, and he also heard bitter curses and discontent. He saw the fruits of hatred, of tender pity, and of love.

From the second heaven, he gazed upon the world of the elements: winds, shifting weather, tempests, and thunder and lightning.

In the third heaven, he beheld the heavenly patterns of every herb, mighty tree, blossom, fruit, and seed of grain. He saw the angels of the Third Day of Creation and their rule over the kingdom of plants.

In the fourth heaven, he beheld the radiant spirits of the stars from the Fourth Day of Creation—those mighty beings who guide and weave in harmony with the music of the sun, the moon, and all the stars.

In the fifth heaven, he saw the powers of warmth and fire, alongside the powers of bitter cold, frost, and ice.

Then Moses was brought before the Rulers of the sixth and seventh heavens. At the dawn of time, those Beings had labored alongside God to shape the very first human being, and now they guide the destiny of every nation and every individual soul. The primeval light of the Garden of Paradise shone all around him in such serene bliss that he almost wished to stay there forever and never go back down to the earth.

Yet he was led downward again, toward the great abyss. He arrived at the gates of the demonic realm and saw clearly all the dark doings of the evil spirits—those who always gloat whenever human beings do wrong and bring misery upon themselves. Upon the threshold, Moses saw the Scales of Justice weighing good and evil. He also saw and heard the torment of souls who come to that place to be purified from every wicked deed they had wrought during life. He whispered within himself:

*"When I return to the earth, I will guide humankind to do what is upright and live in goodness!"*

Now Gabriel led him upward once more into the Fields of Bliss, which the Scriptures call the Garden of Paradise. Near the Tree of Life, he met three noble patriarchs: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He spoke with them face to face. The three fathers rejoiced with all their hearts to see that their people had found in Moses a leader so righteous and steadfast.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A stately cadence through the seven heavens:** Keep your voice steady and luminous, rising gently in wonder from the first heaven to the seventh so young listeners can clearly follow the order of the cosmos.
- **Contrast the abyss and the Land of Bliss:** Drop into a heavy, solemn tone beside the Scales of Justice, then warm again with quiet joy when Moses greets the three patriarchs beside the Tree of Life.

### Chapter 34: The Ten Commandments

For forty days and forty nights, Moses remained in the high heavens above Mount Sinai. After his soul had journeyed through the loftiest heights and deepest abysses of the spiritual world, the Lord spoke to him:

"So that your people waiting below may know how each one must walk upon earthly paths, I will give you the Ten Commandments."

The Lord let Moses behold them shining forth in letters of living fire, and with his own hands Moses carved every word onto two tablets of stone. He was to carry these Ten Commandments down the mountain to his people, teaching Israel to live by the path of righteousness:

The First Commandment: You shall have no other gods before Me.

The Second Commandment: You shall not carve any idol to bow down and pray to.

The Third Commandment: You shall not curse or take the name of the Lord in vain.

The Fourth Commandment: You shall keep the Sabbath day holy.

The Fifth Commandment: You shall honor your father and your mother.

The Sixth Commandment: You shall not murder.

The Seventh Commandment: You shall not be unfaithful or commit adultery.

The Eighth Commandment: You shall not steal.

The Ninth Commandment: You shall not bear false witness to harm your neighbor.

The Tenth Commandment: You shall not covet anything that belongs to your neighbor.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Majesty on the mountain peak:** Read slowly, evoking the glowing letters of fire amidst forty quiet days and nights.
- **Distinct pauses for each commandment:** Pause briefly after each commandment from the first to the tenth so every law rings out clearly, as though freshly carved in stone.

### Chapter 35: The Golden Calf

For many weeks, the people waited for Moses to return. When they saw no sign of him coming down the mountain, many grew convinced he would never come back at all. The evil spirit Semael whispered to himself: *"Moses is gone! Now is my chance to lead Israel astray!"*

Down in Egypt, the people had seen statues of sacred bulls inside the temples, standing as images of the gods. Semael slipped into many minds the notion that they ought to cast a golden calf as an image of the Lord, just like the Egyptians did. Both men and women brought out their golden jewelry to give. Skilled metalworkers melted the gold down into a gleaming golden calf and set it upon a stone altar. The people had just kindled sacrificial fires and begun dancing round the calf when the fortieth day arrived—the very hour Moses made ready to come down the mountain.

The voice of the Lord spoke to Moses as they parted:

"Go down to your people now. They are doing wickedly. They have cast a golden calf and are bowing in prayer before it."

On the path down the mountain, Moses met Joshua again—through all those long days, Joshua had waited faithfully for his return. Stepping out onto a jutting ledge of rock, the two men looked down into the valley where the camp lay spread out. They heard wild, rowdy singing floating upward and saw men and women clasping hands and dancing in a ring around the golden calf.

In fierce wrath, Moses hurled the two stone tablets down against the cliffside, shattering them to pieces! Like a roaring lion, he charged straight down into the middle of the crowd, sending the people scattering in terror on every side. He seized a heavy mallet and smashed the idol apart, then ordered the goldsmiths to grind every scrap into fine gold dust. Moses poured all that gold dust into a great vat of water, stirred it well, and made every single person who had sacrificed and danced around the calf drink down that bitter, gold-filled water to the last drop.

Then a dazzling radiance never seen before shone forth from Moses's face and brow. He carried that heavenly glow down with him from Mount Sinai, where he had bathed in the light of God.

Moses spoke to the whole assembly:

"You have committed a terrible sin. I must speak with the Lord to learn how you may atone for it. You are not yet ready to enter the land of Canaan. You must pass through many more trials first. Now your wandering through the desert will last for many years!"

Many hung their heads in deep shame that in one fleeting moment they had forgotten their divine Deliverer and traded the God of Mount Sinai for a calf of gold.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A sly voice for Semael:** Drop into a cunning, shadowy whisper as the evil spirit tempts the people to remember the bull idols of Egypt.
- **Powerful climax:** Pick up speed and intensity as Moses shatters the stone tablets, charges down like a roaring lion, and smashes the golden idol—then soften into awe when that unearthly radiance shines upon his brow.

### Chapter 36: The Tabernacle

Once more, Moses climbed Mount Sinai and carved the Ten Commandments anew upon two tablets of stone. Up on the mountain, God spoke with him again:

"The people need a sanctuary where they can worship. Build a sacred tent that can serve as a temple traveling alongside you on your journey."

The Lord gave Moses the design, measurements, and clear instructions for how everything inside should be arranged. This sacred tent was called the Tabernacle.

All the Israelites who had not cast their gold into the golden calf now brought gold with glad hearts to build the Tabernacle. Those who had no gold gave colorful woven cloth or fine carpets. Skilled craftspeople raised the sacred tent and fashioned all the holy vessels.

Inside the tent lay the deepest, innermost room, called the Holy of Holies, where stood a wooden chest holding the two stone tablets of the Ten Commandments. They named it the Ark of the Covenant, because the Lord had sealed a new covenant with the people through those commandments. Two golden figures of angels knelt upon its lid. In front of the Ark stood the Mercy Seat, where Moses sat whenever he spoke with the Lord. Only Moses was allowed to step inside the Holy of Holies.

Outside that secret inner room was the Holy Place set apart for the priests. Here stood the seven-branched lampstand, mirroring the seven days of creation and the seven wandering planets moving across the sky. From two incense burners, sweet, fragrant smoke drifted upward as a sign of sacrifice. Twelve loaves of bread lay displayed, standing for the twelve tribes of Israel and the twelve houses of the heavens. The walls of the tent were hung with curtains and rich tapestries, stretched over sturdy wooden posts.

According to God's will, Moses appointed his brother Aaron as High Priest of the Tabernacle and Aaron's sons as priests to assist him. Out in the courtyard before the Tabernacle stood the altar, where day after day the fire of thanksgiving burned bright. Beside the altar stood a large water basin where the priests washed their hands before offering sacrifice. The sacred tent was fashioned so that it could be taken apart whenever the people set out on the march again.

When Moses brought the Ten Commandments back from Sinai, he gathered all the people together and read out the laws in a clear, ringing voice that reached every heart. Everyone vowed to keep them. Every day Aaron and his priestly sons read them aloud, so before long many knew them by heart.

When the Tabernacle was finished at last, the whole nation held a service of consecration. The tribes gathered round the altar while Moses and Aaron made ready the offering. Suddenly a wonder came to pass: the very cloud that had rested atop Mount Sinai came gliding down and stood upright like a tall pillar of cloud above the Holy of Holies! Now all the people knew without a doubt: *"God is with us! From this day on, we will never bow to a golden calf again. We offer our prayers and sacrifices to the living God, who led us out of Egypt!"*

The Holy of Holies filled with heavenly radiance whenever Moses entered to speak with the Lord. And so the sacred tent became the living sign that God journeyed with His people from Sinai onward. Whenever they needed a new place to camp, the pillar of cloud lowered itself over a spot where they could dig wells for water, and there they pitched the tent and stayed. When the pillar of cloud rose high into the sky again, that was the signal to strike camp and continue on their way.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Measured, clear description:** Read slowly and distinctly as you walk through the Tabernacle—from the Holy of Holies and the Ark of the Covenant out to the seven-branched lampstand and the twelve loaves of bread.
- **The moment of wonder:** Pause for a beat right before the cloud descends from Mount Sinai to stand upright as a pillar above the Holy of Holies.

### Chapter 37: The Wilderness of Zin

In the Wilderness of Zin, both people and beasts suffered a fierce, burning thirst. Loud complaints broke out on every side:

"Why did we ever leave Egypt behind? Back there we had plenty of water to drink! Have we come all this way only to die of thirst in the middle of the desert?"

Moses went inside the Holy of Holies to speak with God. The voice of the Lord answered him:

"Gather all the people together. Stand before them and speak to the rocks, so that they may share their water with you."

Obeying His word, Moses called the entire assembly before the desert boulders. Twice he struck the rock face with his staff. The rock split wide apart, and a stream of pure, sparkling water leaped forth, bringing cool relief to both herds and people! Many who had just been arguing bitterly with Moses now bowed their heads in shame.

On another day, while the people were encamped at the foot of Mount Hor, the Lord revealed to Moses:

"Go up the mountain with Aaron and his son Eleazar. Aaron's time upon the earth is finished. When you reach the summit, place the father's priestly garments upon the son."

Moses did as the Lord commanded. As he climbed the mountain on that final walk beside his brother, Moses remembered the day Aaron had gone with him to face Pharaoh, and how bravely his brother had stood shoulder to shoulder with him on every road and in every battle. High atop Mount Hor, Aaron passed the High Priest's vestments to his son Eleazar, and Moses gave his blessing to both father and son. Then Aaron's soul left his body and soared straight above the mountaintop into heaven to meet the patriarchs. Eleazar piled up a burial mound for his father right there on Mount Hor.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrast thirst and cooling relief:** Let tension tighten your voice in the cries of thirst, then open into fresh relief when cool water bursts from the split rock.
- **Quiet tenderness at Aaron's farewell:** Slow down with warm brotherly affection as Moses recalls their shared journey and speaks his blessing atop Mount Hor.

### Chapter 38: The Bronze Serpent

Throughout the long journey across the wilderness, the people grumbled and complained time and again. Leaving Mount Hor behind, they crossed a dry, barren, miserable stretch of land. This time the Israelites did not merely blame Moses—they muttered bitterly against God Himself! A full-blown rebellion was ready to erupt.

To awaken them from their folly, the Lord sent a stern punishment: from near and far on every side, swarms of venomous snakes came slithering right into the camp! They bit the grumblers, and many died from the deadly venom. Terrified, the people ran weeping and pleading to Moses:

"We have sinned by daring to speak against the Lord and against you! Pray that God may have mercy on us!"

Then Moses had a tall pole raised high in the camp, and atop it he set the figure of a serpent cast in bronze, curved in the shape of the letter 'S.' Whoever had been bitten by a snake and looked up with trust toward that sign did not die from the bite, and the venomous serpents crept away of their own accord and vanished.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Urgent, warning atmosphere:** Convey creeping unease as snakes slither into the camp from all four sides amid angry grumbling.
- **The healing gaze:** Brighten and clarify your tone on the image of looking upward to the bronze serpent shaped like the letter 'S' raised high upon the pole.

### Chapter 39: Balaam Appears Again

The people of Moab and their ruler, Prince Balak, were gripped with fear when they heard that the Israelites were about to march past their borders. Balak hastily sent messengers to summon the sorcerer Balaam—who had left Egypt a very long time ago. The messengers gave him Balak's message:

"Balaam, I want you to lay a curse upon this nation, and when they pass near, use your magic arts to spellbind them and drive them back! I will pay you a rich reward for doing this."

With two servants trailing behind him, Balaam climbed onto his donkey and set off down the road to meet Balak. Midway along the journey, an angel of God stood squarely in the path right before the donkey. Balaam could not see the angel at all, yet his donkey saw him very clearly! Frightened out of its wits, the poor beast bolted straight off the road into a field and stood rooted as stiff as a wooden post. Balaam lashed and beat the donkey; his two servants rushed to haul it back onto the trail, and the little company traveled onward a ways farther. Soon they came to a stretch where the road squeezed through a narrow gap between two stone walls.

Once again, the angel stood directly across their path. This time the donkey could dodge neither to the right nor to the left. It jammed itself so hard against one stone wall that Balaam's foot got crushed tight against the rock! Balaam yelled at the top of his lungs and thrashed the donkey soundly, while the two servants crowded in—one pushing from one side, the other tugging from the other. Left with no escape, the donkey plodded unwillingly onward. At an even tighter pinch in the narrow lane, the angel blocked the way a third time. Down dropped the donkey onto both front knees, leaving Balaam sliding right off its back to stand awkwardly on his own two feet in the middle of the path. Balaam's temper exploded! He seized his walking stick in both hands, ready to beat the poor animal's hide to ribbons. Yet suddenly, the donkey made strange sounds such as Balaam had never heard in all his life. His stick slipped right out of his fingers, and he listened in amazement as the donkey stammered in a thick, hoarse, halting voice:

"Wh-wh-why do you b-b-beat your faithful d-d-donkey so hard? L-l-look straight a-a-head there, Ba-la-a-am!"

Then at last Balaam's eyes were opened! Finally he saw an angel holding a shining sword, standing tall and motionless right in the center of the road. He threw himself flat upon the ground, pressing his face into the dust. The angel spoke to him:

"Go on and meet Balak. But you may speak only the exact words I tell you: The people of Israel are blessed by God!"

When Balak heard that Balaam was drawing near, he came out himself to give him a grand welcome and led the sorcerer up to a high hilltop from which they could gaze out over the Israelite camp far below. Balak hurriedly had an altar built for Balaam. As soon as the sacrifice stood ready, Balaam heard that very same voice sounding within him: *"The people of Israel are blessed by God!"*

Balak urged him impatiently:

"Come now, Balaam, speak your curse right away!"

Balaam replied:

"How can I curse where God bestows blessing?"

Hearing that, Balak flew into a towering rage:

"I sent for you to lay a curse upon my enemies, and here you are blessing them instead! Clear out and go straight back where you came from!"

Balaam climbed back onto his donkey, and away it galloped briskly—as cheerful and full of pep as if it had just feasted to its heart's content on seventy-seven wild thistles!

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Blend humor with suspense:** Give lively pace to the three times the donkey dodges the angel—bolting into the field, scraping Balaam's foot against the stone wall, and dropping to its knees in the narrow pass.
- **A one-of-a-kind donkey voice:** Draw out the donkey's stammering scold so 9–10-year-old listeners laugh with delight, then shift immediately into wide-eyed awe when Balaam's eyes are opened.
- **Lighthearted chapter ending:** Read briskly and playfully as the donkey gallops off as joyfully as though it had eaten seventy-seven wild thistles.

## Part VI: Joshua

### Chapter 40: Joshua Takes Leadership

The Lord spoke to Moses:

"Before long, you too shall go to join your forefathers just as your brother Aaron did, right before this people crosses into the land of Canaan. Before the eyes of the whole nation, pass your great charge on to Joshua and give him your blessing. When you depart from this earth and can lead the tribes no longer, Joshua will guide their way."

Moses sent word to gather the entire nation before the doorway of the Tent of Meeting. This was done only when some deeply momentous decree had to be announced. On that day, no one knew what was about to happen—no one except High Priest Eleazar, Aaron's son, to whom Moses had already confided God's plan for Joshua.

The young priests kindled the fire for a solemn sacrifice right before the Tent of Meeting. Moses, Eleazar, and Joshua stepped forward together. Moses lifted his voice and spoke:

"O people of Israel, my own people! The Lord has made known to me that only a brief time remains for me to guide you upon this earth. Therefore I proclaim His holy will to you: when my appointed hour closes, Joshua shall lead you upon the field of battle and along every path toward the land of Canaan."

With those words, Moses laid both his hands upon Joshua's shoulders. The newly named leader sank to his knees. In full sight of the assembled people, Moses blessed him and handed on to him the majesty and dignity of his leadership. He gave Joshua this charge:

"Be strong and steadfast, for you will lead this people into the land the Lord has given them. He Himself goes before you. Do not be afraid—be strong and steadfast!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Ceremonial grandeur before the Tent of Meeting:** Speak slowly and clearly, like a trumpet summoning the whole nation to a sacred ceremony.
- **The core words of succession:** Give warm, resonant depth to *"Do not be afraid—be strong and steadfast!"* so the phrase echoes forward into the very next chapter.

### Chapter 41: The Unknown Grave

While Moses, Eleazar, and Joshua finished setting everything in order, the people were encamped at the foot of Mount Nebo. God spoke to Moses:

"Climb up this mountain. From its height you may gaze out over all the land of Canaan, which shall become the homeland of your people. Your feet will not step onto that soil, for right upon this mountaintop, I will set your soul free to rise into the heavenly world."

As Moses said farewell to Joshua and Eleazar, Joshua begged him earnestly:

"Moses, if you must leave us here upon the earth, please at least stay ever near to us in spirit!"

Once more, Moses lifted his hands to bless Joshua. Soon afterward, he slipped quietly out of the people's camp. Step by step, he climbed higher and higher up toward the peak of Mount Nebo. From that lofty height, he looked far out across the rich, fertile land of Canaan, where green forests flourished lush and full. He stretched both arms wide, as though longing to gather that entire land straight into his heart. Then he saw a radiant glow spreading far and bright across all Canaan. Like a song, an echo floated down from high heaven:

"Here is where the Messiah shall one day be born—out of the very people you have led!"

Then Moses's soul was lifted up on high, while his body sank gently down upon the earth. People tell that the archangels Michael and Gabriel carried his soul upward through the nine heavens—the very same two angels who long ago had led him from the peak of Sinai into the spiritual world.

Moses has no grave known to any mortal. The people searched for him far and wide, yet they could never find his body and so could build him no tomb. Many believed the earth itself had opened softly to welcome him into its keeping. Only his spirit appeared to Joshua one last time, speaking a final farewell with those same faithful words of old:

"Do not be afraid. Be strong and steadfast!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Vast vista from Mount Nebo:** Keep your voice gentle and expansive as Moses opens his arms to fold Canaan into his heart and listens to the prophetic song of the Messiah.
- **Serene, quiet closing:** Drop to a soft hush as Moses's body sinks to the earth, pausing gently before his final farewell spoken to Joshua alone.

### Chapter 42: Crossing the Jordan

Together with Moses's blessing, the Lord's gift of inner revelation passed on to Joshua as well. That quiet voice within his soul spoke to Joshua again, giving him this command:

"Take up the Ark of the Covenant holding the two stone tablets and set out upon the road! When the bearers reach the edge of the River Jordan, they must walk straight into the water without the slightest hesitation."

Joshua chose one priest from every tribe to carry the Ark of the Covenant. Those twelve priests were to bear the Ark at the very front of the column, while the whole nation followed close behind.

The long column marched right up to the riverbank. Full of courage, the priests leading the way stepped straight into the flowing current—and wonder of wonders, the river slowly parted to either side before them! Right in the center of the dry riverbed, the priests set the Ark of the Covenant down and stood still. Every single soul of Israel walked across the bed of the River Jordan with dry feet.

Through that first mighty wonder, Joshua won deep trust from the entire nation, showing them that God's word and guidance truly stood with him, and from that day onward everyone faithfully followed his commands. Joshua then had twelve great tall stones raised upright along the banks of the River Jordan to keep the memory of this miracle alive forever.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Resolute forward march:** Keep a steady, determined cadence matching the twelve priests carrying the Ark straight into the river without pausing.
- **Highlight the second water miracle:** Soften slightly in amazement at the moment the river parts and the whole nation crosses dry-shod.

---

### Chapter 43: The Fall of Jericho

Perched high upon the steep hilltops guarding the gateway into Canaan stood the strongly walled city of Jericho. When the king of that city heard news that the Israelites were drawing near, he declared:

"We will never let these foreigners pass peacefully by! We shall go out and fight them!"

Just then, Joshua secretly sent two clever, quick-witted young men as spies to Jericho to see how matters stood inside. He gave them a handful of silver coins to carry along for safety. When the two young men arrived at the city gate, the guard barked in a gruff, unpleasant voice:

"What do you two want inside the city?"

They answered politely:

"Please let us come in to quench our thirst, and may we pay you this good silver to fill our waterskins?"

They pressed a silver coin into the guard's hand; seeing the money, he nodded gladly and opened the gate to let them through. The two young men strolled through every lane and street of the city, using sharp eyes to note the layout of the roads and the thickness of the city walls. Afterward, they stopped by a well to fill their waterskins with drinking water.

A woman named Rahab struck up a friendly chat with the two young men. Since twilight was closing in and she liked the polite pair, she invited them back to her house to stay the night. Yet another woman had been listening secretly to their talk beside the well; she guessed right away that the strangers were Israelites because their way of speaking sounded unlike the local people. She ran straight to the palace to carry her suspicions to the king:

"Your Majesty, Rahab has brought two young Israelites into her house to lodge!"

Inside her home, Rahab had already fixed a hidden sleeping spot for the two young men right up beneath the roof. She told them:

"I know you two are Israelites, yet God is with you. Everyone in our city has already heard the story of how you crossed the River Jordan. Truly, God is always by your side."

Scarcely had she finished speaking when loud, frantic pounding rattled the door downstairs! Startled and frightened, Rahab urged the two young men quickly:

"Hurry, crawl up and hide under those bundles of flax! The soldiers are searching for you! If they find you, they will kill you both!"

She rushed downstairs and opened the door. Standing outside on the porch was a captain of the king with a band of armed soldiers. The officer shouted straight into Rahab's face:

"Hand over the two men who just went home with you! They are Israelite spies!"

Keeping wonderfully calm, Rahab answered:

"It is true that two men did stop by my house a little while ago, but right as dusk fell and it came time to close the city gates, they slipped out again. I have no idea which road they took. They left only moments ago—they cannot have gotten far! If you chase after them right now, you are sure to catch them!"

Hearing that, the king's captain spun sharply on his heel, ordered the city gates thrown open at once, and led his soldiers racing out into the dark toward the River Jordan.

When Rahab climbed back up to where the two young men lay hidden, she spoke in a low voice:

"If one day you capture this city, please promise me that you will protect and spare my father and mother, my brothers and sisters, and all their children. Will you swear this oath to me?"

The two young men promised firmly:

"We pledge our very lives as a guarantee—for in our moment of danger just now, you saved us from certain death!"

Guessing that the patrol would surely come back late at night to search every corner of the house, Rahab tied a rope and let the two young men climb down to the ground through her window. Because her house was built directly onto the city wall, as soon as the two slid down to the earth, they stood completely outside the city! Before parting, the two young men gave her clear instructions:

"Your rope is dyed deep crimson. When the great army of Israel marches up, tie this red rope in your window and bring all your relatives together inside your house. We will make sure your home is kept safe."

The very next morning, Rahab tied the crimson rope out of her window and fastened another length right beside her front door. She had no idea which day or which hour the Israelite army might arrive.

When the two scouts circled back by safe paths to Joshua, they told him clearly everything that had happened inside Jericho. Listening to their report, Joshua declared firmly:

"Not a single hair on Rahab's head—nor on anyone in her family—shall ever be harmed!"

Meanwhile, the king of Jericho hatched a plan of his own: *I will let the Israelites march right on past while I hold firm behind these thick stone walls. Once they have gone by, I will launch surprise raids from behind, wave after wave, wearing their army down piece by piece.*

Joshua, however, meant to do just the opposite—he intended to surround Jericho and take it!

When the vast army of Israel drew near, Jericho slammed shut its giant wooden gates. Joshua pitched a siege camp directly in front of the city. On the seventh day, the priests carrying the Ark of the Covenant marched around the city walls seven times in a row. The soldiers followed behind amidst the ringing blasts of seven bronze trumpets and ram's-horn trumpets, while the rear guard held steady behind to protect the column. Joshua ordered the entire army that the instant they saw his signal, every citizen and warrior must lift up one mighty, thunderous roar together. They could not step too close beneath the walls, for Jericho's defenders shot down showers of arrows from above and slung stones furiously at the Israelites.

At last, when Joshua raised his hand to give the signal, the seven bronze trumpets and the ram's horns blared skyward all at once, and from more than a hundred thousand throats burst an earth-shaking shout that made the very air shudder! All at once, a miracle took place: the sturdy stone walls of Jericho trembled, began to crack apart, and collapsed in roaring heaps of rubble! Israelite warriors scrambled swiftly over the mounds of shattered stone and rushed inside. Fierce flames blazed up and burned the whole city to ashes. Crowds of townsfolk fled in terror for their lives.

Even as the stronghold fell into Israel's hands, the two young scouts hurried straight to the house marked with the red rope to protect Rahab and all her family. Rahab and her loved ones were led safely outside and continued onward alongside the people of Israel along the journey ahead. Jericho remained a heap of desolate ruins and was never rebuilt again. To this very day, weathered traces of those ancient walls still stand near the shores of the Dead Sea.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Suspense like a spy adventure:** Lower your voice conspiratorially as the two scouts slip through the gate, hide under bundles of flax, and as Rahab calmly outwits the king's soldiers pounding at her door.
- **Slow down at the crimson rope:** Read the promise between Rahab and the scouts clearly so 9–10-year-old listeners recognize a protective sign just like the lamb's blood on Passover night.
- **Wall-shattering climax:** Build volume and brisk momentum through the seven circuits, the blast of trumpets and ram's horns, and the shout of over a hundred thousand voices bringing down the walls.

### Chapter 44: In the Land of Canaan

The people of Israel had passed through countless harsh trials and hard-fought battles across forty years of wandering through the wilderness. Now, at long last, the hour had come for them to settle peacefully in the land of Canaan. The sacred tent—the Tabernacle with the Ark of the Covenant inside—was carried solemnly to rest upon the hill of Baala. There, day after day, the priests offered sacrifices upon the altar, generation after generation.

In later days, King David brought the Sanctuary into the royal city of Jerusalem. And right there, his son King Solomon built the splendid Temple and set the Ark of the Covenant together with all the holy vessels inside that Temple. From those days onward rang out the prophecy foretelling that one day the Messiah would appear in Jerusalem and bring fresh, joyful tidings—not only for the people of Israel, but for all the nations across the entire earth.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Gentle transition into a new age:** Read unhurriedly and with quiet relief after forty weary years, bringing the desert journey to a peaceful close.
- **Lingering echo of prophecy at the Book's end:** Slow slightly and keep a warm, luminous tone on the final two sentences speaking of the Temple in Jerusalem and the future Messiah of all nations.

## Part VII: Saul and David

### Chapter 45: Samuel and Saul

After the people of Israel had lived in Canaan for quite a long time, they began saying to one another more and more:

"We must have a king, just like all the other nations!"

In those days, there lived in Israel a priest and highest judge named Samuel. He could hear the voice of God speaking deep within his own heart. Samuel was not pleased to see so many of the people demanding a king. He told them:

"God is your King!"

Yet the people still wanted a visible king to rule right in their midst. Samuel prayed to God, asking for a sign to guide him in this matter. God spoke to Samuel:

"Let them have an earthly king. I will give you a sign."

Samuel did not yet know who would be worthy to be raised to the throne, so he waited quietly for the promised sign.

Now in the tribe of Benjamin there lived a wealthy man named Kish, who owned great herds of livestock. He had a son named Saul, who stood a full head and shoulders taller than every other young man. No youth was handsomer than Saul, and wherever he went, he was warmly welcomed. One day, his father Kish said to him:

"Saul, my son, three of our she-asses have broken loose and run away. Take a servant with you and go search for them. Pack plenty of bread in your bag, for the road may be long."

Saul and the servant crossed rolling hills and searched through the rugged mountains for the donkeys. Here and there, whenever they met travelers and asked about the straying animals, they heard the reply:

"Yes, we saw your donkeys! They were galloping along, chasing one another!"

Saul and his servant followed the trail. Every now and then they spotted fresh donkey droppings along the path, and both of them cheered right up. When the first night fell, they slept outdoors beneath the starry sky. The next day, they pressed on. Suddenly, they lost every trace of the animals and wandered farther and farther from home. On the third day, just as the two of them were about to turn back, a wayfarer—whom they had asked for news, though he knew nothing about the donkeys—suddenly said:

"In the very next city there lives a wise man, a seer. He serves as a judge and can see hidden things. Go and ask him! Who knows—perhaps he can find your lost donkeys!"

When the traveler was out of sight, Saul asked his servant:

"What gift do we have to offer this wise man if we ask for his counsel? We have eaten every last scrap of our bread."

The servant answered:

"Master, I have a silver piece in my pouch that I brought along for an emergency. You can present this silver coin to the seer when we ask for his help."

Saul agreed, and together the two set off toward the city.

Only the day before, God had given Samuel a sign: *"Tomorrow, the man whom you must raise up as king of Israel will meet you at the city gate."* So early the next morning, Samuel stood waiting right there at the gate. When Saul's tall figure stepped through the gateway, Samuel watched him closely. And behold, the stranger walked straight up to him and asked:

"Sir, could you tell me where in this city the house of Samuel the seer is?"

Samuel looked at the young man with a warm smile and replied:

"I am he. Come with me. The two of you may eat and drink at my house. I will tell you everything your heart wishes to know, and tomorrow I will send you on your way. As for the three donkeys that ran away from your father's house three days ago, worry no more about them. They have already been found and are safely back home."

Saul was amazed at the seer's calm certainty, and at how he already knew all about the donkeys. He thought to himself, *"It seems he has something else he wants to tell me."* Yet he had no idea what Samuel meant to do. Samuel brought the two travelers home and prepared a feast for them. At the table, Saul was invited to sit in the highest seat of honor. He wondered silently, *"I am only an ordinary farmer's son from the tribe of Benjamin. Why does the seer show me such great respect?"*

Samuel's house had a flat roof, as was the custom for every house in that land. A staircase led straight up to the roof. Samuel said to Saul:

"Sleep upon the rooftop beneath the dome of stars. Your servant may sleep on the straw with the sheep. Tomorrow morning I will wake you."

He had a soft carpet spread on the flat roof as a bed for Saul. For a long while, Saul could not close his eyes, turning over and over in his mind everything he had spoken about with the seer. He tried to guess what the old man meant to do with him, to treat him with such deep reverence. Only when the midnight stars rose high overhead did Saul finally drift off to sleep.

When the rosy dawn began to brighten the sky, Samuel climbed the stairs, woke Saul, and bade him stand. As the rising sun cast its first golden rays across the heavens, Samuel poured a few drops of sacred oil from a little flask upon Saul's head. He kissed him and declared solemnly:

"God has chosen you to be prince and leader over the people of Israel."

Saul stood stunned, scarcely able to fathom what was happening to him. Samuel continued:

"Today, when you leave my house, you will meet two men beside Rachel's tomb outside Bethlehem. They will tell you, 'The donkeys have been found, and now your father is worried because you have been gone so long.' Going farther on to the oak of Tabor, three men will meet you there. One will be carrying three young kids, the second will carry three loaves of bread, and the third will carry a wineskin. They will greet you and offer you two of the loaves. Take them! After that, you will come to the city of Gibeah. There you will meet a band of priests. Ahead of them will go the sound of harps, hand drums, flutes, and lyres. As they walk, they will sing and dance in sacred ecstasy. Join them! You will be transformed into another man. On the seventh day, I will follow you there to offer peace offerings with you."

Every word and every sign flashed through Saul's soul like sparks of fire. When he and his servant left the house, God transformed his heart through that wondrous experience. Every single sign came true, just as the seer had foretold: beside Rachel's tomb, he heard that the donkeys had returned home; at the oak of Tabor, he received the two loaves of bread; and in Gibeah, he met the joyful procession of priests, entered into their sacred ecstasy, and walked along with them. People in Gibeah who had known Saul before asked one another:

"Whatever has happened to Saul, the son of Kish? Has he become a prophet too?"

After the sacrifice that Samuel came to offer with him, Saul returned home to his father. All the donkeys were safely there. And Saul kept tucked quietly inside his heart everything that Samuel had entrusted to him in secret.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Lively traveling rhythm:** Keep the tone natural and lightly humorous as master and servant search for the donkeys and rejoice at spotting droppings along the road.
- **Quiet under the stars:** Slow down and lower your voice while Saul lies awake on the flat roof until the midnight stars rise high.
- **Three signs at dawn:** Read Samuel's anointing at daybreak with solemn reverence, then mark a clear beat for each of the three signs—Rachel's tomb, the oak of Tabor, and the band of priests in Gibeah.

### Chapter 46: Saul Becomes King

Samuel, the seer and prophet, sent word summoning representatives from all twelve tribes to gather in a great assembly of the whole people. He proclaimed that the time had come to choose a king!

First, they cast lots to see from which of the twelve tribes the king would come. The lot fell upon the tribe of Benjamin. Next, they cast lots among the clans. The lot fell upon the clan of the Matrites. Within that clan, each man in turn had to draw a lot. The lot fell straight upon Saul, the son of Kish! His father had drawn the lot on his behalf, and now everyone went looking for Saul—for he had slipped away and hidden himself among the baggage! When no one could find him at first, Samuel said:

"Go and pull him out from among the baggage!"

And sure enough, right there he was! They brought Saul forward before Samuel, right into the midst of all the people—where his tall figure towered above everyone else. Samuel declared:

"Do you see the man whom God has chosen? Among all the people there is no one like him!"

The people cheered with a mighty shout:

"Long live the King!"

Many pressed forward and cried:

"Let us be your bodyguards! Let us be your servants!"

Samuel wrote down all the laws and rights of a king: how the people must obey the king, and what duties the king himself must faithfully uphold. He presented this scroll to the people and placed it inside the Tabernacle beside the Ten Commandments.

From then on, Saul and his warriors defended the people whenever foreign princes or kings marched in to trouble them. Time after time the king rode out to war, and every single time he defeated his enemies. Yet those victories kindled a great pride in King Saul's heart. He raised triumphal arches and enriched himself with the spoils of war. Little by little, he led the people into battle solely out of greed to plunder booty.

After these things, a sacred voice commanded Samuel to go and search for a worthier king. Samuel grew frightened, for Saul already held immense power over the people. Yet God stood firm:

"Fill your horn with oil! In Bethlehem you will find a son in the house of Jesse. He has been chosen as the future king. Anoint him with the holy oil as a sign!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **The assembly and a touch of humor:** Pick up the pace with each round of casting lots, then bring out a playful, amused note when tall Saul is pulled out from the baggage to cries of *"Long live the King!"*
- **A darker turning point:** Drop your tone as victory curdles into pride and triumphal arches, then deliver the command to anoint a new king in Bethlehem with steady resolve.

### Chapter 47: The Shepherd Boy David

Old Jesse had eight sons. The eldest was named Eliab, and the youngest was named David—and he was also the smallest of all eight brothers. The older brothers worked as merchants traveling far and wide across the country, and they said among themselves:

"Let our youngest brother stick to the boring work of watching the sheep."

And so David spent nearly the whole year out in the pastures around Bethlehem with the flock. Yet to him, that work was never boring—not for a single day! His eyes grew wide with wonder, delighting in the beauty of trees, wild herbs, and blossoms. He loved the date palms and olive trees that always cast cool, refreshing shade. He knew the songs of every bird, watched in enchantment as the clouds played with the sunlight, and at night gazed in awe at the host of stars. When no one was around to hear, he sang songs he had composed himself about the beauty of the world. Once his father gave him two sheep. David traded them for a small harp. From then on, he could pluck the strings and sing his songs to its music.

From time to time a desert wolf or a wild dog prowled past, waiting to snatch a lamb from the flock. Because of that, every single day the shepherd boy David practiced hurling stones with his leather sling for hours on end. For a target, he picked a large boulder out in the field and balanced a small white stone on top of it. Starting close up and then stepping farther and farther back, he aimed his sling to knock that white stone clean off. The surer his aim grew, the more often he tested himself with larger stones. Whenever his throwing arm grew tired, he picked up his harp again, plucked the strings, and sang along. That way, time never dragged heavily for him, even when his brothers left him all alone with the sheep for weeks at a stretch.

One day, as David sat near a cave playing his harp in the shade of the date palms, the whole flock out in the meadow suddenly scattered in panic around a great boulder. David sprang to his feet at once and saw a lion that had just seized a little lamb in its jaws. Without a trace of fear, gripping his heavy knotted staff, David charged straight at the lion just as it was about to trot off calmly with its prey. Putting all his strength into the blow, the young shepherd brought his knotted staff crashing down upon the lion's head. The fierce beast dropped the lamb from its jaws. Stunned, it staggered a few paces, and when a second blow struck straight across its skull, it collapsed to the ground, and David slew it. The little lamb was scarcely hurt and recovered in no time. And David carried the lion's pelt home as a gift for his father, Jesse.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Pastoral poetry:** Use a gentle, luminous voice as David marvels at wildflowers, clouds playing in the light, and trades two sheep for his little harp.
- **Rhythm of practice and sudden action:** Keep a crisp beat as he steps farther and farther back from the white target stone, then quicken sharply when the sheep scatter and David swings his knotted staff to fell the lion.

### Chapter 48: Who Will Be the New King?

Samuel could not let anyone suspect that he was out searching for a new king. He instructed his servants:

"Prepare a young bullock for me and come with me to Bethlehem to offer a sacrifice there. Invite Jesse and all his sons to the sacrificial feast!"

The servants arranged everything with care. Everyone who attended the ceremony received Samuel's blessing. Old Jesse arrived with only seven of his sons, for youngest David was out in the pastures watching the sheep. When Samuel saw the eldest brother, tall and broad-shouldered Eliab, he thought to himself, *"Surely this must be the one."* Yet when Samuel blessed him, God gave no sign that he was to be king—nor for any of the other six brothers. Samuel asked Jesse:

"Are these all the sons you have?"

The father replied:

"The youngest is out in the fields tending the sheep."

Samuel said:

"Send for him and bring him here. He must take part in the sacrifice too."

Samuel thought silently to himself, *"It must certainly be the shepherd boy. What does he look like, I wonder? Could he be even taller than these brothers of his?"*

When David walked in, Samuel saw that he was the smallest of them all. Tucked into his belt was his leather sling, and cradled in his left arm was his harp. His hair gleamed with a reddish-gold sheen, and his eyes shone clear and keen, as though they had gathered deep blue skies into them during his days as a shepherd. As the small yet sturdy boy stood before him, the seer beheld with the eyes of his spirit how vast and radiant David's soul was. An inner voice spoke to him: *"It is he. Anoint him!"*

Samuel did as he was bidden right there in front of David's brothers, and a sacred majesty descended upon the boy and remained with him forever. Even so, the young lad had not the slightest inkling of what lay ahead for him. Nor did Samuel himself know when or how David would ascend the throne, so he returned home to his town of Ramah.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From mistaken guess to wonder:** Sound confident as Samuel looks at eldest Eliab, grow puzzled through all seven brothers, and soften in wonder when the smallest son arrives carrying his leather sling and harp.
- **Eyes full of blue sky:** Slow down warmly on the description of David's clear eyes and the quiet inner voice at the moment of anointing.

### Chapter 49: David and Saul

Because of King Saul's pride and arrogance, sacred grace drifted farther and farther away from him. Every now and again, a heavy gloom swept over his soul, until people whispered that an evil spirit was tormenting the King! One of his counselors advised him:

"Saul, Your Majesty ought to soothe your spirit with music. Somewhere in this land there must be a harp player who can make music for you."

One of Saul's courtiers, who hailed from Bethlehem, knew old Jesse and his sons well and had heard David play. He spoke to the King:

"Your Majesty, I know the sons of Jesse in Bethlehem. The youngest, named David, is deeply skilled in the art of playing stringed instruments. He is still young and tends the sheep. He is courageous and very handsome to look upon."

Hearing this, the king sent that very courtier on his way:

"Go as my messenger to the house of Jesse and bring the boy with the harp back to me!"

Old Jesse felt deeply honored when the royal messenger arrived with that request. He sent one of his sons to guide the messenger out to the pastures to fetch David. Their path led over a hill to the cool, shady cave where David loved to sit. Lush date palms grew thick around the cave, casting welcome shade and offering shelter for the flock whenever wind and rain blew in. David had no idea the two men were approaching, for his whole heart and mind were wrapped up in the music of his harp. The sheep rested all around him in the cool shadow, and a little lamb lay fast asleep right across his lap. The messenger thought to himself, *"If David can bring the King the same peace he weaves right here, the King's soul will surely be healed!"*

Suddenly a sheep let out a bleat, and only then did David notice them. The messenger delivered the king's summons, and David's brother confirmed their father's word that he would watch the flock in David's place. David was dismayed at the news. He asked:

"How long will this service to the King last? Will I be allowed to come back to my sheep from time to time?"

The messenger answered:

"That is something you will have to ask the King yourself!"

So David handed his shepherd's staff to his brother as his father had instructed and set out with the messenger. They had gone only a short distance when David darted back to the cave to fetch his leather sling, which he had hung on a bush. He tucked the sling into his belt and asked the messenger:

"At the royal court, will I be allowed to use this sling?"

The messenger burst out laughing:

"Out in the pastures you certainly may! But you know, the King's warriors shoot bows and arrows and hurl javelins—they don't bother with ordinary pebbles!"

And so David arrived at the royal court.

Word was brought to Saul that the harp player had arrived. At that very moment, the king was plunged into one of his darkest moods. He had just hurled a wine pitcher straight at a servant's head as the man served him wine. Even so, Saul ordered the young boy brought into his presence. The moment Saul saw handsome young David looking him straight in the eye, so open and utterly unafraid, Saul dropped his gaze to the floor and muttered:

"Play a tune!"

David sat down upon a brightly patterned woven rug. A few deep notes rang out, and then lighter, higher notes floated gently between them, like a breeze rustling through the fronds of date palms. All at once, David felt as though he were sitting among his flock once more. He closed his eyes and sang softly without words, weaving his voice into the sound of the strings.

Saul lifted his eyes and watched David's slender fingers glide lightly over the harp strings. The king looked upon the young shepherd with his sun-bronzed skin and reddish-gold hair—and suddenly the King's heart opened! He felt a peace his soul had not known for a very long time. The attendants stared at one another in astonishment: the King was smiling!

From that moment on, the king took David warmly into his heart. When David stopped playing, looked around questioningly, and then raised his eyes straight to Saul, the king did not turn his face away. He nodded in gratitude and asked:

"Why do you wear a sling tucked into your belt?"

David answered:

"Out in the pastures, many times I had to drive off wild dogs, once I faced a wolf, and once even a lion! That is where I learned to aim true and hit whatever mark I choose!"

Saul said:

"If you love a fight so much, you may become my armor-bearer whenever I ride into battle."

Thus Saul befriended the young harpist, and every time the king marched out to war, David carried his shield and spear behind him.

From time to time, David grew homesick. He longed to see Bethlehem once more and be with his sheep. He also felt that his harp needed a fresh breeze blowing across its strings—the breeze of the palm groves and olive orchards. One day, seeing Saul in high spirits, David asked:

"Your Majesty, might I go home to Bethlehem now and then to spend a little time with my sheep?"

Saul gladly replied:

"Yes, you may—only do not stay away too long, for I cannot bear to be without your music and your songs."

David was overjoyed to stroke the sheep and little lambs again, and to play his harp and sing near the shady cave. Yet every single time, after seven days at most, he returned to serve as Saul's singer and attendant.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Healing sound:** Speak softly and let your voice linger gently as David sits on the brightly woven rug and plucks music like wind whispering through palm leaves, then softly emphasize the line of wonder: *"The King was smiling!"*
- **Artless country spirit:** Sound slightly anxious when David fears leaving his sheep, then bright and eager as he dashes back for the sling hanging on the bush and asks to visit the breeze of palm and olive trees.

### Chapter 50: David and Goliath

The neighboring Philistines marched forth, threatening to invade Israel and sounding their war trumpets loud. King Saul had to muster the army to defend the land. Three of David's older brothers had joined the soldiers marching against the Philistines. One day, while David was at home watching the sheep, his father Jesse rode out on a donkey to the pasture and told him:

"David, my son, word has just reached me that the Philistines have crossed into our land. Saul has had to lead the army out to the battlefield. Hurry and set out at once! Take bread, fresh curds of cheese, and roasted grain. Carry them to your brothers at the front so they do not go hungry."

David left the flock in the care of another shepherd. His father loaded the provisions onto him, and David set out for the Valley of Terebinthia, where Saul and his army were encamped. Drawing near the camp, he heard a clamor of shouts, battle cries, and the clanging of weapons. Saul's soldiers had just drawn up in battle array to meet the advancing Philistine host. David set down his bundle of supplies, went searching through the ranks for his brothers, and greeted them.

While he was still talking with his brothers, out from the Philistine ranks strode a warrior of monstrous size. It was the giant Goliath! His mighty body was clad in a coat of scale armor, iron greaves strapped around his legs, and an iron helmet upon his head. Goliath roared across the valley to the Israelites:

"You cursed cowards! Pitiful slaves of Saul, you filthy Israelites! Send out your strongest champion to fight me man to man—before we hack the lot of you to pieces and dance upon your bones!"

Hearing those bitter insults and beholding the iron-clad giant standing before the enemy host, Saul's soldiers lost all courage and fell into panic. Goliath took a few heavy strides closer, brandishing a spear as thick and long as a weaver's beam. Many of Saul's men shrank back in terror.

David sprang forward right in front of the King and cried out:

"How can that towering mountain of flesh and bone dare to mock Israel—the people of God—and go unpunished? If none of your champions will step out to fight him, I will face him myself! Whenever a lion or a bear sprang upon my flock, I struck it down, and the Lord saved me from the lion's claws! Let me go out and fight that Philistine!"

David spoke as though a blazing fire of courage burned within him. Saul felt so deeply heartened by the boy's words that he said:

"Go then, and fight him. May the Lord be with you!"

With that, the king dressed David in his own armor. He placed a bronze helmet on his head and strapped a sword around his waist. Yet David had never worn armor in his life, and he could scarcely move in it. Everything hung far too loose and heavy on him. He told Saul:

"This will never do. I must be able to move freely and lightly, like a shepherd."

He stripped off all the armor, took up his shepherd's staff and his leather sling, picked up five smooth pebbles and slipped them into his cloth pouch, and walked straight out to meet the Philistine.

Goliath stood watching the reddish-gold-haired boy walk toward him. He gave a mocking sneer and bellowed:

"Am I a dog, that you come at me with a stick? You cursed spawn of Jehovah! Come here! I will feed your flesh to the birds of the air and the desert foxes!"

Hearing the giant blaspheme the holy name of the Lord like that, David called out boldly to Goliath:

"You come against me with sword and javelin. I come against you in the name of God, the One whom you mock. What you mean to do to me will happen to you instead!"

Goliath let out a savage roar of attack and charged forward with giant strides. David let his shepherd's staff drop to the ground, reached into his pouch for a solid stone, and fitted it into his sling. With one mighty whirl of his arm, he sent the stone flying straight through the air—and it struck Goliath square on the forehead, right between his eyes! For a split second, the giant seemed to tower even taller than before. Then he let his spear fall, and he crashed face-first onto the earth! David sprinted forward, drew the giant's own sword from its sheath, and cut off Goliath's head!

Everything happened so swiftly that soldiers on both battle lines stood frozen, speechless, holding their breath as they watched. Then all at once, the army of Israel erupted in a thunderous roar of joy! As for the Philistines, seeing their champion Goliath lying headless in the dust, they broke ranks and fled in every direction. Israel's cheers of triumph turned instantly into a fierce battle cry. They chased after the Philistines, shattering every enemy band they overtook, and won a complete victory.

David grasped Goliath's head and sword and brought both before King Saul. When he reached the King, Prince Jonathan—the king's own son—was overflowing with excitement and admiration for David. He was just David's age and had watched him with boundless awe throughout the battle. As David laid Goliath's sword and head at Saul's feet, Jonathan threw his arms around him and said:

"Oh, David, let me be your friend! You are as dear to me as my own life!"

Jonathan draped his own royal cloak over David's shoulders and fastened it tight. Wishing to seal a lifelong bond of friendship, he gave David his sword, his bow and arrows, and his belt as well. King Saul warmly praised David's heroic deed and said:

"From this day on, you must never return to the sheep again, but stay always at my side. Whenever the Philistines march against us again, you will help me drive them back!"

After that victory, Saul's army marched back to Jerusalem. Goliath's head and sword were borne at the front of the victory procession. Women came out to greet them, clashing cymbals, dancing, and singing aloud:

*"Saul has slain his thousands,*
*And David his tens of thousands!"*

That made Saul burn with fury, for he saw David set above himself, and he grew jealous watching the people honor David higher than the King. Hoping to bind the young hero tightly to his royal house, Saul gave his daughter, Princess Michal, to David in marriage.

One day, an evil spirit seized hold of Saul and made him want to kill David. Through the halls of the palace, King Saul raged like a madman, slashing his spear wildly through the air and shouting strange names. David was summoned to play the harp and soothe the king. As he began to play, the raging monarch did indeed grow calmer—yet the spear never once left his hand. Right in the middle of David's playing, Saul hurled his spear straight at him! At the very last split second, David dodged aside—otherwise the iron point would have pierced him clean through. The spear plunged deep into the wall and stuck fast there. David fled straight back to his own house.

When night fell, Saul sent soldiers to David's house. Their orders were to guard the doorway. As soon as David stepped outside the next morning, they were to bind him and haul him back before Saul. The dark demon in the king's mind whispered that David had to die.

That night, while everyone else was fast asleep, Michal heard faint sounds outside. She quietly parted the window curtain, peered out, and spotted her father's armed guards waiting in the deep shadows of the night. She shook David awake and whispered:

"Wake up quickly and run! My father's armed guards are lying in ambush outside the house! Tomorrow morning they mean to seize you and put you to death! At the back of the house, you can tie a rope and climb down through the window. Flee somewhere safe!"

David knew this was wise and prudent counsel. Without letting anyone notice, he slipped away into the night and went straight to Samuel to ask what he should do next.

The next morning, Saul's soldiers waited in vain outside the door. David never came out. When at last they broke down the door and burst inside, the man they sought was nowhere to be found.

Saul's son Jonathan learned that his father was bent on killing David. He met David in secret and said:

"Oh, David, I have sworn with you a lifelong covenant of brotherly friendship. I warn you now: stay far away from my father. He will never rest until the demon inside his heart sees your blood spilled!"

The two friends embraced, kissed one another farewell, and wept together to see such a dark shadow fall over their friendship.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Climax in the Valley of Terebinthia:** Drop into a proud, growling voice for Goliath's challenge, then brighten to a clear, ringing tone as David casts off the heavy armor and answers him before battle.
- **The moment of the pebble:** Read briskly and tautly as the stone flies straight to the center of the giant's forehead, then pause a single beat as Goliath *"seemed to tower even taller than before"* before crashing down.
- **Shift from glory to shadow:** Keep the clashing cymbals joyful on the march into Jerusalem, then drop low and tense for the spear thudding into the wall, Michal's urgent whisper, and Jonathan's tearful farewell.

### Chapter 51: In the Cave

Once, while David was hiding inside a cave near Engedi with a band of armed followers who shielded him from King Saul, the king happened to lead his guards right past that very spot.

Catching sight of the cave mouth, Saul said to his soldiers:

"There is a cave over there. I want to go inside and rest for a little while. Halt here, take your rest, and wait for me."

David's companions saw him coming, and one of them whispered the news to David:

"Look, David! Your mortal enemy is walking straight into our hiding place completely unarmed! The Lord is delivering him right into your hands. You can kill him!"

David answered:

"I will never lift my hand against the Lord's anointed! Not one of you may harm a single hair upon his head!"

When Saul had finished resting in the shadows of the cave and made ready to step outside, David stepped quietly up from behind and stood right beside him with a drawn sword in his hand. He bowed low to the ground before Saul and said:

"My Lord King, why do you listen to those who say that David seeks to destroy you? Today the Lord led you straight before the point of my sword, yet I will never lay a hand upon the Lord's anointed!"

Saul turned pale as chalk in terror, his whole body trembling. He could not stir a single step from the spot. Once again David bowed low to the ground before the king.

At last, Saul found his voice:

"David, my son! You are more righteous than I am. You have treated me with goodness. I know full well that one day you will rule as King over the land of Israel. Swear to me now that after I am gone, you will not wipe out my descendants or blot out my name!"

David swore to keep that promise faithfully. Saul burst into tears, and David folded him in an embrace as though he were holding Jonathan, his dearest friend. At that moment the evil spirit left Saul, and the king walked back out to his soldiers—who had no idea at all of everything that had just happened inside.

Yet David's companions had witnessed the entire meeting with the king. They murmured softly among themselves:

"Could a new law of mercy be entering the world with David? Must we now forgive those who do evil? Do good to those who persecute us? Even love our enemies?"

When David walked back to his men at the far end of the cave, every one of them had fallen silent. He told them:

"Come, let us set out for Ramah."

Along the road, David received word that Samuel, the seer and prophet, had passed away on the very day he and Saul had met inside the cave and found peace and reconciliation together.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Darkness of the cave:** Keep your voice low and hushed as the follower whispers the news, then speak David's refusal in a deep, steady tone.
- **From fear to forgiveness:** Pace the dialogue between David and Saul slowly; let warmth fill your voice when Saul breaks into tears.
- **The three final questions:** Pause cleanly between each whispered question to let the new spirit of mercy linger in the air.

### Chapter 52: The Death of Saul and Jonathan

Not long afterward, the Philistines resolved to march against Israel and avenge the death of Goliath! King Saul was forced to lead his army and his sons into battle among the mountains of Gilboa. At that same time, David was away on a march to drive back a raid by the Amalekites.

In the thick of a fierce battle, the Philistines pressed their fiercest attack straight against King Saul and his sons. The king was struck by an arrow and gravely wounded, while Jonathan and his brothers, Saul's sons, were all slain.

Saul called to his armor-bearer standing beside him:

"Draw your sword and run me through, lest these Philistines make sport of me!"

The armor-bearer could not bring himself to strike the king. So Saul fell upon his own sword and ended his life himself. His armor-bearer died right there beside him as well. When the men of Israel saw their king and his princes lying dead upon the battlefield, they turned and fled, and the Philistines carried the day.

Meanwhile, in a distant region, David had driven back the Amalekites in triumph. A soldier who had escaped from the northern battle found David and brought him news of the defeat and of King Saul's death. He presented David with the king's golden headband and golden armlet, which he had managed to rescue from the field.

When David heard that Saul's sons—and among them his beloved Jonathan—had also fallen in battle, grief pierced him so deeply that he tore his garments. He wept for King Saul, for the king's sons, and for all the warriors who had fallen. Then David lifted up his lament:

> *"O unhappy Israel, how your heroes have fallen!*
> *Saul and Jonathan, beloved and dear,*
> *In life and in death never parted,*
> *Have fallen upon the field of battle.*
> *Weep, O daughters of Israel!*
> *For you, my brother Jonathan,*
> *My heart aches with sorrow without end. How kind you ever were to me!*
> *Your tender love was more precious to me*
> *Than the love of a woman.*
> *O wretched Israel, your heroes are no more!"*

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Solemn pace for the battle:** Read the fight on Mount Gilboa at a brisk, steady, serious clip without dwelling on bloodshed.
- **Voice for the lament:** Pause briefly before entering the verse; read slowly, letting deep sorrow and David's lifelong devotion to Jonathan resonate in every line.

### Chapter 53: David Becomes King

After Saul's death, all the people raised David to the throne as king. He moved to the city of Jerusalem and chose it to be the capital of his kingdom. During the early years of his reign, time and again the king still had to march out to war against the Philistines and other tribes. Every single time, he won complete victory.

Right beside the city of Jerusalem, upon Mount Zion, David commanded a precious tent to be pitched for the Ark of the Covenant and the sacred vessels, built to the exact pattern and measurements of the Tabernacle of old. The priests solemnly consecrated that new sacred tent, and thousands of people gathered for the great feast day.

Songs rang out resoundingly, instruments swelled in rich harmony, and clouds of sacrificial incense rose skyward for days on end together with the prayers of the whole people lifted toward heaven. It was King David himself who had composed those hymns of praise and supplication. Back when he was a lonely shepherd boy, he had sung his very first songs to his harp in Bethlehem.

Now, in the heart of Jerusalem, a choir of a thousand voices lifted up their song in mighty, ringing harmony:

> *"Give thanks unto the Lord,*
> *Call upon His Name,*
> *Proclaim to all the nations the wondrous deeds He has done!*
> *Sing unto Him, make music for Him,*
> *Tell of all His marvels.*
> *The earth stands firm and fast,*
> *It can never be shaken.*
> *The great ocean roars and thunders—*
> *How vast and boundless it stretches!*
> *The meadows leap with joy*
> *In the splendor of bright blossoms everywhere!*
> *The trees in the deep forest shout for gladness!*
> *Give thanks unto the Lord,*
> *For He is gentle and kind,*
> *And His mercy endures forever!"*

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Bright, jubilant tone:** Shift cleanly into the festive, solemn yet joyous spirit of the great feast upon Mount Zion.
- **Resounding hymn cadence:** Mark a crisp beat for each pair of nature images—the firm earth, the roaring ocean, the flowering meadows, and the singing forest trees—to evoke the sound of a thousand-voice choir.

### Chapter 54: The Great Assembly

David reigned for forty years. His power and wealth grew greater and greater in Jerusalem. King Hiram of Tyre sent David master craftsmen and skilled carpenters to build him a magnificent royal palace.

Then the king summoned all the princes of Israel, the leaders of the twelve tribes, the commanders of the army, and the highest counselors of the realm.

When everyone had assembled in full number, they murmured eagerly to one another:

"What can David wish to speak to all of us about? Or does the King face some new trouble?"

They guessed one thing after another. Then a bronze horn rang out, calling the assembly to silence.

King David rose in the midst of the great assembly. He spoke to them all:

"Hear me, my brothers, hear me, my people! For a long time I have prepared to turn the sacred tent on Mount Zion into a steadfast Temple. That Temple must stand right here in Jerusalem. Yet God spoke to me and said, 'You may not build a house for My name, because you have shed too much blood in war. Your son Solomon shall do this. He shall build the Temple and reign as king when your days upon this earth draw to a close!' That is what I heard from God, and today I pass His word on to you."

All the princes, commanders, and high officials agreed with one heart and acknowledged Solomon as David's rightful heir.

That very night, an angel of the Lord appeared to Solomon and said:

"Solomon, you may ask God for one gift. What do you wish Him to grant you?"

Solomon replied:

"May the Lord grant me wisdom and an understanding heart, so that I may guide this great people with justice!"

And everything came to pass just so: after his father King David passed away, Solomon, seated upon the throne as the new king, commanded the Temple to be built in Jerusalem. It was told that one day, inside that very Temple, the Messiah—the Redeemer who was to come—would teach His word. He would help humankind overcome evil and walk ever more steadfastly along the path of goodness.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Shifting from buzz to solemnity:** Keep a curious, murmuring hum for the nobles' guesses, stop cleanly as the bronze horn sounds, then deliver David's declaration with warm strength.
- **Solomon's humble voice:** Soften into sincere humility when Solomon asks for wisdom; slow down gently at the end as the story looks ahead toward the coming Messiah.

### Chapter 55: David's Dream

On his very last day upon the earth, King David dreamed a wondrous dream. He saw his two sons, Solomon and Nathan, lying asleep upon the ground out in the fields around Bethlehem.

From the body of each son, a tree sprang upward toward the high heavens. Each time a new branch sprouted forth, a name was spoken aloud. On Nathan's tree, the names began with Matthata, Menna, Melea; while on Solomon's tree came Rehabeam, Abia, Asaph. The higher the two trees climbed into the sky, the more names rang out.

When their topmost crowns gently touched the rim of heaven, a star blazed forth, dazzling bright as the sun! In that radiance, the two trees bowed toward one another. Their boughs twined together and grew into a single World Tree. Choirs of angels burst into jubilant song. Down from the stars, a shower of light rained upon the interwoven crown, and countless little flames came to rest upon its leaves and branches.

When King David awoke from that dream, he pondered deeply over what he had seen. A deep peace spread throughout his soul—just like the peaceful calm of long ago when he played his harp in Bethlehem. David said quietly within himself, *"Now I can die in peace. My children, and their children's children in days to come, will one day look up and behold the light of the Messiah!"*

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Gentle vision atmosphere:** Keep an unhurried pace that brightens gradually as the two trees climb toward heaven; pronounce each branching name clearly: *Matthata, Menna, Melea* and *Rehabeam, Abia, Asaph*.
- **Moment of the World Tree:** Let your voice shine when the star blazes like the sun, then settle into a soft sigh of peace for King David's final words.

### Chapter 56: The Psalms of David

Ancient tradition attributes one hundred and fifty songs and Psalms to David. Through him, song and music upon the earth became living and full of fresh vitality. Here is a passage from Psalm 104:

> *Praise the Lord, O my soul,*
> *For He wears a mantle of light.*
> *He stretches out the heavens like a tent curtain.*
> *He takes the clouds as His royal chariot,*
> *Drawn by angels gliding upon the wind.*
> *Blazing flames of fire are His servants,*
> *And He has set the earth firmly*
> *upon a steadfast foundation above the deep.*
> *Once the Great Flood covered the globe like a cloak,*
> *and the waters stood higher than the tallest mountains.*
> *The waters fled back at the sound of His rolling thunder,*
> *hills and mountains rose high into the air,*
> *and valleys and deep gorges sank down low.*
> *Then green grass sprouted there for the cattle to graze.*
> *And in the golden ears of grain, the kernels of bread ripened full.*
> *Sing out, all you birds! Leap and frolic, you young lambs!*
> *Wheel in great circles, you eagles, high up in the open sky!*
> *He made the moon to mark the four seasons.*
> *The sun knows well when to rise and when to set.*
> *Night after night, the beasts of the deep forest prowl abroad,*
> *and the lion roars in search of prey.*
> *Yet when the sun rises once more,*
> *they slink back and curl up inside their dens.*
> *Standing upright, humankind steps forth to its daily work.*
> *O Lord, how vast and countless are Your works!*
> *In wisdom You have ordered them all,*
> *And the whole earth overflows with Your loving-kindness!*

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Chanted close to Book II:** Read every line of Psalm 104 clearly with gentle pauses at each line's end so sky, earth, and living creatures shine vividly.
- **Bright resonance at the close:** Let your voice sparkle when calling the birds to sing, lambs to leap, and eagles to soar; close with solemn warmth on the final three lines.
