# Book IV: The Two Christmas Children and Dawn on the Jordan
*(Full Text Edition — Ages 9–10)*

---

## Part I: The Royal Child and the Shepherd Child

### Chapter 1: Two Christmas Stories

Winter night. The sky is clear and cold.

High above, the stars appear one by one, until the whole dome of heaven is sparkling.

Down below, in small houses, people light their fires. Woodsmoke rises and mingles with the chilly air. Children sit close by the hearth. Grown-ups tidy up and talk together about small everyday things. Outside, the silent road stretches away toward the horizon.

People are waiting for a night.

That night returns every year, bringing two stories so very old that each time they are told, they seem new again.

Outside the town, on a hillside, burns a small fire.

Shepherds sit around that fire. They tuck their legs up close, hands clasping their staffs, rough cloaks wrapped around their shoulders. The sheep lie huddled together inside a brushwood fold, their breath rising in thin wisps of mist. No one speaks. There is only the crackle of the fire, and the faint rustle of a young lamb stirring.

As the night deepens, the fire burns lower. Still the shepherds stay awake, their eyes peering into the dark to guard the flock until morning.

Very far away, toward the rising sun, a new star has just appeared.

It is not in any familiar place. It stands motionless in one quarter of the sky, bright and clear, and will not leave.

In a house in that distant land, someone sits up. A door opens. Several people step out one by one into the courtyard and look up at the sky. They stand there a very long time, without saying a word to one another. Then one turns back inside, lights more lamps, and wakes those who are still sleeping.

In these two stories there are two lights: a small fire out in the pasture, and a star in a distant land.

No one on this side knows anything about the other. The shepherds know only their flock. The star-watchers know only that star.

Out in the field, the little fire slowly burns low, while high above, the star stands still in one place.

And so people tell those two stories again: one story beginning beside a small fire, and one story beginning beneath a star.

#### Storytelling Notes
- This chapter is very short; tell it like a prelude—slowly, in a low voice, keeping plenty of stillness. Tell the passage about the fire and the sheep evenly and warmly, like quiet breathing; tell the passage about the star a little slower and slightly higher. Pause completely for a beat after *"In these two stories there are two lights..."*, then speak the last two sentences very lightly—without explaining, without emphasizing.

### Chapter 2: The Royal Child

Not far from Jerusalem, there is a little town nestled on gentle rolling hills. People call that place Bethlehem.

In Bethlehem lived a family that had dwelt there for many generations. Inside their house, the elders still told one another something very old indeed: that their line had once held kings. Their distant forefather was King David, and one branch of that family came down through King Solomon. For a very long time, no one had worn a crown. Yet the story of the kings remained, told over and over beside the hearth-fire, from one generation to the next.

In that family there was a mother named Mary, and a father named Joseph.

Then a baby boy was born, right in that very house, in Bethlehem.

People said this little boy was different from other children. He did not speak lofty words. Yet the way he looked at things was like no one else.

When he was still very small, he only needed to look at a face once to remember it. When grown-ups talked, he sat still and listened, and he understood things people thought he did not yet understand. Walking along a road, he noticed right away how today differed from yesterday: a tree that had just shed its leaves, a cloud changing color, a stranger who had just passed by. When someone showed him how to do a task—tie a cord, fold a piece of cloth—he watched once and could do it himself.

Yet he was also just a child. He ate, he slept, he ran out into the sunshine. Only, in his eyes there was a quiet wakefulness, as though he had just returned from somewhere very far away.

Then one day, in a far-off land toward the rising sun, several people set out on a journey.

They were wise men. Night after night, they sat out in their courtyard, gazing at the heavens and keeping the stars in memory. They knew the sky by heart just as a shepherd knows the path of every sheep.

One night, a strange star appeared in the sky.

That star was not in any familiar place. It stood still in one quarter of the sky, bright and clear, and refused to leave. The wise men looked at one another for a long time, and together they understood one thing: the time had come to set out.

They packed their belongings. They chose three precious gifts—gold, frankincense, and myrrh—and wrapped them carefully. They led out their camels, brought food for the road, and followed the star.

By night they walked watching the star; by day they rested in the shade of trees. Through many villages, across many fields, over many hills and mountains, they pressed onward toward a great city on a hill: Jerusalem.

Entering the city, they asked:

"Where is the newborn king?"

Hearing that question, the people of the city grew stirred and restless. Word passed from house to house until it reached the ears of the man sitting upon the throne.

That king's name was Herod. When he heard that a new king had just been born, his heart sank. He sat upon his throne, and inside his belly grew a dark shadow. He was afraid. Someone who has grown too used to power is always afraid of losing it.

But Joseph and Mother Mary in Bethlehem knew nothing of that fear. Nor did anyone in the little house know that far away, strangers were searching for their child.

That night, the star stopped.

The wise men followed it until it stood motionless above one roof. They paused at the door. They stepped inside.

Within the house were a young mother and a little infant.

They opened the wrapped gifts they had carried along the whole great road, laid them down before the mother, and bowed down very low.

The house fell silent. Outside, above the roof, the star stayed still in one place.

That night passed.

Then one night, as Joseph lay sleeping, in a dream he heard something that woke him in the middle of the night.

He sat up in the dark, silent for a very long time.

Then he woke Mother Mary.

They gathered very few things. An animal to carry their bundle. The mother held the child against her heart. And before daybreak, they had already set out—journeying far away from Bethlehem, toward the dry lands of Egypt.

The road was long, the sand blowing. Darkness covered the hills, while overhead, the stars still journeyed with them.

They traveled for many days. Many weeks. Then many months.

In Bethlehem, no one knew where they had gone.

Time passed. The days and months softened old fears. When word came that the danger was over, the little family made ready to return.

They traveled northward. Yet they did not go back to Bethlehem.

They chose another little town, farther north, where there were craftsmen's workshops and roads of red earth. That town's name was Nazareth.

That afternoon, the sun went down behind the hills. A small family walked along the road leading into the town. The father led the pack animal. The mother held the child in her arms. They walked slowly, for the way had been long and the day was turning to evening.

No one in the little company knew that this road was bringing them close to another roof.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell the passage describing the family and the child's character slowly, making each detail a small clear picture—do not say *"he was very clever"*; let the children see it for themselves. When reaching Herod, drop your voice low and slow; keep the focus on his fear of losing his throne, without telling it harshly. Tell the departure by night almost in a whisper. Let the final sentence fall very softly, then rest in silence—that is where the children bridge into the next chapter themselves.

### Chapter 3: The Shepherd Child

In those same days, in a small town to the north called Nazareth, there lived another family.

This family also remembered that they belonged to King David's line, though through another son of the king, named Nathan. For a long, long time no one in that branch had been a king. Those in the household lived by the work of their hands.

The father's name was Joseph. The mother's name was Mary.

Then one day, an imperial herald passed through the towns and read out a decree: everyone must return to the home town of their ancestors to have their names written in the register.

For this family, that ancestral place was Bethlehem—several days' journey from Nazareth.

Mary was expecting a child, and the time of birth was already drawing near. Yet the decree spared no one. So the two packed their belongings, led out a pack animal to carry their bundles, and set forth.

The road was long. One hill followed another, and then another. Stones beneath their feet, dust upon their shoulders. Some stretches of road ran straight ahead with no one in sight for miles. Other stretches wound along rocky ledges where they had to go step by careful step.

At noon they rested under the shade of trees. At night they slept in wayside houses, or lay beside a grassy bank under a thin cloth, listening to the wind.

At last, Bethlehem came into view before them, resting upon its gentle rolling hills.

But the town was already crowded to the brim.

Everyone had come back here to be registered. Everyone needed a place to sleep. From one house to the next, Joseph knocked at doors and asked. People shook their heads. Night was closing in darker and darker.

At last, someone pointed them toward a shelter round the back, where animals were kept overnight. It was not a house. There was only a roof, rough stone walls, a little straw, and a wooden trough for dry fodder.

They stepped inside and pulled the door shut behind them.

That night, the child was born.

The mother took a cloth, wrapped her baby tenderly, and laid him down—inside the manger, the trough where people poured hay for the animals to eat.

The animals in the stable stood still. Not one made a sound. It was as if the whole stable were holding its breath, so as not to make a noise around the sleeping child.

Outside the town, out in the fields, shepherds were also keeping watch that night—around their small fire, amid their huddled flock.

Then the sky above the field suddenly shone bright.

A light not of fire, nor of stars. That light folded round the whole pasture, and inside that light spoke a voice. The shepherds heard tidings: tonight, in the town, a little child had just been born.

They stood up. They left the sheep inside the brushwood fold and walked toward the town.

They found the shelter. The doorway was low. They had to bow their heads to step inside.

Inside were a young mother, a father, and a newborn child lying in a manger.

The shepherds stood still a moment at the door. They brought nothing with them, save their hands and their hearts. Then they sat down around the manger, just as they used to sit around their fire out in the fields. The animals in the stable shifted gently aside to make room for the strangers.

No one spoke. Only the soft rustle of a sheep stirring upon the straw.

A short time afterward, according to custom, the family carried the child up to Jerusalem to present him in the Temple.

That day, in the Temple, there was an old man named Simeon. He had waited for this for a very long time—so long that many people had forgotten he was still waiting. When the mother entered carrying the infant, the old man came forward, asked to hold the child in his arms, and spoke words of thanksgiving. The mother listened without understanding it all.

There was also an elderly woman named Anna, of very great age, who sat day after day inside the Temple. She came up beside them and also spoke words of thanksgiving.

The mother understood no more than before. Yet she kept all those things hidden in her heart.

Then the family went back to Nazareth.

This time the journey was lighter, because the mother held her child in her arms.

In the afternoon, as they came into the town, they walked past the low rooftops and the workshops fragrant with fresh wood. People in the neighborhood looked up to see them pass.

That night, in Nazareth, an oil lamp shone inside a house.

Outside, night had fallen. Upon the hills round the town, other shepherds were guarding their flocks beside their own small fires.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell the journey evenly, like the steady rhythm of footsteps, without stressing hardship. When reaching *"That night, the child was born"*, drop your voice very low and slow right down—this is a place where silence matters more than words. Tell the coming of the shepherds warmly and unhurriedly, in contrast to the swifter pace of Chapter 2. Tell the Temple scene lightly, as though passing swiftly by, and let the ending rest very gently without emphasis.

### Chapter 4: Conversations of the Two Marys

In Nazareth, those two families lived not far apart.

In the neighborhood, people often mistook the two families for one another. For both fathers were named Joseph. Both mothers were named Mary. And both sons were named Jesus.

Once the vegetable seller at the head of the market called out loudly:

"Mistress Mary, the greens are here!"

Both Marys turned round together and smiled together. The vegetable seller grew so embarrassed he could only divide the greens between them both.

Yet nobody mixed them up for long. For the two little boys were far too different.

The Royal Child grew up like this: he remembered the faces of people he had met only once. He remembered where each lane led, which house had a barking dog, which village the potter came from. Once, standing to watch someone build a table, he looked on for a very long time, and then asked:

"Why do people drive the nails in at a slant, sir?"

Grown-ups often had to think for a while before they could answer. Then he asked again. And asked again after that, until he understood it through and through.

The Shepherd Child grew up quite differently. He did not remember many things like that. Yet he noticed what others missed: whether Mother looked tired today, whether the neighbor's dog had been fed yet, what the child next door was crying over. Once he saved his own piece of bread for a little child he did not know at all, and sat watching the child eat it, looking as happy as if he himself had just eaten.

The two boys grew up beside each other, like two brothers.

In the afternoons, while the boys played in the yard, the two Marys sat together on the doorstep. Neither woman's hands were idle, yet their talk never ran out.

Mary of the royal line said:

"My boy remembers everything. Once he repeated to me what a stranger passing our house had said, word for word, months afterward. Sometimes I ask myself if there is any empty corner left inside his head."

Mary of the shepherd line laughed softly:

"And my boy can't hold things like that in his head at all. Yet every morning he sets aside the first cup of water for the little lamb. And any day someone in the house is sad, he knows right away, even when nobody says a single word to him."

The two mothers looked at one another for a moment, and then both fell silent.

Both realized one thing: these two children held one another very dear. When they were side by side, the one boy seemed to understand what the other wanted to say before he could say it. And the other boy was always the first to notice whenever his friend was unhappy.

Another afternoon, the two boys were playing in the courtyard.

The Royal Child was busy with a small wooden cart. Its wheel would not turn. He bent over it, spun the wheel, studied it closely, took it apart, and fitted it back together.

Meanwhile, the Shepherd Child was standing beneath the eaves, and heard a very tiny sound.

Up under the eaves, a bird's nest had fallen down. Upon the stone paving below lay a baby bird, its wings still damp, shivering in little waves.

The boy bent down, cupped the little bird in both hands, and looked up at the nest high above.

Too high. Out of reach.

The Royal Child set down the cart, walked over, looked for a moment, and said:

"Wait."

He ran into the house and came back out with a low stool. He placed the stool tight against the wall, tried standing on it, reached up—still a span short.

He thought a moment, then moved the stool to one side, choosing a spot on the wall where a stone jutted out. He set the stool right beneath that stone and said:

"You climb up here. Put your foot on this stone first."

The Shepherd Child held the little bird with one hand, steadied himself with the other on his friend's shoulder, and climbed up. The Royal Child stood below, holding the stool firm and pointing out each place for his friend to set his foot.

Then the baby bird was placed safely back inside its nest.

The two boys climbed down, stood under the eaves, and looked up together. A moment later, from inside the nest came a very soft chirp.

Then the two went back to the wooden cart. The wheel still would not turn. Yet this time, both boys sat down side by side and bent their heads together over the same spot.

That afternoon, in the courtyard, neither spoke a word to the other about what had just happened. Up under the eaves, the baby bird had fallen peacefully asleep in its nest.

Under the shade of the tree, the two boys still sat side by side, bending over the little wooden cart.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell the opening slightly briskly and with a smile at the vegetable seller's mix-up—children will delight in the moment both mothers turn around at once. Tell the two passages describing the two boys slowly, giving each boy his own cadence without ranking one above the other. Tell the conversation between the two Marys as though they are truly sitting chatting together. Tell the scene with the little bird in a slightly hushed voice, and let the final sentence settle into complete quiet—do not add anything about what it means.

### Chapter 5: The Twelfth Year

Time slipped onward through the little town.

Day after day, people scarcely notice children growing taller. Then one day, looking back, they suddenly see the children have already reached their shoulders.

That year, the two boys entered their twelfth year.

They no longer raced carefree through the lanes as before. In the mornings, the Royal Child went out with the grown-ups to learn new things. The Shepherd Child stayed at home, went out into the garden, and looked after one animal or another. When evening came, the two still met, yet their conversation had changed.

That winter, the Royal Child grew quieter.

He still answered when spoken to, still did his chores, still ate meals with the family. Yet there were times when he sat completely still for a very long while, gazing steadily into one place. His mother asked:

"Are you tired?"

He shook his head and answered:

"I am listening."

His mother asked no further. Yet inside her heart she felt a quiet wonder. For at that moment, the house was still, and outside in the yard all was still as well.

The Shepherd Child, however, did not change at all: he still noticed which animal had not yet been fed, still shared out his own portion, still asked kindly after travelers passing the gate. And he was still the one who could make his friend laugh out loud right in the midst of sitting silent for so long.

Then one afternoon, the two boys climbed the hill.

That hill rose right behind the town. Looking down from its crest, one could see roof after roof, the white road winding out through the town gate, and even the shepherds' fires far away in the distance.

The two boys sat down side by side.

For a long while neither spoke. Then the Shepherd Child told stories from home: the newborn lamb, the trees in the garden, how his mother baked bread. The Royal Child listened, nodding every now and then.

They sat together a little longer, until the sun went down behind the far hills.

Then together they walked back down.

Spring came. The roads around Nazareth began to fill with travelers.

Every year at this season, people gathered to go up to Jerusalem for the feast. They traveled in caravans, leading donkeys and sheep, carrying bread, water, and mats to sleep on along the way. Children ran ahead of the caravan. Old folk walked at the back, leaning on their staffs.

The Shepherd Child's family went up every year. This year, he was old enough to walk beside his parents like a grown man, no longer riding on a donkey's back.

His mother made ready the loaves. Joseph tightened the straps binding the packs. The two boys stood at the gate, looking out down the road.

The first caravan had already passed. Then the second. Dust rose in clouds and drifted down again.

The Royal Child stood gazing down the road for a very long time, until dusk settled. He said nothing.

"When I get back, I'll tell you all about it," said the Shepherd Child.

The other boy nodded.

Next morning, before daylight had fully broken, the caravan set off.

The boy staying behind stood beside the gate, watching them go. One arm lifted in a wave. Then the white road slowly became empty again.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell most of this chapter in an even, ordinary voice, like everyday life, without underlining anything. When you reach *"I am listening,"* speak the words and then hold a very long silence—that silence matters more than speech. Tell the scene of the two boys on the hill slowly and softly, like an ordinary afternoon. Let the closing sentence fall very lightly, then stop.

### Chapter 6: Jesus in the Temple

The road up to Jerusalem was longer than the boy had imagined.

The caravan walked from morning till evening. Through fields of green wheat, through olive groves, along stony ridges. When tired, they sat down by the wayside to rest. When hungry, they broke loaves of bread together. As night fell, the whole company pitched camp on the hillsides, lit fires, and the grown-ups told old stories to one another.

The Shepherd Child walked amid the caravan. He did not say much, yet his eyes opened wide. For the first time he beheld tall city gates, stone-paved streets, and crowds pouring in from every land.

And then he saw the Temple.

It stood upon a high terrace, shining in the sunlight so brightly that people had to squint. The boy stood still for a moment before stepping onward.

The feast days passed. People brought offerings, sang, prayed, and met with kinsfolk. Children ran all across the courtyards.

During those very same days, back in Nazareth, the Royal Child sat still even more often.

He sat upon the doorstep, leaning his back against the wall, silent for long stretches. Sometimes he slept a long sleep in the middle of the day. His mother sat beside him and laid her hand upon his brow. He opened his eyes, smiled at his mother, and closed them again.

She understood nothing of it. She only sat there.

Then the final day of the feast arrived. The caravan packed its belongings and turned onto the road home.

The Shepherd Child stayed behind in Jerusalem. His parents did not know.

The caravan walked through an entire day's journey. Toward evening, when the company stopped to rest, his parents looked round and could not see their son.

They thought he must be somewhere in the caravan, among their many relatives and kinsfolk. They called out. No one answered.

The father went from place to place, asking everyone. Then the mother went asking too. The more they asked, the colder their hearts grew within them.

Nobody had seen the boy.

Next morning, the two turned back toward Jerusalem.

They walked back along the road they had come the day before. They inquired at the city gates, at the water-stalls, wherever people gathered. They called their son's name amid the crowds until their voices were hoarse.

The first day. Then the second day.

On the third day, they walked into the Temple courtyard.

And there, in a roofed corner of the courtyard, the boy was sitting in the midst of the elders.

The Teachers of the Law sat in a circle. They spoke together about the law and about questions hundreds of years old. The boy sat inside that circle, listening carefully, and now and then asked a question.

He asked very simple questions. Yet they were questions that made the elders fall silent for a long moment before they could answer.

Everyone who walked past turned back to look. Nobody understood why a twelve-year-old boy was sitting there, or why the teachers stayed seated to listen to him.

The mother stood at the edge of the courtyard and did not step up right away. She watched her son. That was still her boy: that way of sitting, those shoulders, the way he bowed his head when listening to someone else speak. Yet when he opened his mouth, she no longer recognized the voice.

She stepped forward and said:

"My son, why have you made your father and me worry so? We have been searching for you for three days!"

The boy looked up at his mother and answered:

"Why were you searching for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?"

The mother heard those words, and did not understand.

Neither did the father understand.

Yet the boy said nothing more. He stood up, bowed respectfully to the teachers, and walked with his parents out of the Temple courtyard.

The caravan walked on in the afternoon. Ahead stretched the road leading back to Nazareth.

The boy stepped steadily beside his mother, holding in his hand a small olive branch picked up along the roadside. He did not speak.

His mother looked over at her son from time to time.

So he returned to Nazareth with his parents. At home, he obeyed his father and mother just as before.

And his mother kept all those things hidden in her heart. She told no one. Yet there were moments, right in the middle of her kitchen work, when she stayed her hands, stood completely still for a moment, and only then went back to her task.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell the journey with a steady, bright rhythm so the children feel the wide open landscape. When you reach *"Next morning, the two turned back toward Jerusalem,"* shift your voice completely: slow down, hold your breath slightly, counting off one day after another. Tell the Temple scene very slowly and softly; let the dialogue between mother and son come out simply and naturally, without theatricality. Let the final sentence fall very gently; add no explanation.

### Chapter 7: Sorrow Arrives

After the pilgrim caravan returned, winter came to Nazareth.

The Royal Child no longer left the house.

He felt no pain. He simply grew fainter, like an oil lamp running dry. His mother tucked extra blankets around him, cracked open the window for fresh air, and sat beside his bed.

Every day the Shepherd Child came over.

He sat down beside the bed without speaking. Sometimes he told his friend little stories from the yard: how the young lamb had grown, how the tree by the gate had put out new leaves. Other times he just held his friend's hand and sat in silence.

One afternoon, the Shepherd Child sat beside the bed. Neither boy spoke a word to the other. Then the Royal Child closed his eyes, and fell asleep.

That night, he slept on and did not wake.

In the morning, the mother sat beside the bed for a very long time. Outside the window, day was just breaking. A bird perched upon the roof, then flew away.

People carried the boy out beyond the town, to a quiet place, and laid him down to rest there.

The Shepherd Child went with them. All the way home, he did not say a single word.

That winter was longer than any winter before it.

During that time, Joseph of the royal line also passed away. And the Shepherd Child's own birth mother departed as well.

Left in the two houses were a carpenter father, a mother who had lost her son, and several young children.

Then the two households moved together under one roof.

Two courtyards became one. One hearth cooked for everyone. Extra chairs were drawn up around the table. That father and that mother cared for the house together, raised the children, and every evening shared supper together.

That evening, inside that house, everyone sat gathered round a single table.

The table was fuller than before. Children's voices piped up, vying for pieces of bread. Spoons clinked softly against bowls.

In the corner of the room, against the wall, a small chair still stood in its place. Nobody moved it away.

The Shepherd Child sat at the head of the table. He ate slowly.

Then he finished his bowl, carried it away to wash, and came back to sit beside everyone a little while longer.

Night fell. The oil lamp faded low. The whole household went to sleep.

#### Storytelling Notes
- This chapter must be told very slowly and serenely, almost as if nothing dramatic were happening in your voice—the weight lies in the silence, not in the words. Pause for a long, full moment after *"That night, he slept on and did not wake."* Speak the two sentences about the further losses a little more quickly and in an even tone, like winter lengthening rather than like a sudden crisis. Never **offer comfort**, never explain, never say a word about the meaning of death. Simply let the ordinary everyday details speak for themselves.

### Chapter 8: The Wise Carpenter

Afterward, in Nazareth, life returned to its familiar rhythm.

People rose early again, opened their doors, went out to the fields and the market. Children ran through the lanes again. Season after season, the trees in the yard put out new leaves.

Joseph the carpenter opened his workshop every morning.

That workshop stood behind the house: a roofed shed stacked high with wood. The scent of fresh-cut timber was so sweet that passersby out in the lane could smell it.

Joseph was not a man of many words. Yet his hands knew many things.

Before cutting a plank, he laid it still upon the bench and stood looking at it. Looking for a long time. He tilted his head and ran his palm along the grain of the wood, watching which way it ran, where it curved, where it twisted, where a knot lay hidden. Only then did he mark it and take up the saw.

Once a stool came in with a broken leg. Someone brought it ready to throw away. Joseph looked it over for a while, then replaced the broken leg with a fresh piece of timber, pegged it tight, and smoothed away the rough scar. That stool went on serving for many more years.

Young Jesus grew up in that workshop.

He stood beside Joseph, watching him work. Soon he took up the tools, learned to drive a nail straight, and made a small shelf.

He learned fast. He only needed to watch once to know how a task was done.

Yet there was something else that made Joseph take notice.

The boy often asked questions like:

"Who is this for, Father?"

At the head of the lane lived an old woman all alone. She had a low stool: easy to sit down upon, yet hard to stand back up from.

One day, Jesus visited her house and saw her bracing herself against the edge of the table just to rise to her feet.

A few days later, he asked Joseph for a bit of wood.

He measured, sawed, and planed. He built a new stool, a little taller than the old one.

Joseph stood nearby watching, saying nothing.

When the stool was finished, Jesus carried it down to the old woman's house and set it beside her table.

She sat down to try it. Then she stood up—and this time she did not have to brace herself on anything at all.

She smiled brightly and thanked him. The boy scratched his head and ran back to the workshop.

Joseph bowed his head and went on with his work.

That afternoon, sunlight slanted across the workshop floor in golden squares. The plane whispered steadily over the wood, swishing softly like gentle rain.

Jesus sat beside Joseph, planing a strip of timber. Now and then he stopped, ran his hand along the spot he had just planed, and then went calmly on.

And here the childhood story of the boy Jesus comes to its end.

#### Storytelling Notes
- Tell the whole chapter slowly and evenly, like the steady sweep of a wood plane, with nowhere needing a raised voice. Tell the passage describing how Joseph studies the grain of wood even more slowly than usual, so children feel that quiet patience. Tell the story of the old woman's stool calmly, without straining for emotion—let the deed speak for itself. Let the final sentence fall very softly and rest in silence, adding no concluding moral.

## Part II: John the Baptist and Dawn on the River Jordan

### Chapter 9: John the Baptist

In the days when King Herod ruled the land of Judea, there lived a priest named Zechariah. His wife, Elizabeth, carried a deep sorrow in her heart. Day by day she grew older, yet the two had never been granted a child. That grieved her beyond measure. Then, an extraordinary thing happened.

According to the custom of the priesthood, lots were cast to choose one priest to enter and offer sacrifice inside the Temple for a full day. This time, the lot fell to Zechariah. Outside in the courtyard, the crowd stood praying, waiting for him to come out and give them a blessing.

When Zechariah began the rite of offering incense inside the Holy Place of the Temple, suddenly a shaft of light appeared beside the altar. An angel spoke aloud:

"Zechariah, your prayer has been heard. Your wife shall bear a son. The child's name shall be John. He will be filled with the Holy Spirit and the strength of the prophet Elijah. He will prepare the way of the Lord to pour blessings down upon all humankind. Rejoice!"

Zechariah was deeply amazed. He asked:

"How can this come to pass? I am already old, and my wife is long past the age of bearing children."

The angel answered:

"I am Gabriel, sent by God to bring you these good tidings. You shall have to keep silent, losing your voice concerning these things until the day your son is born, so that every doubt regarding the wondrous ways of God may vanish from your heart."

Having spoken, the angel vanished. Outside, the people waited eagerly for Zechariah to come out and pronounce the blessing of the offering. When at last he stepped out of the Temple, he had lost his voice completely. Unable to utter a single word, he walked right through the crowd. People murmured in bewilderment:

"What happened inside the Temple to take away his speech? Could he have seen a vision inside?"

For days afterward, he still could not speak. And that wondrous story spread throughout the whole land.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Solemn cadence of the Temple:** Begin with a quiet, subdued voice for Elizabeth's sorrow, then brighten clearly when the light and the angel Gabriel appear beside the altar.
- **Contrast upon stepping out:** Speak the crowd's curious murmuring in a whisper, and slow down the closing sentence as Zechariah remains speechless for many days.

---

### Chapter 10: A Strange Encounter

When Elizabeth was six months with child carrying little John, the Archangel Gabriel also appeared to Mary in Nazareth. The angel brought her tidings of an auspicious birth. Not long afterward, a voice spoke from within, telling Mary:

"Go visit Elizabeth and Zechariah! Stay with them until the day their child is born!"

Straightaway Mary set out, journeying into the hill country of Judah to the town where her cousin and her cousin's husband lived. She stepped inside the house, called out to Elizabeth in a clear, ringing voice, and clasped her close. At that very instant, the child inside Elizabeth's womb leaped for joy. Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit, and from her heart poured words of blessing:

"Blessed are you, Mary, and blessed is the little child Jesus whom you will soon bring into the light of day! The moment your greeting reached my ears, the child inside me leaped for joy!"

Mary stayed with Elizabeth for three full months. Both women overflowed with the warmth of joyful expectation and tender love. Day after day together, they often talked with one another:

"What will our two children bring to the world? Both have been sent down upon the earth, and the Archangel Gabriel foretold the birth of them both."

When three months had passed, Mary returned home, and Elizabeth gave birth to John. Six months after that, in Bethlehem, Mary gave birth to her son Jesus—the child who would later become the Messiah.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Warm, clear tone:** Tell evenly and gently of the joyful reunion of the two mothers in the hill country of Judah.
- **The moment the child leaps:** Gently highlight Mary's clear greeting and Elizabeth's spontaneous blessing; let the closing line about John and Jesus rest softly.

---

### Chapter 11: John Lives with the Essenes

When John grew up into a young man, an inner restlessness urged him out into the wide world.

One day while he was tending sheep out in the pastures, a man dressed in a long robe of pure white drew near. The stranger came and sat down beside him. John took out his leather water-flask and offered the man a drink. The old man drank to quench his thirst and thanked him. He looked deep into the young man's soul and said:

"You were not meant to spend your whole life merely watching sheep. Look: I belong to the brotherhood of the Essene community beside the shores of the Dead Sea."

John asked:

"Since you are a community, what do you strive toward?"

The Essene monk replied:

"Inside human souls, evil and wickedness are running wild. Hatred, falsehood, suspicion, division, and sickness have taken hold of humanity. If humankind keeps living in that darkness of soul, they will perish."

John asked:

"Is there no way to help them?"

The Essene monk answered:

"Every person must begin with himself. To do that with all our soul, long ago we founded communities of quiet discipline. Through daily watchfulness and training, we learn to strengthen the good human being within us, so we may master evil and keep it far away from us. That makes the spirit firm and steady. Many among us can heal sickness and guide people onto a better path."

John asked further:

"How can one become one of the Essenes?"

The stranger answered:

"We welcome young people like you to join us. We teach them through ancient books rich in wisdom, showing them how to help the spirit grow and help the soul find peace through a life of moderation and a pure heart."

As the Essene spoke, a gentle light shone from his eyes. John's heart was deeply stirred, and he asked:

"May I also become one of the Essenes? My father Zechariah has already passed away. I am free to walk my own path."

The monk advised him:

"Think it over very carefully first, young man! You still have your mother! If after seven days you still feel the same, come seek us at Qumran. The brothers will confer together to decide whether to receive you for a time of trial. I am Brother Hiskia. When you arrive, ask for my name! Speak first about this with your mother Elizabeth and your kinsfolk. After all, if you are to join us, it is best to depart with warm blessings!"

Having spoken those words, the monk traced a sign upon John's forehead. Gratefully, John kissed both of the old man's hands, then stood watching the white-robed figure disappear eastward among the rows of palm trees.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Pure open-field atmosphere:** Tell lightly and warmly when John offers water from his leather flask, then slow down through the dialogue about training oneself from within.
- **Emphasize filial care:** Read warmly and attentively Brother Hiskia's counsel about waiting seven days and asking Mother Elizabeth's leave; let the image of the white robe vanishing into the palm grove settle gently.

### Chapter 12: By the Shores of the Dead Sea

One full week passed. After asking leave of his mother Elizabeth, John set out for the settlement of the Essenes near the Dead Sea. Its rough stone walls stood silent in the afternoon light. Everything around was so quiet that he scarcely dared knock at the gate, and sat down on the stone steps instead. When at last he gathered courage to tap softly upon the door with his shepherd's staff, after a long while the wooden bolt inside was drawn back and the gate opened a crack.

"What are you seeking?" asked the man who stepped out.

"Brother Hiskia told me to come here and ask for him by name," the young man replied.

"Come in! What is your name?"

"John, son of Zechariah."

At that very moment, they heard the resonant chime of a gong. The Essene brother said:

"Step inside! The brothers are gathering for evening prayer and stillness. Stand over there against the wall. Hiskia will be here too."

John stood watching row after row of white-robed monks enter silently. Each laid his right arm across his chest. They formed a circle and began humming a low, soothing chant. Next, the brothers placed their hands upon one another's shoulders and the whole circle began slowly turning. The humming gave way to spoken prayer. The circle came to rest again; all knelt prostrate upon the ground. Eyes and hands lifted toward the night sky, praying with the stars. At the end, everyone rose together as one, and the brothers returned to their separate rooms.

Brother Hiskia had recognized John. He stepped over and welcomed him as kindly as a father. He showed John a place to sleep for the night upon dry sand out in the courtyard. From a clay pitcher he poured water into a cup and handed it to the young man along with two dried figs:

"This is your supper. Tomorrow I will introduce you to the brothers. Early in the morning, when the waking call sounds, you may come out to the courtyard and pray with us facing the sun. I wish you a blessed rest!"

So John joined the Essene community at Qumran. He stayed with them for many years, learning wisdom from ancient parchment scrolls, learning the art of speaking, and practicing humility by turning away from worldly pleasures like a true Essene. Yet one longing grew ever stronger within him: to step out and live among his own people. Whenever he went outside the community, his heart grew heavy at seeing darkness spreading through falsehood, hatred, quarreling, unbelief, and all manner of suffering and sickness. Time and again, he visited the villages and the huts of the poor, bringing light into the midst of the people's darkness and pain.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Evening stillness of Qumran:** Read slowly, almost holding your breath when John knocks at the gate with his shepherd's staff, then keep an even cadence following every turn of the circle ceremony beneath the stars.
- **Simple supper:** Read warmly the details of the dry sand, the clay cup of water, and Brother Hiskia's two dried figs before shifting into John's growing longing to serve the poor.

### Chapter 13: The Preacher in the Wilderness

After many years of living with the community, John loosened his ties with the Essene order. A call had come to him, bidding him go wait beside the waters of the River Jordan to greet all who wished to hear his words.

John built a simple hut to shelter from wind and rain and scorching sun. Following the Essene custom, whenever anyone stood ready to repent and be cleansed of sin, they plunged that person beneath the water. Great throngs came to the River Jordan to hear John preach. His words rang with power, filling people with fresh courage and new life.

John spoke:

"Darkness has fallen upon our world! Evil is seizing hold of human hearts. Dulled eyes no longer see the wonders of creation. Ears no longer hear the sacred word of God. That word cannot touch people's hearts, because their hearts have turned hard as stone! They chase after earthly pleasures. Their spirits and souls have grown pale. In that very way, the Evil One attacks them from within. Humankind is walking toward ruin because of its sins.

"Change your hearts, my countrymen! Recognize the dark night in which you are living! Awake to greet the Light of the world!"

More and more people streamed to the River Jordan to hear John speak. After each sermon, many asked him to baptize them and resolved to live better lives.

The priests and the Pharisees at the Jerusalem Temple began to grow jealous of John because he drew such vast crowds. Several among them decided to go down to the River Jordan to hear what that strange prophet was saying. They thought: *"He will say something that gives us an excuse to attack him and force him into silence. The Temple is the center of the world, not the River Jordan!"*

They went down to the riverbank and stood mingled into the crowd a little to one side. At first, the prophet spoke of the light shining in the dark night, recognized by no one. Suddenly, he turned straight toward the priests and Pharisees and spoke directly to them:

"Your wisdom has turned into foolishness! You are ruled by the lust for power. You have turned into a brood of vipers!"

Boiling with anger, the priests and Pharisees stamped their feet and stormed away. From that day on, their hatred toward the prophet beside the River Jordan grew ever deeper. They spread rumors that John was stirring up the people against King Herod.

At the same time, many people came asking John:

"Are you the Messiah, the One promised to us?"

John answered:

"I am not that One. I am preparing the way for Him. He will come after me. I baptize you with water; but He will baptize you with the fire of the Holy Spirit! I am not worthy even to untie the straps of His sandals."

A small band of disciples gathered around John and were taught by him about the hidden pathways of the soul. When the crowds coming to the river grew too vast, the disciples were also given leave to baptize. Before long, thousands had received baptism and awaited the day the Messiah would come.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A voice crying in the wilderness:** Read loudly and clearly the sermon about darkness and the call: *"Change your hearts!"*
- **Contrast between two tones:** Let your voice ring sharp and resolute when John rebukes the priests and Pharisees as a *"brood of vipers,"* then soften into humility and sincerity when he answers their question about the Messiah.

### Chapter 14: The Baptism of Jesus

John received an inner prompting that the Messiah was walking down to the River Jordan. From afar, he saw Jesus of Nazareth approaching alone, his whole form bathed in light. Yet above his head hung a dark shadow of sorrow, from all the countless sufferings and sorrows of humankind. John stepped forward to meet him and wished to fall down before his feet.

But Jesus commanded:

"John, baptize Me!"

John's disciples stepped aside, and two of them came forward to hold Jesus's cloak. When Jesus stepped down into the river, John took the baptismal bowl, dipped up water, and poured it downward in three streams: onto the back of His head, over the crown of His head, and across His brow down over His face. As he poured the water, John spoke:

"May God pour His blessing down upon You, through the Cherubim and Seraphim, in Wisdom, Love, and Strength!"

Suddenly there came a mighty roar from high above. Thunder rolled echoing across the sky and the heavens tore wide open. Inside a blazing cloud, a white-winged being like a dove alighted, pouring floods of radiance over Jesus. From the midst of the thunder, the voice of God rang out:

"This is My Beloved Son!"

John was overwhelmed by the sight of the Heavenly Hosts, and understood clearly: now the Christ stood present among humankind as the Son of God. Yet when he lifted his eyes upstream toward the headwaters of the River Jordan, he saw a pitch-black mass of cloud rolling in thick coils, filled within by dark forms led by the princes of Hell. At once he knew that all the powers of Hell would rise up to oppose the Messiah's work upon the earth.

As Jesus Christ walked away from that place, John spoke to two disciples standing beside him:

"Behold Him! Walking along that riverbank is the Greatest Man of God, humble as a little lamb! My work is complete. He will grow greater, while I must grow smaller. Follow Him, and receive His words!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Slow cadence for the rite of the three streams:** Read slowly, clearly separating the three places where the water is poured *(back of the head, crown of the head, brow and face)* and the three virtues *(Wisdom, Love, Strength)*.
- **From heaven's thunder to the dark cloud:** Lift your voice bright and radiant when the white dove descends and the voice of God speaks; drop into a brief, troubled hush when looking upstream at the black cloud over the Jordan's headwaters; then let the parting words to the two disciples fall gently.

### Chapter 15: On the Death of the Forerunner

The Pharisees and priests never forgave John for calling them a brood of vipers. They sought the backing of King Herod—the son of the king who had slaughtered the infants in Bethlehem. The priests brought many precious gifts from the Temple treasury to offer the king so that he would carry out their wishes. King Herod promptly sent soldiers to arrest John the Baptist, falsely accused him of deceiving and stirring up the people, and locked him away in a dark dungeon inside the fortress of Machaerus.

Two of John's disciples had now become followers of Christ and witnessed His miracles. They made their way into the prison to visit John and told their teacher of the deeds Christ was doing:

"He makes the blind see, makes the lame walk again, cleanses lepers, gives hearing back to the deaf, and raises the dead to life!"

Great joy welled up in John's heart, and he said:

"It was granted to me to shed the light of the moon. But He Himself is the glorious radiance of the sun!"

When dark tidings reached Christ and His disciples that King Herod had ordered John put to death, He declared:

"Since the days of Adam until now, no purer soul has ever lived in human form. He is the angel who prepares the way and the last of the prophets. Truly, he is Elijah! Comfort your hearts! From this day forth, he will always be present in our midst!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Do not dwell on violence:** Tell concisely and calmly of the arrest and imprisonment at the fortress of Machaerus, preserving the solemn atmosphere suited for ages 9–10.
- **Moonlight yielding to the Sun:** Speak warmly and serenely in John's reply, then settle deeply and steadily into Christ's immortal tribute closing Book IV.
