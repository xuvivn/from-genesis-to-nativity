# Book III: We Will Build a Temple
*(Full Text Edition — Ages 9–10)*

---

## Cluster 1: King Solomon

### Chapter 1: Solomon Ascends the Throne

When King David had grown very old, he sent word to the prophet Nathan and the priest Zadok:

"Anoint my son Solomon as king! Sound the trumpets far and wide so all the people may hear! Set him upon the royal mule, lead him into my palace, and walk beside him to bring him to the throne!"

They did everything just as King David had commanded.

Throughout his years as king, David had gathered a great treasure of gold, silver, and precious stones. He told his son Solomon:

"I place all of this treasure into your hands so that you may build the Temple."

So David's vast treasure was entrusted to Solomon, that he might build a worthy Temple in Jerusalem.

When King David passed away, the Lord poured wisdom abundantly into Solomon's spirit, so that he might rule the people with justice and raise the Temple as a sacred sanctuary.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A solemn opening cadence:** Read slowly and firmly through King David's parting command, then let your voice ring out brightly with the sound of the trumpets proclaiming it to all the people.
- **A peaceful passing of the torch:** Soften your voice gently in the final two sentences, placing tender emphasis on the father's heartfelt treasure and the stream of wisdom the Lord poured into Solomon's spirit.

---

### Chapter 2: Solomon's Sacrifice and Prayer

Once, King Solomon traveled to Gibeon to offer burnt sacrifices upon the mountaintop. That night, the voice of God spoke to him:

"Solomon, you may ask one favor of Me. What gift would you have Me bestow upon you?"

Solomon answered:

"O Lord, grant me a wise and understanding heart to rule Your people—a heart that knows how to tell Good from Evil."

The voice of God replied:

"You shall have a deep and wise heart. And it shall bring you both honor and riches."

Solomon awoke and realized it had been a dream—yet it was a dream that spoke the truth.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Night air on the mountaintop:** Keep your voice quiet and still in the deep night at Gibeon as the Lord's voice speaks within Solomon's sleep.
- **A humble prayer:** Speak slowly and clearly through his request for *"a wise and understanding heart... that knows how to tell Good from Evil,"* then let the final sentence about the truth of the dream linger softly.

---

### Chapter 3: The Judgment of Solomon

One day, a servant stepped before Solomon's throne and announced:

"Outside the door, two women are waiting. They beg leave to come before you and state their case."

Solomon replied:

"Let them enter; in my name, they are welcome."

The two women entered and bowed low before the throne. One held a baby in her arms; the other carried nothing but an empty blanket. The king spoke to the woman without the child:

"Tell me your grievance; what do you ask for? If justice stands on your side, your wish shall be granted!"

The woman cried out through her sobs:

"This woman's house and mine stand side by side. Last evening, I lay down to sleep beside my child. When I woke early this morning, I found her dead baby lying in my room—and my own child was gone!"

With a sharp and bitter tongue, the second woman snapped back:

"That is not true; you are lying! You are deceiving the King! It was your child that died, and mine is the one alive. O King, judge between us! Restore what is rightfully mine!"

On and on the two women argued fiercely over the living child.

Solomon spoke to his armed guard:

"Draw your sword. Cut the child in two! Give one half to each woman!"

The woman standing on the king's left spoke at once:

"Yes, yes, divide the child between the two of us! One half for me and the other half for her. I agree to that!"

With those words, she held the infant out toward the guard.

But in the true mother's heart, deep and genuine love for her child suddenly blazed forth. She wept aloud:

"Oh, my lord King, please do not! Do not cut the baby in two! Spare that tender little life! Better that I give the child to her, even though she has lied."

At once Solomon declared:

"Stay your hand, swordsman! Do not strike the child! The one who would rather give her baby away than see it die—she is the true mother."

When the guard placed the baby into her arms, her warm tears fell onto the infant's tiny face. Filled with gratitude, the mother lifted her hands toward Solomon, and the king raised his hand to trace a blessing over the child.

The other woman sprang up to flee, but the guard seized her firmly by the arm and held her back. She sank to her knees before Solomon's throne and pleaded:

"Forgive me, O King! The grief of losing my own child clouded my mind and drove me mad."

Seeing her sorrow and remorse, Solomon spoke gently:

"Rise to your feet! Go home in peace, and sin no more!"

Before long, everyone in Jerusalem was telling one another the story of the two women and the baby who was spared from the sword. Praise for Solomon's wisdom and fairness echoed throughout the entire land.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrasting the two women:** Use a trembling, choked voice for the grieving mother who lost her child, and a shrill, harsh tone for the woman who lies.
- **The climax of the sword:** Keep Solomon's voice stern and cold when commanding the child to be divided; pause for a beat, then burst out with the true mother's heartbroken cry, before warming into gentle mercy as Solomon grants forgiveness.

### Chapter 4: The Wealth and Glory of Solomon

Solomon built many cities, and his wealth grew greater day by day. The king's fleet of merchant ships sailed across the wide seas, bringing back countless goods and treasures for the land. The King of Tyre sent him tall cedars from Lebanon along with many skilled carpenters, and Solomon set them to work building a magnificent palace.

Next, the king wished those master builders to raise a Temple as the House of God in Jerusalem. That was the sacred charge his father, King David, had left him before his death.

The new Temple would follow the form of the sacred tent—the Tabernacle—that had once stood upon the high hill of Gibeon. Ever since the days of Moses, that tent had been the one House of God where the priests performed their holy service. Now the old tent was worn with age and needed to be replaced by a lasting Temple right in the heart of Jerusalem. The new Temple was planned to be twice as large, built solidly of timber and stone, just as God had revealed to Moses upon Mount Sinai. And the sacred chest—the Ark of the Covenant holding the two stone tablets of the Ten Commandments that Moses had carried down from Sinai—would once again be placed with all honor inside the Holy of Holies.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A bright, expansive rhythm:** Speak vividly and cheerfully of the merchant ships crossing the seas and the fragrant cedars of Lebanon arriving in Jerusalem.
- **The thread of sacred tradition:** Slow down with quiet reverence when recalling the Tabernacle from the time of Moses and the Ark of the Covenant about to enter the Holy of Holies.

---

### Chapter 5: Building the Temple

Solomon hired thousands of skilled Phoenician carpenters, and a vast host of Israelite workers labored beside them. To direct the work, he invited the master craftsman Hiram of Tyre, renowned far and wide for his skill in casting bronze. Hiram accepted the call. He knew every secret of melting and molding metal. With his own art, he cast the two colossal bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz, which stood like *"silent watchmen"* before the entrance of the Temple.

The Temple had an outer porch, followed by the main sanctuary where only the priests might enter. A five-sided doorway led into the innermost chamber, called the Holy of Holies. Once the Temple was consecrated, not even the priests would be permitted to step into that final chamber, for it belonged solely to the Spirit of God and the Ark of the Covenant. Every wall inside the Temple was overlaid with plates of pure gold.

When the great work stood complete, a solemn festival of dedication was held. The Ark of the Covenant and all the holy vessels were carried from the Tabernacle and made ready before the Temple. Only after the firewood for the sacrificial flame had been stacked upon the great altar in the courtyard were the priests permitted to carry the sacred vessels inside.

The ram's horns sounded a deep, rolling blast, and the High Priest cried out in a ringing voice:

> *"Carry into the consecrated place*
> *the sacred Ark bearing the Ten Commandments!"*

Bearing it upon long carrying poles, twelve priests carried the Ark of the Covenant inside and set it down in the Holy of Holies.

Once more the horns sounded, and again the High Priest called out:

> *"Bring inside the two golden Seraphim,*
> *set them beside the sacred Ark,*
> *one to the right and one to the left,*
> *so that the tips of their wings gently touch!"*

When this was finished, the High Priest closed the doors of the Holy of Holies tightly and stepped back into the long hall of the main sanctuary. Each time before he spoke, the horns blew a resonant blast. Now once again he called aloud:

> *"Bring in the lampstand!*
> *Seven branches spreading wide,*
> *according to the number of the planets!"*

Ten priests—exactly ten, one for each Commandment—carried the seven-branched lampstand into the main hall of the Temple.

When that was done, the High Priest proclaimed:

> *"Now arrange the twelve loaves of bread*
> *in a heavenly circle within the House of the Lord!"*

With deep reverence, the priests placed the loaves upon the golden table as a sign of the vast circle of the stars.

The horns echoed yet again, and the High Priest called out once more:

> *"Hang before the doorway of the Holy of Holies*
> *the veil woven in the colors of the four elements!"*

Last of all, the altar bearing the vessels of incense was carried inside, and soon a wondrous fragrance drifted throughout the entire Temple.

When all these rites were fulfilled, Solomon and the priests stepped out into the inner courtyard and stood before the great altar. The outer courts of the Temple were crowded to overflowing with people. The horns sounded a hymn of praise, and the whole city grew hush-quiet in awe and devotion.

Solomon knelt down upon the ground before the altar and prayed for the Lord's blessing. The priests and the sea of gathered people all bowed low with him.

The instant the final *"Amen"* left his lips, a bolt of lightning flashed straight down from high heaven onto the stacked wood of the altar, kindling the sacrificial fire into a mighty blaze. Rolling thunder shook the multitude to their hearts. Every eye turned upward toward the sky. A luminous cloud descended from above and rested, glowing brightly, over the Temple.

Then the people whispered softly to one another:

"Now the glory of the Lord fills the Temple and makes this place holy."

Solomon proclaimed in a loud, clear voice:

"From this hour onward, only the priests may enter the inner chambers of the Temple! And this fire upon the altar, kindled from high heaven, must burn night and day for as long as the Temple stands!"

At once the priests appointed watchers to tend the sacred flame so that it would never go out.

When the sacrifice was over, Solomon walked over to the enormous basin of water resting upon the backs of twelve bronze oxen. Master Hiram had cast this basin, which was called *"the bronze sea."* Every bronze ox was fashioned with such marvelous skill that pure water poured forth from the mouth of each one, allowing the priests to wash and cleanse themselves before offering sacrifices.

After that great festival day, the people of Israel said to one another:

"Jerusalem and our people now have a heart!"

From that very day, the fire upon the altar before the Temple burned steadily, night and day, for many centuries.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **The refrain of consecration:** Pause briefly before each chant of the High Priest, as if the blast of the horns has just faded; read the verse lines with solemn dignity, giving clear emphasis to the symbolic numbers (*12 priests*, *the 7-branched lampstand — the 7 planets*, *the 12 loaves — the heavenly circle*, *the 4 elements*).
- **The moment of heavenly fire:** Drop your voice softly on *"Amen,"* ring out boldly on the lightning bolt and rolling thunder, then warm into quiet wonder on the whisper, *"Jerusalem and our people now have a heart!"*

### Chapter 6: The Division of the Kingdom

Whenever a noble and good work brings blessing to humankind, Satan rises up and tries to mingle evil into it. So it happened with Solomon as well. As he grew older and his spirit weakened, evil crept toward him again and again in the darkness of night.

The king allowed altars to be built upon three hilltops for the idols of foreign gods, so that visitors coming from other lands to his kingdom might have places to worship. More than once, Solomon even took part in their sacrifices himself. Thus he strayed from the right path, and the light of the Temple slowly began to dim.

After Solomon died, strife broke out across the kingdom. His son Rehoboam was proud and haughty, so only two tribes remained loyal to him—Judah and Benjamin—along with the capital city of Jerusalem.

The remaining ten tribes formed a northern kingdom and crowned Jeroboam—who had once been a capable officer under Solomon—as their king. They named their country Israel, and Jeroboam made Shechem his capital. So that his people might have places to worship without making the long pilgrimage to Jerusalem, the new king soon set up two shrines with statues of golden calves. Before the altars of those Egyptian-style idols, his people bowed in prayer.

Then endless turmoil and conflict erupted between the two brother kingdoms, bringing sorrow and hardship down upon the land of Palestine. By the time an especially cruel king named Ahab ruled over Israel, God awakened the prophet Elijah. He stepped forward as a true servant of God to confront the corruption of both the people and the royal house.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A somber note of warning:** Read the opening slowly and thoughtfully, conveying how evil steals quietly closer when the spirit grows weak.
- **Division and transition:** Tell of the splitting of the kingdom in firm, clear strokes, then steady your voice with quiet strength on the closing sentence introducing the prophet Elijah.

## Cluster 2: The Prophet Elijah

### Chapter 7: The Prophet Elijah

King Ahab of Israel took for his wife Jezebel, a princess from the distant land of Sidonia. She was a haughty woman, and people whispered that she was a sorceress. In the wagons that followed her, Jezebel brought not only her carved idols, but also the priests of her god, Baal. King Ahab was forced to build a temple for his wife's god in his capital city of Samaria. That false temple became a rival to Solomon's holy sanctuary in Jerusalem. Soon the people of Israel turned away from worshiping the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

A terrible drought fell upon the entire land. No rain came from the sky, and no dew settled in the night. First the grass withered away, and then the trees and the crops in the fields. Next the cattle perished, and the parched fields turned into barren desert. In the dreadful famine that followed, people began to collapse and die.

In those days, near the city of Samaria, there lived a farmer named Naboth. Through him, the lofty spirit of God's messenger—Elijah—spoke forth. The Lord commanded him:

"Go out among the people and speak to them, that they may turn away from praying to false idols. Do this quietly, and do not reveal who you are."

One by one, Elijah appeared in humble thatched huts, in quiet villages, and along lonely country paths. He urged the people to serve the one true God. His words rang with such earnestness that everyone's heart was stirred and listened. Yet every time the villagers tried to invite the mysterious prophet to stay a little longer or find a bed for the night, he had already vanished. Soon afterward, he would appear in another place and move the hearts of the people there. Everywhere, folks whispered in wonder about the hidden prophet, though no one knew who he truly was.

Before long, rumors of this new prophet reached the ears of King Ahab and Jezebel. The whole affair filled them with an eerie dread. The king suspected that some plot was brewing against him. Jezebel muttered uneasily:

"How can we stop this prophet from doing us harm?"

The king sent out spies to hunt down this nameless threat, yet no one could find him. Meanwhile, the voice of God spoke to Elijah:

"Go to King Ahab and tell him that the people must restore their faith in the God of Abraham. Until they do so, not a single drop of rain shall fall upon the earth."

Then suddenly one day, the prophet stood right before the gates of the king's palace. He said to the guard:

"Go inside and tell the king that the prophet is here and wishes to speak with him."

When the king heard this message, fear gripped his heart; nevertheless, he ordered the prophet to be brought before him. In mighty words ringing with authority, Elijah declared to the king:

"No rain shall fall upon the earth until you and your people return to the true God!"

Having spoken those words, he strode out of the palace and vanished.

The Lord spoke to Naboth-Elijah:

"Go into solitude by the brook Cherith; make your heart strong there so that you may stand firm before the king and the people."

In the deep stillness of a stone cave, Elijah gave himself over to earnest prayer and received sustaining light from the spiritual world. At that time, a little water still trickled in the brook Cherith for Elijah to drink. Each day, two ravens flew to him bearing food to keep him alive in his hiding place. Then at last the little brook dried up, and the voice of God guided Elijah toward the town of Zarephath.

When he reached the town gate, Elijah met a widow gathering sticks of firewood. He sat down upon a stone nearby. The woman looked at him and saw that the stranger was faint with thirst. He asked her:

"Could you please bring me a little water and a piece of bread? My throat is parched dry."

She answered:

"I have no bread left—only a handful of flour and a drop of oil. Soon my son and I will starve to death."

She went indoors and brought out a pitcher of water for the stranger to drink. Afterward he said to her:

"Take that flour and oil and bake a small flat loaf. The flour and oil in your house shall never run dry."

She went back inside and did exactly as the stranger had told her, and neither the flour nor the oil grew any less. Every day from then on, Elijah, the widow, and her young son had enough to eat. Elijah stayed in the small upper room at the top of their house.

Not long afterward, the boy fell gravely ill—so ill that no sign of life remained in him. Elijah carried the boy up to his own upper room and laid him gently upon his own bed. Three times he bent low over the child and cried out in prayer:

"O Lord, my God, let this child's life return into his body!"

Look! Life stirred once more within the boy. Elijah carried him downstairs, placed him in his mother's arms, and said:

"See, good woman—your son is alive!"

Filled with sacred awe, she cried out:

"Now I know that you are a man of God, and a true prophet indeed!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A fleeting, mysterious presence:** Keep a brisk, airy pace as Elijah moves through humble huts and quiet villages before vanishing; turn crisp and resolute when he stands before Ahab's palace gates.
- **Two moments with the widow:** Speak warmly through the promise of the unfailing jar of flour, then slow down into deep stillness as Elijah bends three times to pray life back into the boy *(this very boy would later grow up to become the prophet Jonah)*.

### Chapter 8: On Mount Carmel

When suffering in the land reached its darkest hour, the voice of God spoke to Elijah:

"Go and show yourself before King Ahab! Through a sacrifice upon Mount Carmel, you shall bring rain back to the earth."

Elijah went to meet the king, and Ahab shouted at him:

"So it is you—the troubler who has brought all these disasters upon Israel?"

Yet Elijah answered him boldly:

"It is not I who brought disaster upon Israel, but you and your royal house. You have forsaken the God of our fathers and led the people astray to worship the idol Baal. Listen to me, O King: summon all the people to Mount Carmel, along with every priest who serves Baal. Tell them to stack wood for a burnt offering, but set no fire to it. I will do the same. Then the priests of Baal shall call upon their god to send down fire. Afterward, I will call upon the God of our fathers. Whichever God sends fire from high heaven to kindle the offering—He is the true God."

King Ahab sent messengers across all Israel, commanding the people and the priests to gather upon Mount Carmel.

On the appointed day, two altars were built and two sacrificial animals were made ready beside them. The priests of Baal raised a massive altar. They strode back and forth in gaudy robes and tall, brightly adorned hats. Elijah, dressed in a simple cloak of coarse cloth, stacked twelve stones one upon another—one stone for each of the tribes of Israel.

Elijah lifted his voice and spoke to the entire multitude gathered there:

"I alone am left as a prophet of the Lord, while the prophets of Baal number four hundred and fifty men! Let them call upon their god Baal. Let him light the fire for their offering. Afterward, I will call upon the name of the Lord. The God who answers with fire—He is the true God."

At once, the priests of Baal began to leap and dance wildly around their altar, howling without pause:

"O Baal, hear us! Send down fire! O Baal, hear us! Send down fire!"

Now the story tells that the priests of Baal had secretly hidden one of their fellows underneath the pile of wood with a pot of ashes and glowing embers. According to their plan, he was to blow upon the embers and light the wood in secret while the others cried out to Baal. But a venomous snake slithered down beneath the woodpile. It bit the hidden priest upon the foot; even before his companions began their loud chanting, he already lay dead beneath the altar.

The dancing priests shrieked louder and louder, yet not a single spark appeared. Driven into a frenzy, they drew knives and swords and slashed at their own skin until blood streamed down onto the ground as they danced. Still, no flame came.

Elijah raised his mighty voice and called out to all the people:

"Come close around me, all of you!"

Following the ancient custom, he prepared the bull, laid it upon the wood, and ordered sacred water to be poured until everything was soaked through. Then he prayed to God:

"O Lord, hear me! Let Your people know that You alone are the true God!"

A dazzling bolt of lightning flashed straight down from the high heavens and set the offering ablaze. Struck with awe and joy, the people shouted aloud:

"The Lord is our God! The Lord alone is our God!"

So the prophecy was fulfilled, and the priests of Baal were put to shame. Elijah climbed to the highest summit of Mount Carmel. He bowed low to the ground, buried his face between his knees, and cast his gaze out toward the distant sea. Above the waters, a small cloud began to form, and he watched it swell into a mighty storm cloud. The gale began to roar. Soon a pouring rain tumbled down, soaking the parched earth through and through. Brooks and rivers flowed once more. Wells filled to the brim again, and within a few days fresh green shoots of grass sprang up from the dry ground.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Dramatic contrast:** Use a quick, chaotic rhythm when the 450 priests dance and when telling of the hidden deceiver bitten by the snake beneath the wood; turn calm, steady, and grounded when Elijah stacks the twelve stones and pours the sacred water.
- **The climax and the rain:** Let your voice blaze brightly with the lightning from heaven and the people's glad shout, then ease into peaceful relief as the little cloud over the sea grows into the rain that heals the dry earth.

### Chapter 9: Elijah on Mount Horeb

King Ahab told his wife, Jezebel, everything Elijah had done upon the mountain. Jezebel was a fierce devotee of Baal, so she bent all her power toward putting Elijah to death. Hearing of her dark plot, Elijah fled into the desert and left his servant behind in Beer-sheba. That evening, before lying down to sleep beneath a broom bush, he prayed:

"O Lord, take my life! I have endured enough."

Yet in the night, an angel of the Lord touched him and said:

"Arise and eat! The journey before you is still very long."

Right beside him he found food and water, and day after day he walked onward until he reached Mount Horeb in the land of Sinai, where he took shelter in a stone cave. There he received sacred revelations that made his soul grow ever more like the souls of the angels. For forty days he dwelt in that deep solitude, just as Moses had once done in that very place.

When Elijah returned, he met a farmer out in the fields driving a team of oxen to plow the soil. Elijah walked up to him and asked:

"What is your name?"

The plowman answered:

"Elisha!"

Elijah recognized that this man was to be his disciple and successor. He took off his own mantle and laid it over the young man's shoulders as a sign that he had taken him under his wing.

The plowman said:

"I will follow you, Master! Only let me go home for a moment to kiss my father and mother goodbye, and then I will walk with you."

So Elisha became the disciple of the prophet Elijah. Through all the remaining years of Elijah's life on earth, his faithful pupil Elisha never left his master's side.

At length, the great prophet knew the time had come for him to leave the earthly world. Together with Elisha and a few disciples, he walked down to the bank of the River Jordan. Elijah rolled up his mantle and struck the water. At once the river parted to either side. Yet Elisha alone was permitted to cross with the prophet to the far shore. When the two of them set foot on the opposite bank, Elijah asked:

"Ask what I may do for you before I am taken away from you."

Elisha answered:

"May your spirit rest upon me through every hour of trial."

Even as master and disciple were speaking together, a blazing chariot drawn by winged horses of fire swept down beside them. Elijah stepped onto the chariot and rose high toward the heavens. Yet he let his mantle fall behind, and it drifted straight down at Elisha's feet. Elisha picked up the mantle and walked along the edge of the River Jordan. Then he struck the water with the mantle, just as Elijah had done. The river parted in two, opening a clear pathway before him. Now Elisha knew for certain that the power and strength of the prophet were with him. The disciples were still waiting on the other bank. Seeing Elisha stride across the dry bed of the Jordan, they bowed low in reverence and said:

"The spirit of Elijah rests upon Elisha!"

In the years that followed, Elisha worked many wonders: he turned foul, murky water pure and sweet; he healed the sick and those stricken with leprosy; he multiplied a small store of food into plenty; and he even brought a dead boy back to life. To the people, he was a bringer of peace; more than once, through patient peacemaking and goodwill, he prevented war altogether.

Elisha anointed a capable army commander named Jehu to become king against Ahab and Jezebel. Jehu swept away the worship of Baal and tore down Baal's temple. He became the judge who brought punishment upon Ahab, Jezebel, and their entire royal house, bringing an end to the dynasty that had brought so much ruin upon the land and its people.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From exhaustion to revelation:** Let your voice droop wearily on Elijah's plea beneath the broom bush, then brighten softly across the forty days of solitude on Mount Horeb.
- **Passing on the mantle:** Speak with solemn awe and crisp power as the fiery chariot with winged horses sweeps down, and hold a brief pause as the mantle falls directly at Elisha's feet.

## Cluster 3: Tobias

### Chapter 10: Tobias

One of the few Jews who did not turn to worshiping idols was a man named Tobias. He too was among the Israelites who had been carried off as captives to the land of the Assyrians. Yet because of his great skill and faithfulness in serving the king, he won much respect. He owned his own house and possessed great wealth, while many of his fellow Israelites were held as slaves and lived in bitter hardship.

Tobias and his wife, Hanna, had only one son, who was also named Tobias. Everyone called him young Tobias. His father often took young Tobias along whenever he went about his affairs. From time to time, father and son visited the places where the Israelites lived in poverty and were forced into grueling labor. The elder Tobias comforted them all, gave wise advice on healing illnesses, and taught them Hebrew prayers. Young Tobias always carried a basket or a pouch at his side, and he was always allowed to share freely with the hungry and poor. In this way, old Tobias eased great suffering, and a kind and tender heart blossomed within the boy.

In those days, King Shalmaneser died. His son Sennacherib oppressed the Israelites even more cruelly than before. Many people were beaten to death over the slightest trifle. When the new king learned that the elder Tobias was going everywhere to aid his fellow Israelites, he flew into a furious rage. He seized Tobias's house and possessions, forcing him to flee for his life with Hanna and their son. Yet after reigning only forty-five days, the tyrant Sennacherib was slain by his own sons. Tobias was then permitted to return home and reclaim all his property and wealth.

One day, the elder Tobias lay resting in the warm sunshine beside the wall of his house. Droppings from a swallow's nest clinging high on the wall above fell straight into his eyes. Through that misfortune, he became blind. From then on, life seemed wrapped in such heavy darkness that he believed God would soon call his soul home to Himself. He called his son to his side and said:

"In this room, high up beneath the roof, a thick wooden beam runs across the ceiling. Right near the middle, a piece of parchment is tucked inside a crack. Reach up and bring it down to me!"

Tobias found the yellowed parchment and placed it into his father's hands. His father went on:

"When you were still a little boy, I lent ten pounds of silver to a man named Gabael, who lives in the city of Rages in Media. He signed his name upon this parchment as proof of the debt. Gabael is an honest man; set out on a journey to find him! He will repay you the silver he borrowed in exchange for this parchment. Be sure to find a trustworthy companion to travel with you. May the blessing of God rest upon you!"

When his pack was ready, young Tobias embraced and kissed both his father and his mother, and set out upon his journey. He had not gone far from home when he met a handsome young man who walked with a strangely weightless grace. Tobias could not know that this was an angel wearing human form. Tobias's little dog, trotting faithfully beside him, leaped up joyfully and bounded forward to greet the stranger.

Tobias spoke to the stranger:

"I am looking for a traveling companion. Would you like to walk with me? I could never hope to find a better companion than you. Will you come with me to the land of the Medes?"

In a clear, bell-like voice, the young man replied:

"I will journey there with you. You may call me Azariah."

When the young man held out his hand to clasp Tobias's in a promise to travel together, Tobias felt a warm glow, like a gentle fire, spread through his whole body.

The first day's walk brought the two travelers to the banks of the River Tigris. Tobias wanted to wash his tired feet in the cool water. All at once, a huge fish swam up and snapped at his toes. Tobias cried out in alarm, while his companion shouted:

"Catch hold of its fin! Pull it out of the water!"

Tobias obeyed at once and hauled the fish up onto the bank. He cleaned the fish and roasted it for their evening meal.

Then the young man told him:

"Do not throw away the fish's gall—keep it with you, and guard it carefully!"

Tobias asked:

"Tell me, Azariah, what am I to do with this fish's gall?"

The young man answered:

"It is a medicine to heal the eyes. Bring it home for your father!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Childhood warmth and a father's trust:** Tell gently of father and son carrying their basket to share with the poor, and the trusting farewell beside the parchment tucked in the ceiling beam.
- **Signs of an angel in disguise:** Gently highlight the little dog leaping with joy and the *"warm glow, like a gentle fire,"* that passed through Azariah's handshake.

### Chapter 11: With Raguel and Sarah

When they reached the first city in the land of Media, Azariah said to Tobias:

"In this city lives a man named Raguel, who is a kinsman of yours. He has only one daughter, named Sarah. If you ask her father for her hand in marriage, I am sure it will bring you boundless happiness."

Tobias marveled that Azariah knew so much about everything, and he agreed to turn aside and visit his kinsman. When they arrived, Tobias told Raguel who he was. At once Raguel wept tears of joy and exclaimed:

"You are the son of my dear cousin! You are the son of a good and righteous man who fears the Lord."

Seeing that Raguel's daughter Sarah was gentle, graceful, and kind, Tobias decided to stay until their wedding day. He asked young Azariah:

"Would you be willing to carry this parchment to Rages for me? Find the worthy Gabael there and give him the paper, and invite him to come join us for our wedding feast."

Azariah paused for a brief moment and looked up toward the sky, as though reading a message written among the clouds. Then he smiled and nodded in agreement. A few days later, he brought old Gabael back with him to the wedding. Gabael said to Tobias:

"Long ago, when I had fallen on hard times, your father helped me with that loan of silver. Now I have gold, silver, and a prosperous home of my own. Here is the silver I borrowed, to carry back to your father."

Azariah behaved in the strangest way. Often he would vanish for days at a time. Yet whenever the hour came to set out on the road, he suddenly stood right beside them again.

After a few days, Tobias said to his bride:

"I know my father and mother at home must be filled with worry because I have been gone so long. Perhaps they even fear that death has overtaken me. Dear Sarah, let us bid farewell to your father and mother and set out on the road back to my home."

Raguel gave the young couple generous wedding gifts and animals to ride on their homeward journey, and Azariah traveled along with them.

After a long journey, as their little caravan drew near the courtyard before Tobias's parents' house, his little dog suddenly dashed ahead to announce his master's return. Close behind came young Tobias, bringing home not only the shining silver, but also a gentle, loving young wife. Azariah stepped quietly to one side while the family embraced with tears and laughter, leaving all their joy to them. Yet a moment later, he stepped up beside young Tobias and whispered softly:

"Rub the fish's gall upon your father's eyes! He will see again!"

At once old Tobias lay back, and his son spread the fish's gall gently over his father's eyes. He lay still for a few moments—and then a thin white film peeled away, and bit by bit, pure bright light flooded into his eyes! The father beheld Sarah standing beside Tobias, beheld Azariah, and gave thanks to high heaven for the miracle and all the goodness bestowed upon him.

Suddenly, Azariah stepped forward and spoke aloud:

"May the angels forever guide the destiny of humankind! Now let all of you know the full truth of who I am: I am Raphael, the healing angel of God!"

Wonder and breathless awe swept over the gathering that only a moment before had rung with cheerful laughter; a trembling reverence fell upon every person there. They bowed low with their faces to the ground. Azariah's voice rang out:

"Give thanks unto the Lord! Proclaim His glory far and wide!"

When the family dared to lift their heads and look up again, Azariah-Raphael had vanished. In the very spot where he had stood, a dazzling radiance was gently fading away like a luminous cloud. The aged father broke into a song of praise, and their joy and gratitude knew no bounds.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Quiet hints of mystery:** Keep an easy, natural pace when Azariah gazes up at the clouds and whenever he vanishes only to reappear right when it is time to set out.
- **Sight restored and the angel revealed:** Read slowly and breathlessly as the white film peels away and light floods old Tobias's eyes; pause for a beat before the declaration *"I am Raphael,"* then let your voice float softly into the fading cloud of light and the closing song of praise.

## Cluster 4: Isaiah

### Chapter 12: Isaiah

In those days, while the people of Israel were carried away into exile in the kingdom of Assyria, a good king named Hezekiah still reigned in Jerusalem. God's prophet Isaiah was both friend and teacher to the king. Hezekiah knew that the Israelites of the northern kingdom had grown weak because they had worshiped foreign gods and forsaken the faith of their ancestors. For that very reason, King Shalmaneser of Assyria had conquered Israel and carried thousands away as captives.

Following the counsel of the prophet Isaiah, King Hezekiah resolved to free the land of Judah from idol worship. He ordered every heathen altar to be torn down, wherever it stood—upon high mountains, in deep forests, or beneath spreading trees. Isaiah preached with fiery zeal to the people about the true faith of their fathers. That was no easy task, for many had already grown fond of those foreign customs.

Isaiah said:

"When the people of Judah choose the path of righteousness, we have nothing to fear from the Assyrians."

Then King Shalmaneser of Assyria died, and his son Sennacherib took the throne. Looking greedily toward Jerusalem, he thought to himself:

"I can conquer this land and that splendid city, just as my father conquered the northern kingdom of Israel. Inside the Temple and palace of Jerusalem lie vast treasures of gold and silver. I will seize them all and bring the whole land of Judea under my heel!"

Sennacherib sent threatening demands to King Hezekiah, ordering him to pay a heavy tribute of gold and silver. Being a lover of peace, King Hezekiah agreed and sent great stores of gold and silver to keep his borders safe. Yet within Sennacherib's soul, a greedy dragon had awakened and seized control. He wanted still more! He wanted all of King Hezekiah's land and the entire city of Jerusalem.

Seeking counsel, Hezekiah sent messengers to the prophet Isaiah. Isaiah always knew how to meet every peril. Turning toward God in deepest prayer, he gave this reply to the messengers:

"Go back and tell the King: Do not fear the Assyrians. God will stand with us and with the city of Jerusalem."

Returning to the court, the messengers reported to King Hezekiah everything they had heard from Isaiah.

King Sennacherib mustered his troops and marched a mighty army against Jerusalem. Deep in prayer, King Hezekiah awaited the approaching foe. Every city gate was bolted shut. The high walls stood firm. With a vast host, the Assyrians ringed Jerusalem tightly round, hoping to break the city's resistance through hunger and thirst.

Yet in the dead of night, strange peals of thunder suddenly began to rumble, growing fiercer with every moment. Looking up in terror at the sky above the besieged city, the Assyrian warriors beheld the glare of a colossal cloud of fire shooting sparks in all directions and hurling bolts straight down into their camp.

Panic-stricken, many fled—and leading the stampede were the horsemen upon their wild, rearing warhorses. Others collapsed senseless to the ground and lay as still as death. Sennacherib and his generals scattered in headlong flight. The Assyrian campaign ended in utter terror, and not long afterward, Sennacherib was assassinated in his own homeland.

The next morning, once the Assyrian army had fled, Isaiah and King Hezekiah stepped out and spoke to the rescued people:

"Give thanks to the Lord, who has delivered you by a miracle of His mighty power!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Greed contrasted with peace:** Keep your tone sharp and tense when describing Sennacherib and the "greedy dragon"; shift into a calm, steady, grounded voice as Hezekiah and Isaiah pray.
- **Midnight climax:** Quicken your pace as thunder roars and the giant cloud of fire shoots blazing shafts down into the Assyrian camp, then brighten warmly with the morning call to thanksgiving.

### Chapter 13: The Prophecy

Once, the prophet Isaiah stayed out alone in the wilderness. It was the deepest hour of night. As he sat absorbed in prayer, the curtain of darkness suddenly lifted from before his eyes. In the midst of dazzling radiance, a vision of God surrounded by thousands upon thousands of the Heavenly Host appeared before him.

One of the Seraphim soared above him and touched a glowing coal of heavenly fire to Isaiah's lips. At once he received the gift to speak pure and mighty words. Then Isaiah heard the voice of God ask:

"Whom shall I send?"

Isaiah cried out:

"I am ready; send me!"

The word of God poured forth into Isaiah. In the spirit, he gazed far into ages yet to come. He foresaw the complete destruction of the Temple, and he beheld the coming of the Messiah. He proclaimed these things to all the people. These were Isaiah's words:

> *"Behold, a virgin shall bear a son, and his name shall be Emmanuel.*
>
> *A shoot shall spring from the root of David, and the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him.*
>
> *God will come and set you free. Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall hear again.*
>
> *He will destroy the tyrant. Righteousness and faithfulness shall reign once more. Peace shall spread across the earth, and the wolf shall lie down to sleep beside the lamb."*

Thus Isaiah gave his people the unshakable certainty that out of their very own lineage, the Messiah—the Savior—would one day be born.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Wonder in the wilderness:** Speak slowly and softly, letting light dawn gradually out of the dark night until the Seraph touches heavenly fire to Isaiah's lips.
- **Cadence of prophecy:** Read the quoted prophecy in clear, resonant, reverent tones, softening into gentle stillness on the closing image: *"the wolf shall lie down to sleep beside the lamb."*

## Cluster 5: The Prophet Jeremiah

### Chapter 14: The Prophet Jeremiah

When Jehoiakim ruled as king over the land of Judea, he made an alliance with Pharaoh of Egypt. Jehoiakim cared only for making his palace ever more lavish by imitating the Egyptians. Upon many hilltops and down through the valleys, idol worship sprang up once more.

The prophet Jeremiah stood up and condemned these wicked ways:

"You are a blind people! Why do you bow down before strange wooden idols? The Lord of the whole world is always with you when you worship the true God. Those idols are only chopped with an axe from forest trees, plated with silver and gold, and hammered down with nails so they will not wobble! They are no better than scarecrows standing in a field—they cannot speak a word. Men have to carry them about because they cannot walk a single step on their own. But the Lord is the living God! The whole earth trembles before His wrath. He created the earth in His wisdom!"

In the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem, there was a place where idol worship took an unusually cruel form. There stood altars to the god Moloch, upon which people even sacrificed little children, leaving the altar stones stained with human blood. One day, right after such a sacrifice, Jeremiah walked there with a few faithful friends. They carried a large clay jar that the prophet had asked them to bring. Coming up beside Moloch's altar, Jeremiah climbed to a high rock where everyone could see and hear him. His friends lifted the clay jar high into the air right before him.

Full of curiosity, a crowd gathered around and asked:

"What is going on?"

Jeremiah spoke out:

"Hear the word of the Lord, O people of Jerusalem! Disaster shall fall upon you because you have defiled this place with the blood of innocent children and burned them in the fire. Just as I smash this jar into a thousand pieces before your eyes, so shall the city of Jerusalem be shattered!"

Jeremiah seized the clay jar and hurled it down hard, shattering it to bits upon the altar of Moloch. A wave of shock rippled through the crowd, yet not a single person dared lay a hand on the prophet.

Afterward, Jeremiah walked into Jerusalem. Stepping into the courtyard of the Temple, he repeated his prophecy of the city's coming downfall. The chief officer in charge of the Temple overheard him, flew into a rage, and ordered his arrest. Jeremiah was bound and locked in wooden stocks at one of the city gates, exposed to the jeers of everyone who passed by.

The next day, when he was released, Jeremiah withdrew into the wilderness. His friend Baruch wrote down in a scroll the sacred prophecies that Jeremiah spoke aloud. Baruch carried the parchment scroll into the marketplaces and read it out clearly before the crowds. One of the king's attendants snatched the scroll away and brought it to King Jehoiakim. Because it was winter, the king sat warming himself beside a brazier of glowing coals. His chief minister had to read Jeremiah's words aloud for the king to hear. Yet the king turned a deaf ear to the message. He ripped the parchment scroll into scraps and tossed them into the glowing coals.

Not long afterward, Jehoiakim died a wretched death. His successor, King Zedekiah, likewise refused to heed Jeremiah's warnings.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Scornful yet solemn:** Keep a crisp, slightly mocking rhythm as Jeremiah compares the wooden idols to scarecrows in a field, then lift your voice with deep majesty when speaking of the living God.
- **The shattered jar and the torn scroll:** Give sharp, decisive impact to the crash of the clay jar upon Moloch's altar; in contrast, use a cold, indifferent tone as Jehoiakim tears the scroll to pieces and drops them into the coals.

### Chapter 15: Jeremiah Is Thrown into the Cistern

Once again, Jeremiah spoke to the people of the danger hanging over Jerusalem and foretold the city's coming ruin. He prophesied:

"Whoever stays within this city shall perish by the sword, by famine, or by plague. Whoever surrenders to the Chaldeans shall spare his own life. This city is about to fall into the hands of the kings of Babylon."

Several nobles went straight to King Zedekiah and protested:

"This Jeremiah must be put to death! He is sapping the strength and courage of all our soldiers!"

The nobles held great power, so Zedekiah gave in and answered:

"I hand him over to you!"

They seized Jeremiah, tied ropes around him, and lowered him down into an empty cistern in the courtyard of the royal guard near the palace, leaving him there to slowly die. At the bottom there was not a drop of water—only thick, soggy mud.

A high-ranking official of the king's court noticed this cruel deed. Since he had learned nothing but goodness from the prophet, the official hurried to the king and pleaded:

"My lord King, the nobles have acted wickedly toward Jeremiah. He is lying down in the thick mud of the cistern beside the guardhouse, and soon he will starve to death. Please reconsider, Your Majesty—after all, he is a man of God!"

Then Zedekiah gave the command:

"Take three men with you. Pull the prophet out before he dies, and bring him to me through the secret doorway. I wish to speak with him."

The official chose three helpers. They lowered ropes down into the cistern and told Jeremiah to fasten them securely around himself. Once he had done so, they hauled him back up into the light of day.

After Jeremiah had washed away the mud and put on clean garments, the official led him through a hidden underground passage to meet King Zedekiah. Above all else, the nobles must know nothing of it.

Zedekiah asked Jeremiah:

"What is going to happen to Jerusalem? Hide nothing from me! Tell me the whole truth!"

Jeremiah replied:

"O King, if I tell you everything, you will order me put to death. And even if I offer you counsel, you will not listen to me."

At once the king swore a solemn oath:

"No harm shall come to you. I swear I will never hand you back to those who seek your life."

Then at last Jeremiah spoke:

"This has been revealed to me: if you go out willingly and surrender to the King of Babylon, your life will be spared, and the city will not be burned. If you do not surrender, Jerusalem will be destroyed, and you yourself shall die."

Filled with terror, King Zedekiah warned him:

"Let no one learn of our conversation. And if the nobles hear of it and ask you, 'What did you say to the king?' then answer them, 'I only begged permission to remain in the royal guard tower.'"

Jeremiah agreed. Yet he remained in that watchtower for only a short time, before the voice of the Lord commanded him to carry the Ark of the Covenant out of the Temple before the Chaldeans arrived.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **From the muddy cistern to the secret door:** Use a heavy, low voice as Jeremiah is lowered into the wet mud; quicken and brighten the tone as he is pulled back into the daylight.
- **Whispers in a hidden chamber:** Lower your voice into an urgent whisper through the dialogue between the frightened Zedekiah and the steadfast Jeremiah, then turn deeply solemn on the command to rescue the Ark of the Covenant.

### Chapter 16: How Jeremiah Saved the Ark of the Covenant

When King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon marched his armies forth to conquer and lay waste to Jerusalem and plunder the Temple, the prophet Jeremiah received a command from heaven to carry the Ark of the Covenant and the two tablets of the Ten Commandments to a place of safety.

Long, long ago, from the lofty summit of Mount Nebo, God had allowed Moses and his people to gaze out over the land of Canaan at the end of their long wandering through the desert. Afterward, God had called Moses home to Himself. The earth opened gently to receive his body. No one ever found his remains, and so Moses has no grave upon this earth.

To that very place—high upon Mount Nebo—Jeremiah was to bring the Ark of the Covenant and hide it away. When Jeremiah and his companions reached the Temple, they found the whole city in uproar at news of the approaching Babylonian army. People were hurrying to haul provisions inside the city and reinforce the walls and gates. Amid all the commotion, Jeremiah saw that the Temple priests were in despair over what would become of the holy sanctuary if Nebuchadnezzar broke through the defenses and poured into Jerusalem.

Jeremiah gathered the priests together and said:

"It is the Lord's will that we carry the Ark of the Covenant and the Ten Commandments out of Jerusalem to a safe hiding place that God has shown to me!"

No one tried to stop him or those who went with him. When night fell, by the light of flickering torches, he and the priests stepped into the inner sanctuary of the Temple. The holy hall shone softly in the glow of the ten lamps that burned day and night.

Jeremiah drew back the veil before the Holy of Holies. Torchlight caught gleaming golden reflections from the walls and from the two Cherubim statues. Four priests lifted the Ark of the Covenant and bore it outside. Others gathered up many sacred vessels and holy instruments, wrapping them carefully in cloth. Under the glow of their torches, they carried those rescued treasures out through the city gate and over the hills around Jerusalem, heading toward the south.

After a long journey, when Jeremiah and his company climbed to the heights of Mount Nebo, he showed the bearers a hidden cave. They carried the Ark of the Covenant and the sacred vessels inside. Then Jeremiah told them to seal the mouth of the cave tightly with stones, sand, and earth. No one could ever find that place again, unless led there by heavenly guidance.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A quiet link to Moses:** Drop into a reverent, deep cadence when recalling Mount Nebo and Moses's unseen grave from ages past.
- **Carrying the Ark by night:** Keep your voice hushed and purposeful amid the city's panic, letting torchlight shimmer over the golden Cherubim before slowing quietly as the secret cave mouth is sealed.

### Chapter 17: The Babylonians Destroy the Temple

Nebuchadnezzar marched a mighty host against Jerusalem. The Babylonians surrounded the city tightly on all sides so that not a single soul could slip out. In the fourth month of the siege, when famine had already claimed its first victims, King Zedekiah secretly ordered his soldiers to break an opening through the city wall from the inside, hoping to steal away through the breach by night and escape the Babylonians.

The flight took place under cover of darkness. The king and his small band of courtiers slipped right past the sleeping enemy camp. Before long, however, the Babylonians chased after them on foot, then sent horsemen galloping in pursuit and overtook him upon the plains of Jericho. They dragged him off in triumph to Babylon, where not long after, he and his companions met a bitter end.

Through the very gap that King Zedekiah's own men had broken open for their escape, the Babylonians poured in that very night. Clutching weapons and blazing torches, they surged over the crumbled stones into the city. Cries of grief echoed everywhere. Anyone who stood in their path was struck down. Here and there, the invaders hurled torches into houses, and before long even the timbered halls of the king's palace were roaring in flames. The red firelight illuminated a terrible scene of ruin.

Many Israelite soldiers, young and old, rushed to the Temple to defend it. The walls of the Temple courtyards still offered some shelter. Amid the burning city, the Israelites fought desperately to save their sacred House. Young archers climbed up onto the high altar in the outer court. Yet the Babylonians brought scaling ladders and swarmed over the courtyard walls. In the leaping firelight and blinding clouds of thick smoke, it was hard to tell friend from foe.

The Babylonians were determined to plunder the gold and treasures of the Temple. They had strict orders not to set the Temple on fire until everything inside had been stripped bare. As they scaled the innermost wall, right at the entranceway, the Israelites locked their shields together into a living wall to block their path. The young archers atop the altar became clear targets for the enemy. Struck by arrows and spears, many of the youths fell back onto the glowing embers of the sacrificial fire, and their own blood quenched the ancient flame. The defense was broken.

With deafening battle cries, the Chaldeans burst straight into the deepest hall of the Temple, led by their commander Sardapal and soldiers hacking their way forward with drawn swords. The priests had fled inside the sanctuary for refuge. As Sardapal stepped across the threshold, he startled and drew back a pace before the dazzling gleam of gold and the ten burning lamps. Yet only an instant later, his wild swordsmen surged past him, and the unarmed priests were struck down. The burning lampstands toppled over, scorching the robes of the fallen. The desecrated hall sank into darkness.

Before the veil of the Holy of Holies, the High Priest stood with both arms stretched wide to guard it. A soldier leaped forward and drove his sword through him. The High Priest fell backward into the Holy of Holies, where his life ebbed away upon the floor. In a fading whisper, he breathed his final words:

"The Ark of the Covenant is safe! Thank you, Jeremiah!"

When Sardapal looked around, the entire chamber had fallen dead silent. One last small flame flickered uneasily in a fallen lamp, as though ashamed to shed light upon the horror inside the Temple. Quietly Sardapal ordered his soldiers:

"Stand guard before the doors! When daylight comes I will return and direct how the Temple is to be cleared. Until then, let no one set foot inside!"

The next morning, Sardapal returned with King Nebuchadnezzar's royal treasurer. First the bodies of the slain were carried out. Then the treasurer decided what should be done with the enormous spoil of gold, silver, and bronze. He had all the golden vessels and sacred instruments stacked in one pile, and did the same with the silver and the bronze. He had brought along armorers equipped with all their heavy tools, and he ordered them to smash to pieces with hammers the two giant bronze pillars standing before the Temple. They stripped the golden plates from the walls of the Holy of Holies and hammered the two Cherubim statues down into heavy lumps of gold. Next, they broke apart the vast basin of *"the bronze sea,"* which the twelve oxen had held aloft, into jagged chunks of bronze. They kept a number of golden and silver goblets and bowls whole, setting them aside to carry back to Babylon for King Nebuchadnezzar's treasury. Piece by piece, they loaded the Temple's treasures and shattered metal onto wagons and the backs of camels. A long caravan laden with spoils carried it all away to Babylon, into the kingdom of the Chaldeans.

Jeremiah survived the terrible day of Jerusalem's destruction, though his hands too were bound fast in iron chains. By that time, King Nebuchadnezzar had heard of Jeremiah's prophecies. Pleased to think that his victory had come as God's instrument of judgment, the king sent the captain of his guard to speak with Jeremiah:

"The King shows you mercy! If you are willing to come with us and the Israelite captives to Babylon, you shall go free and live under my protection. But if you prefer to stay in this land among the poor farmers whom we are leaving behind, you may stay right here."

Jeremiah answered:

"Let me stay here with my people!"

So Jeremiah was not taken away to Chaldea as a captive. He stayed behind with the farmers and the poor people left in the land, comforting them in the midst of their despair.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Tragic grandeur with gentle restraint:** Build an urgent pace through the burning palace and the defense of the Temple courts, yet keep your tone dignified and restrained—never dwelling harshly on bloodshed for listeners aged 9–10.
- **Inside the Holy of Holies:** Pause clearly when Sardapal halts before the golden glow, then drop to a hushed whisper for the High Priest's dying words: *"The Ark of the Covenant is safe! Thank you, Jeremiah!"*
- **Faithfulness at the close:** After the cold ring of hammers breaking the bronze pillars, shift into a warm, steady voice as Jeremiah asks to remain behind with his struggling people.

## Cluster 6: Daniel

### Chapter 18: Daniel

After conquering Jerusalem and leading many captives back to Babylon, King Nebuchadnezzar gave an order to his chief official:

"From among the noble Jewish families, find me several of the finest and most gifted young men. I want them trained to serve in the royal palace. Two things matter above all to me: a noble bearing and a keen, intelligent mind! Bring them into the palace school alongside the youths of our own land, and teach them our language like true Babylonians. After three years, they must be ready to serve and appear before me."

The chief official made his selection with great care. The young men chosen were Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. They were dressed in fine robes and fed rich meats so that their bodies would grow strong and sturdy. At every meal, they were also given wine to drink so that they might have a bold and fiery spirit, as people believed in those days.

Daniel in particular had a handsome face and a noble bearing. He spoke to his three friends:

"Let us stand firmly side by side. We share the same blood. Drinking wine every day will dull our spirits, and eating rich meats from offerings made to idols will make us sluggish and heavy."

Hananiah answered:

"How can we change anything? We are slaves!"

Daniel replied:

"I will speak with the kitchen steward. He has always been kind to me. Just recently, he even confided in me about some of his worries."

At once, Daniel and his three friends went to see the kitchen steward. Daniel bowed respectfully and said:

"Honored Sir, is something troubling you? It looks as though you have something on your mind. I would like to do something good to help you."

The kitchen steward was greatly surprised. In truth, that very morning the head cook had made him terribly angry. He replied:

"Tell me, Daniel, what good thing is it that you wish to do for me?"

Daniel answered:

"I will sing you a song from my homeland."

Daniel lifted his voice in a lively, joyful song, while his three friends clapped their hands to keep the rhythm. Never before had the kitchen steward been so delighted and entertained. *"What a wonderful young man!"* he thought to himself. Then Daniel stepped closer, took the steward's hand, and kissed it with deep courtesy.

"Sir," he said, "you have it in your power to make all four of us truly happy. We have come from the land of Judah, and our customs are different from those of the Babylonians. When we eat, might we drink plain water instead of wine? And might we eat grains and vegetables instead of rich meats? You see, back in Jerusalem, that is what we have always been used to."

The kitchen steward thought this over deeply. After weighing it for a moment, he answered:

"If you boys grow thin and gaunt, the chief official will punish me."

Daniel pleaded earnestly:

"Sir, I assure you we will never grow gaunt! Please, just let us try it for ten days!"

The kitchen steward replied:

"Very well, we shall try it for ten days. But if after five days you begin to look thin, you must go back to eating the rich meats."

After ten days, Daniel and his friends looked noticeably healthier and stronger than all those who drank wine and ate meat. From that day onward, the kitchen steward no longer hesitated to let them eat and drink as they wished. Now and then, he even asked them to cheer him up with a Hebrew song. In the palace school, the four friends eagerly studied the Chaldean tongue, geometry, and the science of the stars. When three years had passed, all the pupils were brought before King Nebuchadnezzar. Among them all, Daniel and his friends stood out for their noble appearance and deep learning. All four were welcomed into the service of the royal court, and everything went well with them.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Warm-hearted resourcefulness:** Shift away from the heavy mood of the previous chapter into a brighter, warmer tone; give Daniel a confident yet respectful and friendly voice.
- **Joyful clapping rhythm:** Read with lively energy when Daniel sings his homeland folk song and his three friends clap along to delight the kitchen steward, then close gently on a note of bright promise after their three years of study.

### Chapter 19: Nebuchadnezzar's Dream

Around this time, King Nebuchadnezzar dreamed a dream that deeply troubled his heart. The king summoned the wise masters of Babylon and asked:

"What did I dream last night? Tell me what it was, and explain what omen the dream carries."

The wise men replied:

"Your Majesty, no one on earth can tell another person what they have dreamed. Yet if Your Majesty tells us what you saw in your dream, we can interpret its meaning for you."

The king flew into a rage at their evasive answer and commanded the captain of his guard:

"My wise men are utterly useless; put every one of them to death!"

Among them was one of Daniel's own teachers. Daniel loved this teacher dearly, so the moment he heard the king's decree, he ran straight to the captain of the guard and said:

"Please, do not kill the wise men! I am one of their pupils, and tomorrow I will tell the King what he dreamed."

The captain hesitated. Yet Daniel stood so straight and fearless before him, shining with youthful grace and strength, that at last the captain answered:

"I can wait one day."

Before going to sleep, Daniel poured out his whole heart in prayer, asking God to grant him light and understanding. And lo, during the night he dreamed the exact same dream the king had dreamed. Early the next morning, he asked for an audience at the palace. Immediately, Daniel was led into the presence of Nebuchadnezzar.

He began:

"Your Majesty, the dream that came to you was of a colossal statue. The statue's head was made of gold, its chest and two arms of silver, its belly of bronze, and its two legs of iron. Its two feet were made of iron mixed with clay. Suddenly, a stone broke away all by itself from a mountain. It hurtled down, struck the statue's feet, and crushed them to dust. Then that mighty statue collapsed and shattered upon itself. Yet the stone that destroyed the statue grew tall as a great mountain and spread until it filled the entire earth."

When Daniel finished speaking, the king stood rooted to the spot, his face pale; then he cried out with joy:

"Yes, that is it! Yes, that is the very dream!"

The king motioned for the interpreter of dreams to step right up beside him and urged eagerly:

"Come now, Daniel, tell me what this dream means!"

Daniel began to explain:

"Your Majesty, great power and glory have been given to you. You are that head of gold. After you, a lesser kingdom will rise—the kingdom of silver. After that, a third kingdom will emerge—the kingdom of bronze—which will wage many wars and spread across the face of the earth. Following that will come a dark and terrible age with iron rulers bringing warfare everywhere. Then a fifth kingdom will appear, mighty as iron yet brittle as clay. Its destiny is to bring about the downfall of all. Yet the stone from the heights of the heavenly mountains will shatter that earthly kingdom. Then a Kingdom of God will rise upon the earth, enduring forever, and nothing will ever destroy it. God has revealed to King Nebuchadnezzar in a dream what will come to pass in days to come."

Hearing these words, the king fell face down upon the floor, stirred to the very depths of his soul. When he managed to rise to his feet again, he said to Daniel:

"O noble youth! Your God has enlightened you. From this day forth, be my foremost wise man and my friend! From today onward, you shall be the Master of the Wise Men of Babylon."

The king showered Daniel with honors and royal gifts. He declared:

"Name a single wish, and it shall be granted."

Daniel answered:

"Your Majesty, please spare your wise men from punishment, and allow my three friends to serve beside me as your faithful officials, so that we may remain together."

Because the king held Daniel in high affection, he granted both wishes. From that day forward, Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah became inseparable friends.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Rising rhythm of the statue:** Read slowly and distinctly through each material from top to bottom (gold, silver, bronze, iron, and iron mixed with clay) so nine- and ten-year-olds can clearly picture the colossal figure before the stone crashes down.
- **Solemn interpretation:** Keep a calm yet luminous voice when speaking of the Kingdom of God, then soften warmly and tenderly on Daniel's loyal wish for his teacher and three friends.

### Chapter 20: The Three Young Men in the Fiery Furnace

King Nebuchadnezzar grew ever richer and more powerful. One day, the thought entered his heart to raise up a monument to his own might. In the heart of the city of Babylon, the king ordered his servants to set up a golden statue that towered above the rooftops of all the houses in the city. Then the king sent heralds throughout the land to proclaim:

"Every morning, when the blast of the bronze trumpets rings out, all people in the city must bow down to the ground and worship the god of my royal power—this very statue I have raised! Anyone who refuses shall be cast alive into the fiery furnace!"

At that time, Daniel was away in a distant province tending to the king's affairs. His three friends—Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah—all served as royal officials and shared one house together. Daniel himself had a fine home with servants, a gift from the king.

When they heard of the king's new decree, the three friends sat down to talk together:

"Moses gave us the commandment forbidding the worship of idols. We cannot do what the king demands."

The next morning, when the sound of bronze trumpets thundered through the streets, all three young men raised their arms high toward heaven and spoke together:

"The Lord, our God, is great indeed!"

Now there were other officials at court who had long envied these three Hebrew officers. They hurried before Nebuchadnezzar to accuse them:

"Your Majesty, there are three men in the city who defy your command. They scorn your royal decree to bow before your statue. They are Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah!"

A fierce fury blazed up inside Nebuchadnezzar. The king ordered all three to be hurled into the blazing furnace just as he had threatened, declaring that he would go in person to scatter their ashes.

The three young men were seized and led away, and the sentence was carried out at once. The king rode in his golden chariot to watch the execution. Yet when he peered through an opening into the roaring flames of the furnace, he froze in astonishment—for he saw four figures standing upright inside! The fourth bore an unearthly, radiant form. It was an angel who had broken the searing fury of the fire.

Terror gripped the king's heart. Hastily he ordered the furnace doors flung wide open and cried out in a loud voice:

"Hananiah, Mishael, Azariah, come out of there!"

All three stepped out of the fiery furnace completely unharmed; not a single hair upon their heads was even singed. Looking up, the king watched the fourth figure soar high into the heavens until it vanished from sight, and he shouted aloud:

"Blessed be the God who has rescued these men! And I issue this warning: from this day forward, if anyone speaks ill of the God of the Jews, that person's house shall be smashed into a heap of rubble!"

Afterward, the king restored all three young men to their former offices and honors.

Nebuchadnezzar went on to defeat all his enemies among the neighboring nations and built a Babylonian empire swollen with pride and arrogance. Whomever he wished to slay, he had the power to slay; whomever he chose to exalt, he exalted. He ruled with harsh cruelty and contempt for his fellow human beings. Then an evil spirit took hold of the king's soul. His mind broke down, he went mad like a wild beast, and he died in madness. His son Belshazzar succeeded him to the throne, yet before long he too was consumed by the same overweening pride that had destroyed his father.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrast between arrogance and steadfastness:** Deliver the royal decree beside the golden statue in a bold, booming voice; shift to a calm, resolute tone when the three young men recall the commandment of Moses and lift their arms toward God.
- **The miracle inside the furnace:** Slow the pace and build quiet tension as the golden chariot arrives, then open your voice in sheer astonishment when the king peers through the opening to see four figures standing upright and the angel rising into heaven.
- **A quiet, somber close:** Lower your voice gently on Nebuchadnezzar's descent into madness like a wild beast, preparing the transition to Belshazzar's pride.

### Chapter 21: Belshazzar's Feast

One night, Belshazzar threw a lavish banquet in Babylon to entertain the princes and nobles of his realm. More than a thousand guests were invited.

Huge jars of red wine passed endlessly from hand to hand around the banquet tables. They even mixed animal blood into the wine, so that before long the young warriors were shouting, cheering, and behaving wildly. Even the king's concubines joined in; the feast turned into a drunken uproar that echoed across Babylon. And Belshazzar behaved worst of all.

Near midnight, the king summoned the keeper of the treasury and roared in a slurred, drunken voice:

"Go into the treasury and bring out at once the gold and silver goblets and drinking cups that my father looted from the temple in Jerusalem!"

When those sacred vessels—which in days of old had been kept holy inside the Temple of King Solomon—were brought out, Belshazzar ordered them filled to the brim with that blood-mixed wine and shouted out his command:

"Take these Jewish vessels and drink to the health of our Babylonian gods!"

He was the first to snatch up a golden goblet, raise it high, and bellow:

"Jehovah is dead; long live Baal!"

Suddenly, every oil lamp and candle in the great hall flickered wildly; many went out completely. Belshazzar stood frozen in his tracks, the golden goblet still clutched in his hand, staring blankly ahead. His face twisted and drained of color, his eyes riveted to the white wall. The entire banquet hall fell as silent as the grave.

Out of a shadowy cloud, a hand appeared and wrote across the wall in letters of fire: *"Mene, Mene, Tekel U-Parsin."*

The hand vanished, yet the fiery words remained glowing brightly upon the wall. The king was so terrified that his knees knocked together violently. He shrieked in panic:

"Bring my magicians and astrologers here at once!"

The king himself could neither take a single step nor summon the strength to sit back down.

The wise men hurried in; they guessed and argued this way and that, yet not one of them could make sense of the mysterious words upon the wall. Suddenly the Queen Mother—the king's own mother—stepped into the great hall. She spoke to Belshazzar:

"In days past, Daniel explained hidden mysteries to your father. Send for him."

In the dim half-light, many of the warriors and princes invited to the feast quietly slipped out of the hall. The vast room grew darker and emptier with every moment. Only the gold and silver vessels of the Temple still glinted faintly beneath the dying torches, while the writing on the wall blazed with undimmed brilliance. The king collapsed backward into his armchair.

Meanwhile, a swift chariot had already raced to fetch Daniel. When he was ushered into the hall, Belshazzar silently raised one trembling arm—unable to utter a word—and pointed toward the writing on the wall. Daniel lifted his prophetic voice and spoke:

"Belshazzar, you learned nothing from your father's miserable death; there is not a spark of humility in your heart. In your arrogance, you have dared to misuse and defile the sacred vessels of the Temple. You have dared to mock the Lord. Hear now what the divine hand has written: Mene means that your kingdom has come to its end! Tekel declares that it has been weighed in the scales and found terribly wanting! U-Parsin means that your kingdom shall be divided between the Medes and the Persians!"

Filled with righteous indignation, Daniel strode straight out of the royal palace.

That very night, Belshazzar was slain by some of his own soldiers. As he died, the fiery writing faded from the wall.

Not long afterward, the kingdom was conquered by Darius, King of the Medes, who marched in with his armies. Hearing everywhere that a clear prophetic spirit dwelt within Daniel, King Darius appointed him chief over all the officials of the realm.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Turning from uproar to dead silence:** Read the drunken revelry with noisy energy, then cut off abruptly right after *"Long live Baal!"*; drop your voice low and slow as the lamps go out, the cloud gathers, and the hand writes in letters of fire.
- **Articulate every word on the wall:** Pronounce *"Mene, Mene, Tekel U-Parsin"* slowly and distinctly, followed by Daniel's blunt, uncompromising interpretation.
- **The dying feast:** Help listeners picture the gold and silver Temple goblets gleaming faintly beneath the fading torches as the guests steal away into the dark.

### Chapter 22: From Bel to the Tower of Babel

In Babylon, the priests built a temple for the worship of the idol Bel. It was a colossal statue with a wide-open, gaping mouth. The priests proclaimed far and wide:

"Every evening, fine food must be brought into the temple. During the night, the god devours great quantities of roasted mutton and goat meat!"

Now it happened that when the temple was first designed, the priests had cleverly built a secret passage leading straight into the innermost sanctuary. Whenever the temple servants carried in great bronze platters heaped with freshly cooked food, crowds of people gathered round to watch the sight. A priest would step forward and shut the temple doors tight with all manner of mysterious incantations and magical gestures. The key was solemnly placed into the hands of the king's treasurer for safekeeping, and returned only the following morning. When the empty platters were brought out again at dawn, many townspeople marveled at the miracle and flocked to the temple to pray before *"the god who eats."* In this way, the temple drew crowds of visitors day after day.

Yet the truth was that every single night, the seventy priests crept in through the secret tunnel, carried off all that delicious food, and feasted upon it with their families. Some of them even stayed right inside the temple to eat the entire banquet and drain the jars of wine offered to Bel.

Even the king himself did not let a single day go by without coming to offer prayers before the statue of Bel. One day, the king said to Daniel:

"Come with me to the temple of Bel. He is a living god—see how much food he eats!"

Daniel answered:

"I do not worship idols fashioned by human hands. I pray to the living God, who created heaven and earth."

The king was astonished:

"Do you not regard Bel as a living god? Have you not seen how much he eats and drinks every single night?"

Daniel laughed and replied:

"Most High King, do not let yourself be deceived! Bel is made of nothing but clay and bronze, and he has never eaten a single bite in all his existence!"

Hearing this, the king grew angry and immediately summoned the priests, declaring to them:

"If you cannot prove to me that Bel truly eats the offerings, you shall die! Yet if you prove it, then Daniel shall die!"

Daniel answered:

"Your Majesty, let it be done just as you have spoken."

Then the king, Daniel, and the seventy priests stepped into the temple of Bel. The priests spoke up:

"My lord, command your servants to bring the food and set it inside! We shall all step outside so that you may shut the door with your own hands and seal it with your royal signet ring. Early tomorrow morning, when you return, if you find that Bel has not eaten everything, we are ready to die. Otherwise, Daniel must die for spreading lies and false accusations against us."

With that, the priests walked out of the temple. Outside, the king ordered the food to be placed upon the table before the statue of Bel. But Daniel quietly asked a servant to bring a basket of hearth ashes. Right before the king's eyes, Daniel scattered an even layer of ash across the entire floor of the temple. When this was done, both of them stepped outside, and the king himself pressed his signet ring into the wax to seal the temple doors fast.

During the night, the priests crept through their secret underground passage just as they always did, and gobbled up every scrap set out upon the table.

In the pale gray light before dawn the next morning, the king and Daniel returned to the temple. The seal upon the door was completely unbroken. As soon as the doors swung open and the king saw the bare, empty table, he cried out in a loud voice:

"O Bel, great indeed are you, a mighty god!"

Yet Daniel only burst out laughing and said:

"Look down at the floor, my lord, and see all the countless shoeprints of the priests still stamped plainly there!"

Filled with fury, the king ordered the priests arrested at once. Unable to deny the truth any longer, they were forced to lead the king straight to the hidden underground passage through which they crept in and out every night to devour the offerings themselves.

In his wrath, the king sentenced all the deceitful priests to death and placed the entire temple of Bel into Daniel's hands. Daniel ordered the false idol smashed to pieces and tore down the deceitful temple as well.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Playful mystery-story tone:** Tell with a touch of humor as the priests put on their show of magic spells, locking doors and handing over keys, then contrast it right away with them sneaking through the underground tunnel to feast with their wives and children.
- **Daniel's confident laugh:** Bring a bright, hearty ring to Daniel's laughter when he says *"Bel is made of nothing but clay and bronze,"* keeping the mood lively and clever for nine- and ten-year-olds.
- **The moment of exposure:** Pause for a beat after the king's shout of praise in the gray dawn light, then lower your voice playfully as Daniel points down to the ash-covered floor stamped with shoeprints.

### Chapter 23: Daniel in the Lions' Den

One hundred and twenty officials at the royal palace resented having the upright and faithful Daniel placed above them to rule over their affairs. They plotted together:

"When it comes to managing the kingdom, we can find no fault or wrongdoing in him to accuse him of. We will have to use his own religion—a faith that is not Babylonian—as our pretext to destroy him!"

So the officials conspired with the priests to lay a trap against Daniel. They drew up a royal decree that read:

"The highest sovereign in all the land is King Darius. In every prayer or petition spoken aloud, his name must be invoked. Whosoever fails to obey shall be cast into the lions' den."

In Babylon in those days, lions were kept as a symbol of the king's majesty. The beasts were held inside a deep pit, and any enemy of the king who was captured was thrown down to be devoured by the hungry animals.

The priests and officials persuaded King Darius to set his signature to the decree. Although Daniel knew every detail of their plot, he kept up his daily habit of climbing to the upper chamber of his house. There he threw open the windows facing westward—toward Jerusalem—and prayed aloud to God three times every day.

The priests and officials sent spies to lurk outside and listen. The spies reported back that in all his prayers, Daniel had not once called upon the king. At once, the whole band marched before King Darius to denounce him:

"Daniel defies your royal decree and prays only to his foreign god!"

Daniel's enemies had achieved their wish; he was arrested and thrown straight down into the deep pit among the ravenous lions.

Yet King Darius felt the sting of a troubled conscience and passed a long, sleepless night tossing and turning. A strange thought kept turning over in the king's mind: at this very hour, the truth would be revealed as to whether Daniel's God was truly a living God. If so, surely He would reach out to save His servant in the lions' den.

Early the next morning, King Darius hurried out to the mouth of the lions' den. The king wanted to see with his own eyes what had happened to Daniel. Bending down to peer into the deep pit, behold—the king saw Daniel still alive, while the lions lay quietly curled at his feet!

At once the king ordered ropes lowered to haul Daniel up from the pit, and he spoke to him:

"Daniel, you are truly a faithful servant of the living God! How did He rescue you from the lions?"

Daniel replied:

"My God sent an angel to me; the angel shut the lions' jaws tight so that they could do me no harm. Blessed be God!"

The king summoned all those who had falsely accused Daniel and ordered every one of them thrown into the deep pit. Instantly the lions sprang upon them, and not a single one survived. King Darius then repealed every ban against prayer and proclaimed throughout the realm that the God of Daniel was wholly free to be worshiped.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Cadence of conspiracy and faithfulness:** Keep your voice hushed and secretive as the hundred and twenty officials plot together; shift to a serene, resonant tone as Daniel climbs to his upper room three times a day, opens his windows westward toward Jerusalem, and prays.
- **Restless night and dawn miracle:** Slow the pace through King Darius's troubled, sleepless night, then brighten with wonder at the sight of the lions resting peacefully at Daniel's feet.
- **A swift, clear conclusion:** Move crisply through the punishment of the false accusers, keeping a warm, steady emphasis on *"Blessed be God!"*

### Chapter 24: Daniel's Prophecy

When Daniel reached a venerable old age, God revealed to him the future destiny of humankind through mighty prophecies and visions.

In the spirit, he beheld four great beasts rising one after another out of the sea across the course of time: a winged lion, a monster resembling a bear, and a winged leopard. Yet the most terrifying of all was the fourth beast, rising up out of the abyss. It had enormous iron teeth and ten horns, crushing everything beneath its feet.

Despite their terrible power, in the end all four beasts were defeated by the heavenly beings and stripped of all dominion.

High upon the clouds of heaven came a majestic One riding the clouds—who bore the likeness of a Human Being. To Him the Most High granted power to establish a kingdom of eternal light.

Yet before that kingdom could take shape, humanity would have to endure a season of fierce oppression beneath the beasts, and human beings would be tested more severely than ever before. At that very hour, the Archangel Michael would stand forth, becoming the protector and ally fighting alongside humankind against the beasts from the abyss.

Daniel wrote down all these things in full and sealed the book with his own hand.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Solemn prophetic tone:** Read slowly and resonantly when describing the four beasts rising from the sea, especially the fourth beast with its iron teeth and ten horns.
- **Light and trust:** Lift and brighten your voice at the vision of the One bearing the likeness of a Human Being riding upon the clouds and the kingdom of eternal light, then speak the name of the Archangel Michael with quiet assurance before closing softly as Daniel seals the book.

## Cluster 7: Nehemiah

### Chapter 25: Nehemiah

When King Cyrus of Persia conquered the kingdom of Babylon, a Jewish man named Nehemiah lived at the royal court, serving as cupbearer to the king. He possessed a clear mind and a noble bearing, so the king liked keeping him close at hand. After the day Jerusalem fell in ruin, Nehemiah had been marched to Babylon in the long procession of captives. Years later, he held an office of trust within the palace. Yet he often wondered how his brothers and sisters in his distant homeland were faring. Was his younger brother Hanani still alive? How were those left behind in earlier days getting along?

King Cyrus had once granted the exiled Jews in Babylon permission to return home and rebuild the Temple. Yet their strength was exhausted. The Temple walls rose with agonizing slowness. Often there were long stretches of stagnation when no one worked at all. They lacked someone to inspire them.

King Cyrus's successor was Artaxerxes, and Nehemiah served him as well. One day, several travelers arrived from afar at the palace gates. They had come from Jerusalem, and among them was Hanani, Nehemiah's younger brother. How gaunt and worn he looked! His clothes were thin and tattered, and his body was wasted as though from years of hunger. His eyes lit up when his finely dressed older brother threw his arms around him, then sank back into sorrow. Nehemiah asked:

"Brother, tell me everything about what is happening in Jerusalem!"

Hanani answered:

"Poor city, wretched people! The city walls are still riddled with breaches from the days of the siege. The gates lie burned to ashes. The shattered entryways offer no shelter at all. The people there are left constantly at the mercy of marauding bands of robbers. Jerusalem is nothing more than a dead city."

Nehemiah had once known Jerusalem in all its splendor. Hearing these words, he could not hold back his tears. When night fell, he lay awake unable to sleep, his heart heavy with grief. He prayed to God:

"Can there truly be no end to the bitterness and suffering of Jerusalem?"

The next morning at breakfast, as Nehemiah poured wine into the royal cup, the king noticed the sorrowful expression on his cupbearer's face. He asked:

"You look utterly worn out. Have you been weeping? Are you ill, or is your heart burdened with grief?"

Holding back his tears, Nehemiah answered:

"Your Majesty, yesterday I received word from my brother of the plight of my homeland, Jerusalem. The city of my ancestors still lies in rubble and desolation. Oh, if only I might return there with my brother and rebuild the city!"

The king pondered in silence for a moment, then asked:

"How long do you wish to stay there?"

Nehemiah named a span of time, and lo, God softened the king's heart. The king spoke:

"If I send you there, you shall go as my Governor of Jerusalem. Make Jerusalem a fair and beautiful city once more!"

Nehemiah bowed low to the floor before the king and kissed the hem of his royal robe. King Artaxerxes gave him letters to carry on his journey, sealed with the royal seal, naming Nehemiah Governor of Jerusalem. He was granted permission to take timber from the royal forests to build new gates and raise houses anew.

Before long, Nehemiah set out with his brother and companions upon the long road home. The king sent officers and cavalry to escort and protect them, so that he might enter Jerusalem in full dignity and authority as the king's Governor.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrast between two worlds:** Keep a warm, stately tone for the Persian court; when Hanani arrives, lower your voice in gentle sorrow, slowing down on the tattered clothes, gaunt frame, and the words *"Jerusalem is nothing more than a dead city."*
- **Conversation at breakfast:** Hold a quiet restraint of emotion as Nehemiah speaks, then open warmly and generously into King Artaxerxes's decree.

### Chapter 26: The Difficult Rebuilding

Upon arriving in Jerusalem, Nehemiah rested to regain his strength after the arduous journey. On the third evening after his arrival, he began inspecting the city walls of Jerusalem, holding a firm resolve in his heart to rebuild the fallen walls. He gathered together the priests, the nobles and elders, and all the poor folk of the city, and spoke to them:

"You all know the suffering of our city. The neighboring tribes mock you and treat you with violence, because you have nothing with which to defend yourselves. Join hearts and hands to rebuild the city walls and set new gates in place. The king of Babylon has granted me leave to do as I wish; he has even promised timber and beams to rebuild the gates. Come, let each of us lend a hand to lift ruined Jerusalem back to its feet and make it a city worthy of the Temple! The hand of God rests over my head, bringing protection and loving-kindness. God is with us!"

Nehemiah inspired the people with those words. Before long, everyone was bustling about clearing away rubble and stones and setting to work. Nehemiah himself took up a pickaxe and shovel to set an example for the people. Day after day, they mended the breaches, and a new city wall began to rise.

Yet before they could fit the very first gate into place, their enemies came marching in. The neighboring tribes—the Horonites and the Ammonites, together with their leaders—wanted to stop the city from being rebuilt. In the dusk of evening or under cover of night, bands of them crept over to tear down the sections of wall the people had built by day.

From that time onward, only half the people worked on each shift, while the other half stood guard with spears, bows, and arrows to protect the work. Even those hauling heavy stones kept a sword strapped at their side at all times. Along the length of the wall, they stationed men with signal horns. Whenever a suspicious band drew near, the horns blew a sharp blast, and the enemy was driven back. They labored from the first rosy flush of dawn until the evening star appeared. Through the night, armed guards kept watch over the walls.

At last the day came when every breach in the wall was sealed, and only the new gates remained to be hung in place. Everywhere, people saw Nehemiah right in the midst of the workers. Here he swung a hammer or shoveled earth, there he saw to the food and water, and he was always ready to take his turn on watch. All the people loved him dearly; his example poured fresh strength and courage into their hearts.

Then the enemy hatched a cunning new plot: *"If we can capture this Nehemiah and do away with him, the people will lose all heart, and then we can break their spirit!"*

They sent Nehemiah a crafty letter inviting him to come outside the city to meet and talk about building friendly neighborly relations. Nehemiah saw straight through their wicked scheme, so he sent word back that for the present he had no time to leave Jerusalem.

Soon the sturdy gates were built and hung in place, and the city could finally sleep in peace with only a few night watchmen on guard. Nehemiah proclaimed a great day of festival. Ezra the priest read aloud to all the people from the books of the law of Moses. They decked their houses, the city gates, and the Temple with green branches, calling this the Feast of Tabernacles. Everywhere musicians played together, and the people sang and danced joyful dances. Through Nehemiah's deeds, a people who had endured so many trials found new hope, and solemnly vowed that from that day onward they would walk in the ways of the Lord.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Rhythm of labor and vigilance:** Read Nehemiah's rallying call and the work from dawn until the evening star with crisp, resolute energy; lower your voice into a hush when the Horonites and Ammonites creep up under cover of night to tear down the stones.
- **Joyful ending:** Lift into a bright, lively tone when the city gates are hung and everyone sings and dances beneath the green branches of the Feast of Tabernacles.

## Cluster 8: Jonah

### Chapter 27: Jonah

Jonah was the son of Amittai. When he was a young boy, he fell gravely ill and lay upon the very brink of death. The Prophet Elijah restored him to life. From that experience, sincere reverence for the Lord took deep root inside the boy's soul. When Jonah grew to manhood, the Lord chose him as His prophet. This is how the story unfolded.

In the city of Jerusalem, many people were treating the Temple and the sacred commandments with contempt. One night, just as he woke from sleep, Jonah heard a voice speaking to him:

"Go to Jerusalem! Proclaim to the people that calamity is about to strike the city. The city shall be destroyed!"

Hard though it was, Jonah set off for Jerusalem to deliver that fearful prophecy. In many quarters of the city and before the doors of the Temple, he lifted a voice burning with sacred fire to proclaim the word of the Lord.

The people were shaken to the core. They listened, mended their ways, returned to keeping the commandments, and begged for God's grace and forgiveness. And lo, the Lord turned aside His fierce anger. Jerusalem was spared from disaster.

Yet from that time on, people called Jonah *"a false prophet."* Stung with pain by that mockery, he resolved to travel far away to escape the scorn of the world. Yet once again the voice came to him in the night and spoke:

"Arise, Jonah, go to Nineveh, the great city of the Assyrians, and proclaim its destruction, for their wickedness has cried all the way up to Heaven!"

Hearing this command, Jonah felt torn and doubtful: would what happened in Jerusalem repeat itself once more? If the people of Nineveh changed their ways after hearing his prophecy, the city would not be destroyed. Once again people would point and cry: look, the false prophet!

Jonah tried to flee from the sacred command. He ran away to the port city of Joppa, intending to board a ship and sail across the sea to the ends of the earth.

Seeing a sailing ship lying at anchor, he asked the captain:

"Will you let me sail aboard your ship?"

As it happened, the vessel was just about to weigh anchor for distant Tarshish, so he received this reply:

"We are sailing very far away, nearly to the ends of the earth."

Jonah paid his fare at once, and they took him aboard.

Upon the ship were passengers from many nations and distant lands. Less than a single day out at sea, a terrible storm broke loose. The passengers cried out to their gods and begged for their lives. Jonah alone had crept deep down into the lower hold and fallen fast asleep. In sheer panic, the captain came down to him, shook him hard to wake him, and shouted:

"All of us up on deck are trembling for our lives, while you sleep as soundly as the dead! You are a Hebrew! Your God must be mighty indeed. Get up and call upon Him to save us!"

Yet Jonah had just passed through a very strange dream. He answered the captain:

"Throw me into the sea, and the waters will grow calm again!"

Amazed, the captain repeated this to everyone aboard. Thinking the storm had addled Jonah's wits, they were unwilling to do such a thing. They cast cargo and provisions overboard to lighten the ship and strained to steer back toward shore, yet raging winds and towering waves hurled the vessel back out into the furious sea. The ship's wooden ribs groaned and cracked at the joints. The sailors were nearly in despair. Then Jonah came up beside them and called out aloud:

"This storm has risen because of me, for I disobeyed the word of the Lord. Throw me into the sea, and the tempest will pass!"

So they took hold of Jonah and dipped his two feet into the water. The howling wind softened just a little. When they pulled him back aboard, the gale roared fiercer than ever, and a giant fish appeared. Furious waves threatened to swallow the ship whole. At last, they surrendered Jonah to the churning sea. Immediately the storm fell still.

The giant fish swallowed Jonah whole. Inside the fish's belly he lay as though inside a coffin. He lost all use of his earthly senses, yet he did not lose his life. One picture after another drifted before Jonah's inner eye as in a vast, boundless dream. He beheld the earth at the very dawn of Creation. He gazed upon the light of the Garden of Paradise and Noah's Ark. He saw Moses and the people of Israel crossing through the Red Sea. He looked up to the high world above with the Heavenly Host, then gazed down into the underworld with its dark evil spirits. Then the radiance of the Temple of God shone forth, and he was permitted to enter alongside the wise sages and pray with them. In his prayer he cried out:

> *"O Lord, where could I ever flee from You?*
> *You are present in every place.*
> *If I borrow the wings of the rosy dawn*
> *And fly to dwell at the farthest edge of the sea,*
> *Even there You are present!*
> *O Lord, I vow to serve You*
> *And fulfill Your holy will!"*

Then the fish swam toward the shore. After carrying Jonah inside its belly for three days, it cast him out onto dry land. Now Jonah began the long journey to the city of Nineveh. There he meant to proclaim, just as the Lord had commanded, the destruction of the city. This time he undertook the task with a willing heart.

Nineveh was a vast royal capital. In the marketplaces and before the gates of palaces and temples, Jonah lifted his prophetic voice with mighty power. The people listened to him and wept aloud:

"Oh, how dreadful! Woe to us! In forty days disaster will strike us down, all because of our sins!"

The king ordered Jonah brought before him, and Jonah spoke sternly to the ruler. The king was deeply shaken. He tore his royal robes, sprinkled ashes upon his head in mourning, and sent a decree ringing throughout the city:

"Every citizen must fast for three days. Turn away from wicked living and return to doing good. Pray to God that He may show mercy upon us!"

Filled with awe, all the people obeyed the king's decree to the letter and begged God for forgiveness. And the Lord let go of His anger and brought no harm upon the city.

When forty days had passed, Jonah once again feared people would think him a false prophet. That thought weighed heavily upon him. The next night, he had a strange dream. He lay beneath a scorching sun. A lush, broad-leaved plant sprang up tall beside him, spreading cool shade over his head. Then a giant worm crawled up and gnawed the plant until every leaf withered dry. The fierce sun beat straight down upon Jonah. He suffered so bitterly that he thought it would be better to die. Suddenly, the voice in the night spoke to him:

"Jonah, you feel pity even for a withered plant. How much more, then, should I feel pity for a great city filled with so many human beings, rather than see it destroyed! Rejoice together with the repentance of that city's people!"

Awakening from sleep, Jonah bowed his face low to the earth. He felt ashamed that for a passing moment he had let petulant anger cloud his heart. Was it not far better a hundred times over to be called a false prophet than to see an entire city wiped out? And he lifted his voice in praise of divine mercy.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Surging rhythm of the sea storm:** Build tension as the ship's wooden ribs creak and waves threaten to swallow the vessel; the instant Jonah hits the water, soften your voice like a sea suddenly stilled.
- **Vision inside the belly of the fish:** Read slowly, letting each image expand from Creation to the Temple, then let your voice linger warmly and peacefully over the prayer of surrender.
- **The plant and the Lord's voice in the night:** Keep a tender, forgiving warmth when the voice in the night helps Jonah understand the meaning of mercy.

## Cluster 9: Heliodorus

### Chapter 28: Heliodorus

After Jerusalem had lived for a long season in peace, the Temple received many precious offerings. Whenever anyone in Palestine possessed much gold and silver or had carefully saved up money, they trusted the priests inside the Temple to keep their wealth safe. Nowhere was a safer place for safekeeping. Thus great treasures filled the side chambers of the Temple.

The High Priest Onias watched over the wealth entrusted to his care. It rested safely under the protection of the Temple until its owners needed it and came to claim it.

Rumors of the treasure inside the Temple reached the ears of the king of Assyria. One day, he spoke to Heliodorus, a commanding general at his court:

"Go in my name to Jerusalem. Take an army with you. That city must submit to me. Demand that the priests show you the treasure in the Temple. Leave only a small portion of what you find there; bring all the rest back and add it to my personal treasury. My gold and silver have run dry after the recent campaign. The priests will not dare defy my command."

When Heliodorus arrived in Jerusalem, the High Priest Onias received him warmly as the king's envoy. Yet when Heliodorus declared the errand on which he had been sent, Onias was stunned and horrified. He tried to explain the truth of the matter to him:

"Sir Heliodorus, most of the treasure within the Temple belongs to hundreds of our citizens who trusted us to safeguard their savings. If you seize it, hundreds of innocent people will be robbed and ruined."

Heliodorus replied:

"Lead me to the treasury chambers! In the king's name, I must see that treasure with my own eyes!"

Once more, the High Priest Onias and the priests pleaded with Heliodorus not to do this. Word of the danger soon leaked out, and a great outcry of protest rose throughout the city. The priests stood in a circle around the altar of fire, weeping aloud and begging God for help. Yet words, weeping, and prayers seemed all in vain. Heliodorus forced them to lead him to the treasury chambers. With a heavy heart and pale face, the High Priest Onias walked surrounded by grim-faced soldiers.

As Heliodorus and his troop of soldiers followed the High Priest toward the treasury, the entire company was overwhelmed by a mighty, sacred apparition. A burst of supernatural light suddenly flooded over them. The air roared like thunder with the pounding hooves of wild charging horses.

Heliodorus alone beheld a radiant horse bearing a terrifying rider clad in golden armor charging straight down upon him. With its two forehooves, the steed struck him flat to the ground. Horse and rider swept past, followed by two mighty youths of awesome bearing who beat Heliodorus relentlessly with the flats of their swords. He collapsed unconscious and lay motionless as though dead.

His soldiers saw only flashes of lightning and heard the roar of thunder; they saw neither the horse nor the two shining companions. Once the uproar passed, they found Heliodorus stretched out like a corpse upon the stone floor. Trembling with terror, they lifted him up and carried him away. The priests praised the almighty power of God. As the news spread through the city, the people cheered with joy. Songs and dances echoed down every street and lane.

Before long, three soldiers came running breathlessly back to the Temple, begging the High Priest Onias:

"Please save our commander's life! He still has not regained consciousness—he is breathing his very last!"

Onias weighed the situation carefully: if Heliodorus died, the king might think they had murdered him; if he recovered, Heliodorus could bear witness to the boundless power of God. With that in mind, Onias offered a sacrifice asking for Heliodorus's recovery and prayed with deep reverence.

Inside the room where Heliodorus lay, consciousness slowly began to return to him. To his left and to his right, he saw two angelic youths. One of them spoke:

"Give thanks to the High Priest Onias! Because of his prayer, the Lord has given you back your life. Wherever you go, proclaim His divine power!"

With those words, the two vanished. Heliodorus was healed. He went to Onias, thanked him for the gift of his life, and offered sacrifice to God. Once fully recovered, he returned to report to the king. Everywhere he went, he told of the majesty of the Lord revealed in the Temple of Jerusalem. The king asked whether he ought to send someone else to the treasury chambers. Heliodorus answered:

"Send only your very worst enemy there, for a mighty God dwells in that place!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Climax of light and pounding hooves:** Keep the pace tense and urgent as the soldiers force their way toward the treasury, then let your voice ring like thunder when the supernatural light bursts forth and the golden-armored rider charges down.
- **Transformation of the invader:** Lower your voice into a warm, reverent stillness as Onias prays and the two angels appear; deliver Heliodorus's final reply crisply, with a touch of dry humor beneath its solemn awe.

## Cluster 10: Job

### Chapter 29: Job

In the land of Uz lived a man named Job. Day after day, it cost him no struggle to walk in the path of righteousness and shun all evil. He had seven sons and three daughters. He was rich in land as well as herds of cattle and camels, and his children lived together in peace and harmony. He lacked not a single earthly blessing. Every day Job gave thanks to the Lord for all the good gifts in his life.

Then one day, the Heavenly Host came to stand before God for the Council of Heaven. Rising up from below, stepping out of the shadows, came Satan. He too wished to be present. The angels turned their eyes toward God, yet He allowed Satan to draw near, so the angels could not bar his way.

Amid that world of light, Satan stood out like a black stain. The Lord spoke to him:

"Where have you come from? What do you have to say to Me?"

Satan replied:

"I have been roaming to and fro across the earth, and I have noticed that more and more people are following the call of my dark spirits."

The Lord asked:

"Have you not noticed Job, My faithful servant? Nowhere on earth is there anyone like him. He is full of goodness and uprightness. Evil cannot touch him. He pays no heed to your demons."

Satan answered:

"It is not for nothing that Job serves You so loyally. You have showered him with every earthly blessing. Just stretch out Your hand and strike everything he owns. Bring disaster down upon him, and soon enough he will curse You to Your face. That is what human beings are like!"

The Lord spoke:

"Very well, then: all the earthly possessions that Job holds are placed into your hands. Yet his soul you are forbidden to touch!"

Satan answered:

"So be it! I shall manage this well! I will pile calamity upon calamity on his head until his own mouth denies You in sheer despair!"

With that, Satan strode away in triumph. God took counsel with the Heavenly Host concerning the destiny of humankind.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Peaceful beauty of the land of Uz:** Read the opening softly and warmly so children can feel Job's abundant life and quiet daily gratitude.
- **Contrast in the Heavenly Council:** When Satan steps forward like *"a black stain,"* lower your pitch to a sharp, arrogant edge; keep God's voice calm, unhurried, and majestic.

### Chapter 30: The Trial

One day, while praying, Job said:

> *"O Lord, hear the voice of Your servant Job!*
> *How can we stand righteous before Your sight, O Almighty God?*
> *You shake the earth and make its pillars tremble.*
> *You command the sun to rise and set.*
> *You stretch out the heavens like a canopy,*
> *And stir the great ocean with rising and falling tides.*
> *You shaped the stars of the Great Bear high in the sky,*
> *Bright Orion and countless twinkling constellations.*
> *Your splendor and Your loving-kindness, O Most High,*
> *Shine down upon my head.*
> *You let me walk within a goodly house,*
> *And Your grace endures forever."*

No sooner had Job finished his prayer than a messenger came bursting breathlessly into the house, stood before him, and cried:

"Master, forgive me for disturbing the peace of your home!"

Before Job could even greet him, the messenger hurried on:

"A most terrible thing has happened out in the fields! The servants were plowing with the oxen while the donkeys grazed close by. Suddenly, raiders from the land of Saba swooped down and drove away every animal. They struck down the servants with the edge of the sword. I alone escaped to bring you word of this calamity."

He had scarcely spoken his last word when a second messenger came rushing into the house. Catching his breath, he told Job:

"Forgive me, Master, for disturbing the peace of your home! I was out in the pastures with the shepherds tending the sheep. All at once, a giant bolt of blazing fire fell straight from the sky into our midst and struck down both the flocks and the servants alike. I alone escaped alive to tell you."

At that very instant came the sound of a third messenger's footsteps. Gasping for air, he reported:

"Master, forgive me for disturbing the peace of your home. This morning, as usual, we led the herd of camels out to water. Three bands of Chaldean raiders swept in from all sides and drove off every camel. All the herdsmen were cut down by the sword. I alone survived to bring you word of this disaster."

Scarcely had he finished speaking when a fourth messenger dragged his heavy steps inside. He could hardly bring himself to open his mouth. Visibly shaken to the core, he forced out the words:

"Master, how can I bear to stand before you? I come bearing tidings of a dreadful tragedy. Your sons and daughters were eating and drinking together inside the house of your eldest son, your firstborn. Suddenly a fierce windstorm roared up out of the desert. So fierce was the wind that it blew the house crashing down upon all the young people inside and crushed them all to death. I alone survived to bring you this news."

Having delivered those horrifying tidings, the messenger sank down onto his knees and buried his face in his hands.

Job leaped to his feet, tore his garments in anguish, and collapsed to the ground. When he recovered his senses, he lifted his voice in prayer:

> *"Naked I came into the world; naked shall I depart!*
> *O my eyes, all that my eyes once beheld is swept away!*
> *O my heart, all that my heart once loved is blotted out!*
> *The Lord has given,*
> *And the Lord has taken away.*
> *Blessed be the name of the Lord!"*

Satan listened silently to everything inside Job's house, waiting for the moment Job would curse it all. Yet even in the depths of grief over his loss, Job remained humble and faithful.

Then the voice of the Lord challenged Satan:

"Look now! In the midst of such bitter sorrow, Job has not turned away from his faithfulness."

Satan replied:

"A man will gladly trade everything he owns for his earthly life! Lay a heavy hand upon Job's own body—upon his flesh and his very bones—strike him with sickness and plague! Then surely he will curse You straight to Your face."

The Lord spoke:

"So be it! Put him to the test! He is in your power; only spare his life!"

At once Satan covered Job from the crown of his head to the soles of his feet with painful, festering sores. As he lay racked with illness, his wife came up to him and said:

"Job, misery after misery the Lord has poured out upon you. Now He torments your own body with disease. Do you still cling to your faithfulness toward Him? Curse your God and die!"

Job answered:

"You speak like a foolish woman! You have received so many good things from God! Why should we not accept trials in the hour of hardship as well?"

Angrily, his wife walked away. Left alone once more, a crushing weight of sorrow pressed down upon Job. He cried out in the darkness of his room:

"Why did my mother ever give me birth? Why did I not die upon my mother's breast? The hand of fate weighs heavy upon me and crushes me from within and without!"

In the dead of night, Job heard a voice whispering through the darkness:

"Job, deny your God! Curse His name! He has cast you—His faithful servant—into the dust like a worm!"

Instantly Job recognized the voice of the tempter as Satan himself. Boldly he answered back into the night:

"Though the heavens above my head split wide open and the earth beneath my feet gape into an abyss, still I know this: my Redeemer lives, and one day He will awaken me into new life!"

The moment those ringing words resounded, Job heard a tremendous crash, a chaotic roar, and a shriek of terror. Satan had been defeated and plunged headlong into the abyss.

From that day onward, Job began to heal. His brothers, sisters, and friends gathered round him, comforting him after his affliction and bringing him gifts of sheep, cattle, and pure silver. As the years passed, he once more had sons, daughters, and grandchildren. Filled with gratitude, he lived to a ripe old age of more than a hundred years.

The angels in Heaven said to one another:

"When a mortal man so cruelly tried as Job can stand firm on his own against Satan like that, how much more must we, the angels of Heaven, do the same!"

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Rapid rhythm of the four messengers:** Open the stargazing prayer in a wide, luminous voice, then quicken the pace through the first three messengers; when the fourth messenger arrives, drop your tone low, slow, and heavy.
- **Confession of faith in the night:** Whisper Satan's temptation in a chilling hush, then let your voice ring out clear and unwavering on *"Though the heavens above my head split wide open..."*

## Cluster 11: Judas Maccabeus

### Chapter 31: Judas Maccabeus

The mighty King Antiochus of Assyria succeeded in conquering Pharaoh's kingdom of Egypt. Sadly, the small land of Israel lay wedged between the two great empires of Assyria and Egypt. On his homeward march after that grand victory, Antiochus led his army through Palestine into Jerusalem. He plundered the Temple, looted its treasury, and ordered the golden sheets stripped clean off the Temple walls. He tore down the walls of the Temple and put many Jews to the sword. All across Jerusalem echoed weeping and deep grief.

Antiochus built a strong Assyrian fortress in Jerusalem. From there, his soldiers ruled the city and its people with an iron fist. Many fled into the mountains to seek refuge. Antiochus issued a decree proclaiming that throughout his entire empire people might worship only his own royal idols, and stationed armed soldiers to enforce it. Even more people fled Jerusalem for the mountains, hiding inside caves to escape tyranny.

Right in the heart of the city, upon the ruins of the Temple, Antiochus erected a giant statue of his god together with an altar, forcing the people to offer sacrifices upon it. He compelled the Jews to burn every holy book and sacred scroll. In any house where such books remained hidden, everyone living inside was put to death.

In those days lived a priest named Mattathias, who dwelt with his five sons in the mountain town of Modin. Mattathias lamented:

"Alas, woe is me! Was I born only to witness the misery of my people and the ruin of the holy city? My hands are bound, my feet are chained! My tongue has grown silent before the might of Assyria. I sit helplessly watching the holy city be destroyed!"

His sturdy sons spoke up:

"Father, no matter what may happen, we will stand fast with you in our faith in the one true God of our people!"

Soon envoys of King Antiochus arrived in Modin and ordered soldiers to raise an idol and an altar in the middle of the town. The chief envoy summoned Mattathias and said:

"You are the most respected and honored priest in this town; you have many sons and enjoy everyone's esteem. Before all the people, you must offer incense and sacrifice to this god. The king will favor you and grant you great wealth and high honor."

Scarcely had the envoy finished speaking than a Jew whom the envoy had bribed stepped toward the altar to kindle the fire of sacrifice. A fierce blaze of righteous wrath surged through Mattathias. Tall and powerfully built, quick as a flash of lightning he wrenched a sword from the hand of a nearby guard and struck the envoy dead. With a single bound, he leaped upon the man lighting the fire and struck him down right upon the altar. His five sons threw all their strength into overturning the stone altar.

Mattathias shouted aloud for all the townspeople around to hear:

"Whoever keeps the covenant with the Lord our God, follow me! Let us leave this town behind!"

The envoy's soldiers stood frozen in shock, then instantly turned and fled.

Mattathias left Modin with his five sons and a large band of faithful followers, heading up into the mountains. Aging Mattathias knew he no longer had the strength or the years left to wage a long war against the Assyrian armies. He instructed his sons:

"Simon, listen well to your father! Of all my sons, you are the wisest. Your brothers must heed your counsel!"

And to Judas Maccabeus, he said:

"You are strong and a true hero! Take command as leader, and defend your people against these cruel heathens!"

Worn down by sorrow, Mattathias sank down, gave his blessing to his sons, and passed away.

From every corner of the land, the children of Israel and Judah rallied to Judas Maccabeus. They armed themselves and launched a war against the brutal invaders. Wherever they marched, they toppled the pagan altars and fought with fearless courage. Judas Maccabeus swung his sword with the bravery of a lion. Simon offered wise and clear-sighted counsel. The Maccabee brothers always fought at the very front, driving the enemy back in retreat. The strength of the Lord stood with them. Their daring struck terror into the hearts of the Assyrians.

Hearing these tidings, King Antiochus flew into a wild rage. He gathered a vast army and commanded his general Lysiam:

"March against Israel! Wipe out this rebellious people!"

With war elephants, cavalry, and countless foot soldiers, they marched into Palestine.

From a high lookout gazing down at the mighty host advancing toward them, Judas Maccabeus prayed:

"Blessed are You, O Lord, who by the hand of David struck down the giant Goliath. Do not let us fall defeated in this righteous battle!"

After his prayer, they charged down from the heights. Showers of stones rained down relentlessly, throwing the horses and war elephants into wild panic. The Assyrian army fell into such chaos that before the battle had scarcely begun, whole regiments were already fleeing the field. Judas Maccabeus and the people of Israel won a complete victory.

Judas and his brothers consulted together:

"Now let us march to Jerusalem and rebuild the ruined sanctuary!"

They smashed every idol to dust, cleansed and purified the sacred precinct, and restored the Temple. Out of the vast stores of gold and silver captured from the Assyrian camp, they cast new sacred vessels and a new lampstand. When all was ready to rekindle the sacred fire upon the altar before the Temple, Israelites who had fled into the deserts, caves, and mountains streamed home to reunite. For six whole days they celebrated in gladness and offered sacrifices of thanksgiving.

When news reached King Antiochus that his grand army had been shattered, such terror seized him that the thread of his life snapped in two, and he perished like a moth plunging into the fire.

Judas Maccabeus and his brothers still had to drive back raiding bands of foreigners along the borders, yet in Jerusalem the flame upon the altar before the Temple burned brightly day and night once more.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Tension building to eruption in Modin:** Read the burning of the holy scrolls in a heavy, somber voice; then accelerate sharply and decisively the instant Mattathias snatches the sword, overturns the altar, and calls the people to the mountains.
- **Victory and the sacred flame:** Deliver the prayer recalling David and the hail of stones that panicked the war elephants with bold, heroic energy; let your voice glow brightly when the Temple is purified and the lamp burns day and night again.

### Chapter 32: The Alliance with Rome

Judas Maccabeus listened closely to the stories told by Roman merchants:

"Far away across the great sea, Rome has grown into the largest empire upon the earth. The Roman armies on land are beyond counting, and their warships crowd the seas. Rome has conquered kingdoms both small and great and placed them under its protection. Yet they never force any nation to pray to any particular god. The rulers in Rome allow each people to keep its own faith and guard them against attacks from outside."

Hearing this, Judas Maccabeus thought to himself: *"Would not Rome's mighty power be a strong shield of protection for us Israelites? Then the Assyrians would have to leave us in peace!"*

He chose two wise friends well versed in the ways of the world, John and Jason, and sent them across the sea as envoys to Rome to arrange a treaty of friendship.

The Romans welcomed any nation that came seeking their protection. They had the terms of the alliance engraved upon a metal tablet, and then dispatched imperial envoys with attendants and an armed guard to Jerusalem to confirm the friendship between the two peoples. Rome had already defeated the Assyrian armies upon the battlefield. Beneath Rome's shelter, the people of Israel could live in lasting peace.

That is why, in later days in Jerusalem, when the Messiah Jesus Christ appeared, the Roman governor Pontius Pilate stood here ruling as the representative of the Roman Empire.

From that time onward, Judas Maccabeus and his brothers were remembered with deep honor in the heart of the entire nation. They had saved Israel from mighty Assyria and utter ruin. After long, hard battles, they brought peace to their homeland and prepared the way for the coming of the Messiah.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Calm tone opening onto a wide horizon of history:** Keep a steady, clear storyteller's cadence—like a historian—as the merchants describe the Roman Empire and the envoys sail across the sea to engrave the metal tablet.
- **A bridge toward the New Testament:** Slow down gently on the mention of the Roman governor Pontius Pilate and the Messiah Jesus Christ, bringing Book III to a solemn, reverent close.
