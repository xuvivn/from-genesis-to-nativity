# Book V (Epilogue): Seeds of Love in the Winter Night
*(Full Text Edition — Ages 9–10)*

---

## Part I: The Life and Stories of Saint Nicholas of Myra

### Chapter 1: The Boy of Patara and the Morning Bell

The winter-night light of Bethlehem and the quiet carpenter's workshop of Nazareth did not close with the ancient pages of history. That warm flame crossed stretches of desert sand, followed white sails sweeping across the deep blue Mediterranean Sea, and came to a sun-drenched port city named Patara.

The city of Patara lay beside a crystal-clear bay, where rows of white limestone houses leaned back against hillsides covered with olive and fig trees. Every morning, while early mist still clung to the tips of the ship masts, from the little church atop the slope rang out the bronze morning bell. Its deep tone drifted over red-tiled roofs and skimmed across the glittering water where wooden boats began dipping their oars to leave harbor.

In a handsome house overlooking the harbor lived a boy named Nicholas. Nicholas's parents had been merchants for many years and were very wealthy, yet anyone stepping inside their home saw no heaps of gold and silver or costly tapestries hung upon the walls. Their daily meals consisted simply of barley bread, olive oil, a few ripe figs, and grilled sea fish.

Every morning when the first bell of the day rang out, young Nicholas went with his parents down to the wooden pier built out on long pilings to the water's edge. There the air always hummed with vendors' cries, the creak of hemp ropes on windlasses, and the warm, briny scent of the sea. While his father spoke with the shipmasters, the boy stood quietly on a stone step looking round. He noticed very quickly who was burdened with worry: a sailor's wife in a worn cloak gazing endlessly toward the horizon where her husband's sail had not yet appeared, or a thin orphan boy huddled shivering beside a pile of old nets, his bare feet purple from the morning breeze.

One chilly morning, as his mother packed a basket of freshly baked bread to carry down to the fishing quarter, Nicholas asked if he might bring along the thick woolen coat that had just been sewn for him. Reaching the far end of the pier, he saw the net-mending boy shivering behind a stone wall out of the wind, yet Nicholas did not call out to him. He waited until his friend turned to watch a flock of seagulls swooping down over the waves, then silently laid the warm coat and two loaves of bread right beside a coil of rope, and slipped briskly around the corner of the wall before the boy could turn back around.

That quiet habit grew up with Nicholas through season after season of sun and wind. Whenever he noticed a widow's roof leaking, by next morning bundles of dry reeds and sound timber lay stacked neatly before her porch; whenever the boatbuilder's woodbasket scraped bottom, opening the door at first light revealed a neatly tied bundle of firewood resting right upon his doorstep. Nicholas never stayed behind to wait for a word of thanks. He wanted only to watch someone breathe a sigh of relief without having to bow their head in debt to anyone.

Then one sorrowful autumn descended upon Patara. A ship arriving from distant lands brought a fierce plague. Within only a few weeks, the sickness claimed the lives of many good townsfolk, among them both Nicholas's father and his mother. On the day he accompanied the two dearest people in his life up the olive-planted hillside, the young man walked back into the spacious stone house that now stood silent and bare. Sea wind swept through the empty corridor, making the oil lamp by the doorway flicker and sway.

That night, Nicholas unlatched the door to the storeroom at the back of the house. Beneath yellow candlelight casting shadows across the stone walls, iron-bound chests of cedar wood stood quietly in a row. He lifted the lid of one large chest. Inside, it brimmed with gleaming gold and silver coins that his parents had gathered through long years of trading across the sea.

Nicholas reached in and scooped up a handful of gold coins. In the candlelight, the golden faces gleamed handsomely, yet resting inside his palm, they felt icy cold as pebbles picked from a winter stream bed. He lifted his gaze through the small window: at the foot of the hill, cold wind was blowing through the fishing quarter, where many roofs sat pitch dark without a single hearth-fire burning.

Quietly he set the handful of gold down beside the base of the candle. Locked tightly inside a chest, gold stays forever nothing but cold metal; only when passed into hungry, freezing hands does it grow warm as a glowing ember in the dead of winter night. Thinking this, Nicholas drew his cloak closer and quietly unlatched the cedar chest for the mornings to come.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A gentle bridging rhythm:** Read the opening slowly, like a bridge carrying listeners from the workshop of Nazareth to the sunlit harbor of Patara and its ringing morning bell.
- **Quiet, unassuming detail:** When telling how Nicholas leaves the warm coat beside the coil of rope and slips out of sight, keep your voice light and natural, avoiding any tone of boasting.
- **Stillness beside the chests of gold:** Lower your voice into a quiet hush when describing the empty house after the funeral and the icy chill of the gold coins in Nicholas's palm.

### Chapter 2: Three Bags of Gold Through the Midnight Window

Not far from Nicholas's stone house stood a small cottage tucked away in a narrow lane leading down to the harbor. Its owner had once been well-to-do, owning an olive grove and two wooden boats that carried oil for sale along the coast. Yet sea storms sank his vessels, and poor harvests followed one after another until he was ruined and left penniless. His wife having passed away early, he was left with three gentle, dutiful daughters already grown to womanhood.

Inside that house with its peeling whitewashed walls, supper often held nothing more than thin porridge and a few salted olives. By day the three sisters took in nets to mend for hire; by night they sat beneath a sputtering oil lamp weaving coarse cloth to help their father earn a few copper coins. Yet according to the custom of that day, every young woman entering marriage needed a modest dowry; without a single silver coin left in the house, the father faced the heartbreaking choice of selling his daughters into servitude to rich households simply to keep them alive.

One late autumn evening, as the sea wind blew biting cold, Nicholas wrapped his cloak round him for a walk after evening prayers. Passing down the narrow lane, he halted before the half-shut wooden window of his neighbor's house. Inside, the hearth had gone out long ago, holding only cold gray ash. Beneath the dim flicker of a candle stub nearly burned down, the gaunt father sat on a stool burying his face in his hands, his shoulders shaking with suppressed sobs for fear of waking his daughters.

"Oh heaven help us..." he whispered through his fingers. "What can we pay our debts with tomorrow? Must I really lead my own children away one by one to spend their lives as servants..."

Standing outside in the dark, Nicholas felt his heart tighten. He glanced across the chilly kitchen. Upon a wooden rod beside the fireplace, several wet woolen stockings freshly washed in well water hung dangling to dry in time for morning.

Nicholas slipped quietly home, took strong linen cloth, wrapped up a bundle of gold coins, and tied it fast with hemp twine. Waiting until the night watchman's footsteps had passed and the whole neighborhood slept deeply beneath the new moon, he pulled a dark cloak up over his head and tiptoed along the stone walls back down the deserted lane. Hearing steady breathing from the inner room, he rose on tiptoe, reached his hand through the window crack, and softly let the pouch drop.

The bag of gold flew lightly through the dark and landed with a soft *"plop"*, dropping straight into the hollow of the first woolen stocking hanging beside the still-warm hearth. Pulling his cloak close, he vanished swiftly down the dark alley before a gust of wind could rattle the door.

Next morning, the whole lane rang with cries of joy mixed with choked tears. The eldest daughter touched her dry stocking and felt it heavy as lead. When her trembling father tipped the stocking upside down onto the wooden table, gold coins tumbled out glittering in the morning sun! He sank to his knees upon the floor, unable to imagine what miracle had visited his poor roof. With that bag of gold, he paid off every debt and gave his eldest daughter a warm wedding to a gentle young boatbuilder in the neighborhood.

A few nights later, a second bag of gold flew silently through the window, landing squarely inside the second stocking to rescue the middle sister from hardship.

When it came to the youngest daughter's turn, the father would sit still no longer. Night after night he spread a mat in a dark corner beside the fireplace, keeping his eyes fixed on the window, determined to catch a glimpse of the nameless benefactor.

On the third night, beneath bright moonlight, the dark-cloaked shadow drew close to the window once more. No sooner had the third bag of gold landed *"plop"* inside the final stocking than the father sprang up at once, flung the wooden door wide, and dashed out into the lane! He caught up, seized the hem of the cloak in the pale moonlight, and sank to his knees at the young man's feet, tears wetting his silver beard.

"Oh, young Nicholas! Was it truly you? How can I ever repay you for saving my whole household..."

Nicholas hurried to stoop down and lift the elderly father up by the shoulders from the cold stone paving. He smiled gently, then pressed one finger softly to his lips for silence:

"Stand up, sir, or the girls will wake. Please keep this hidden and tell no one, not even your own daughters. Let the joy of giving stay completely peaceful, with no one owing thanks and no one looking for praise."

The father nodded through his tears, standing to watch the dark-cloaked figure disappear up the hillside slope. From that night on, woolen stockings hung beside the fireplace became that little family's warmest secret whenever winter winds returned.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **A hush of secretive suspense:** Lower your voice softly and keep an even pace as Nicholas creeps along the walls by night, gently highlighting the soft *"plop"* of the bag of gold into the stocking.
- **Contrast of dark and light:** Give the cold-ash hearth a slow, subdued tone so that when morning sunlight shines down on the gold-strewn wooden table, your voice brightens warmly with relief.
- **Finger pressed to the lips:** Read Nicholas's parting request slowly, with friendly warmth rather than a lecturing tone.

### Chapter 3: The Distant Voyage and the Storm on the Great Sea

After setting aside most of his inheritance to establish a fund for orphans and poor sailors' families in Patara, Nicholas felt a long-cherished wish stirring within him. He wanted to make a distant sea voyage southeastward to the ancient land of Jerusalem, to walk with his own eyes upon the very paths where the winter-night story and the little carpenter's shop had unfolded long ago.

One fair-wind morning, Nicholas packed a linen satchel, a heavy woolen cloak, and a little dried bread, and stepped aboard a cedar-wood merchant ship just spreading wide its white sails. The vessel glided smoothly out past the mouth of Patara bay, leaving behind the white stone houses and the familiar bell tower.

The first days upon the Mediterranean Sea passed calmly. Golden sunlight poured like honey across deep blue waves. Pods of gray dolphins leaped beside the bow from time to time, sending up white spray before diving deep once more. By day, Nicholas helped the sailors coil ropes, mend torn nets, and share dried figs with the apprentice ship's boys. By night, beneath a sky crowded thick with stars, the whole crew gathered round the mast to listen as he told gentle old stories until they drifted off to sleep.

On the fourth day, when nothing lay in any direction save dark deep water, the sun was suddenly swallowed by heavy banks of black cloud. A steady breeze abruptly shifted into a fierce gale, slamming the sails sideways until the entire hull heeled hard. Lightning tore dazzlingly across a bruised purple sky, followed by thunder booming like crumbling mountains.

The tempest broke so swiftly the crew had no time to prepare. Waves reared like walls of black water, crashing over the rail to thunder down across the deck. A sharp, dry *"crack"* split the air: the main yardarm holding the great sail snapped clean in two under the gale, and the torn canvas whipped wildly amid stinging rain.

"We're done for! The ship is going to sink!" shouted the helmsman, his voice nearly lost in the shrieking wind.

Weather-beaten seamen sank down beside the bulwarks, faces white with despair. Down inside the hold, cargo casks banged thunderously together while seawater flooded across the planks.

Amid the pitching deck, Nicholas did not panic. He stepped to the foot of the mainmast, knelt down on the drenched boards, and locked his fingers tight. Amid the roar of furious waves, he prayed calmly, asking the Creator to shelter honest seafarers working hard to earn their bread upon the waters.

Having prayed, Nicholas stood up, quickly knotted one end of a stout rope round his waist, and called out clearly:

"Brothers! Grip the anchor lines tight and hold fast to the helm—do not let go!"

Hearing that steady voice, the sailors roused as though filled with new strength and rushed together to haul the ropes and keep the bow from turning broadside to the towering waves. At that very instant, a mountainous wave slammed across the deck, rearing the ship almost straight upright. High up on the mast, a young sailor struggling to undo a tangled knot lost his footing on the slick timber and plunged straight down toward the side of the ship!

Everyone screamed in terror. Without time to think, Nicholas lunged across the slippery boards, braced both feet firmly against a wooden cleat, and flung wide his strong arms to catch him. In a split second, his hands seized the falling boy by the cloak shoulders and belt, yanking him safely back onto the deck. Gasping for breath, the youth leaned trembling against the mast with tears brimming in his eyes.

Immediately after that moment, a wondrous thing happened. The piercing shriek of the wind softened beat by beat, as though an unseen hand had just closed the door of the storm. Dense black clouds overhead slowly split in two, revealing a clear night sky sparkling bright with stars. The sea raged no longer, subsiding into gentle swells lapping softly against the wooden hull.

The entire crew stared upward in amazement at the starry heavens, then turned to look at the young traveler still quietly untying the rope from round his waist. They patched the torn sail as best they could and steered straight onward by starlight to drop anchor safe in port.

From that voyage on, whenever they put in at any harbor along the Mediterranean shore, sailors told one another the story of the young man from Patara whose arms had caught a falling boy mid-tempest and whose prayer had calmed the waves. And for countless generations thereafter, seafarers weighing anchor spoke Nicholas's name as a faithful companion protecting every ship upon the great sea.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Rising and subsiding waves:** Tell the departure calmly; as the storm strikes, pick up the pace moderately, emphasizing the sharp *"crack"* of the snapping yardarm and the howling wind.
- **Catching the falling sailor:** Keep the tempo brisk as the boy slips from the mast, then release into a steady, firm cadence as Nicholas's hands catch him tight.
- **Starry sky after the storm:** When the clouds part, soften your voice into serene brightness so listeners feel peace returning across the water.

### Chapter 4: The Church Door of Myra at Dawn

Leaving the holy land of Jerusalem after many months of pilgrimage through dry hills and quiet villages, Nicholas set out northward for home. Following the rugged limestone coast, he stopped at the port city of Myra—an ancient town resting at the foot of tall mountains, where cool streams poured down from the peaks through green valleys before meeting the open sea.

As Nicholas walked through the gray stone city gate in the late afternoon, all of Myra lay wrapped in an unusually quiet air. Out at the harbor, dockworkers spoke in lowered voices, while before the great church in the center of town, groups of young and old stood gathered with anxious faces. The revered Bishop who had cared for the city's poor for decades had passed away a few days before, and the town had not yet found a worthy successor.

Throughout that night, inside the church lit only by a few flickering oil lamps, the council of elders kept vigil together. They conferred for a long time, naming one learned scholar after another and one nobleman after another, yet no one's heart felt settled. Who had patience enough to hear out the troubles of poor boatmen? Who had courage enough to stand up and shield widows and orphans when officials grew oppressive?

Near daybreak, as a chilly sea breeze began slipping through the high stone windows, the oldest elder stood up after a long silent prayer. He looked round the wooden table and spoke slowly:

"Brothers, let us stop choosing by our own lights. Let us wait for one simple sign: tomorrow morning just as dawn breaks, the first humble man named Nicholas who crosses the church doorway to pray—that man is the spiritual father our city has been waiting for."

The whole council of elders nodded together. They sat silently, keeping their eyes turned toward the two heavy oak doors at the main entrance.

Outside, the eastern sky began to glow a soft rose pink. Fishing boats just returned from hauling nets lay resting quietly on the sand. Along the cobbled street still damp with night dew, a traveler walked quietly up. Wrapped in a warm, dark-red woolen cloak worn by road wind and dust, an old cloth bag slung from his shoulder, Nicholas had come to the church to offer morning prayer before continuing home toward Patara.

Lightly he pushed open the heavy oak door and stepped inside. The delicate scent of beeswax and frankincense mingling with the crisp morning air slowed his footsteps at once.

The moment they saw a stranger cross the threshold, two elders hurried out from the wooden pews, bowed deeply, and asked softly:

"Brother traveler, may we ask your name?"

Nicholas was slightly surprised by so solemn a greeting at dawn. He bowed his head and answered very quietly:

"I am Nicholas, a traveler from Patara who arrived in the city last evening."

Hearing that very name spoken inside the silent sanctuary, the elders' eyes shone bright. The eldest among them stepped forward and placed into Nicholas's hands a simply carved shepherd's staff along with solemn robes of office, earnestly begging him to remain as Bishop guiding the seaside city of Myra.

Nicholas took a step back, his eyes wide with amazement:

"Oh, honored elders, surely there has been some mistake! I am still young, an ordinary person who has never had charge of any flock. Please choose an older, wiser man than I..."

Yet the elders stood still, waiting. At that very instant, the first sunlight of dawn poured straight through the wide-open doorway. Outside on the stone steps, the people of Myra had heard the news and quietly assembled until they filled the little courtyard—mothers holding babies, boatbuilders with pine resin still clinging to their hands, old folk leaning on staffs. No one shouted or cheered loudly; everyone simply lifted trusting, hopeful eyes toward the young traveler in the dark-red cloak standing framed inside the sunlit door.

Gazing into hundreds of gentle eyes longing for a steady support, Nicholas understood that he could no longer turn round and walk away. He drew a deep breath of the sea air blowing across the steps, then bowed his head and closed his hands around the warm wooden shepherd's staff.

From that dawn onward, Nicholas stayed in the city of Myra, beginning a life of tireless service in the heart of the city by the sea.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Dawn vigil expectancy:** Read slowly and softly while the elders keep watch through the night inside the church, building quiet solemnity before the oak doors open.
- **Nicholas's humility:** Read *"I am Nicholas, a traveler from Patara..."* very lightly and simply, contrasting with the elders' joyful excitement.
- **The sunlit doorway:** Open your voice brighter and warmer as morning sunlight pours across the stone steps and the people of Myra appear behind.

### Chapter 5: The Miracle of the Grain Ships in Time of Famine

A few years after Nicholas received the shepherd's staff in Myra, a severe drought struck unexpectedly across the whole land of Lycia. Spring brought not a single rain shower; by summer, the sun hung like a cauldron of fire in a parched sky; by autumn, every mountain stream pouring down into the valleys had dried down to bare white stones.

Fields around the city split apart in deep cracks. Olive trees dropped their unripe fruit shriveled dry, and the wheat could scarcely form grain. The city's reserve granaries soon reached the last layer of dust at the bottom of the storage jars. In the harbor quarters, hearth smoke grew thinner every evening. Mothers stared into pots of watery gruel and turned away to hide their tears, while small children who usually raced along the wooden piers now sat leaning quietly against stone walls so hunger would not wear them out so fast.

Bishop Nicholas gave out all the barley and silver in the church to large families and frail elders, yet sack after sack emptied against a famine stretching on month after month. Evening after evening, wrapped in his frayed dark-red cloak, he walked down to the wooden pier and gazed out over the deep blue bay, longing for a blessing from across the open sea.

One late afternoon, as a headwind blew from the north, broad square sails suddenly appeared on the horizon heading into the bay. An immense imperial merchant fleet was sailing laden high with golden wheat from Egypt bound for the capital of Constantinople. Caught by fierce headwinds off the headland, the elderly captain brought the entire fleet into Myra harbor to drop anchor and wait out the gale.

Hearing that grain ships had arrived, the townspeople hurried down to the seawall, only to be stopped by guards outside the gangplanks. Only Bishop Nicholas walked calmly forward to ask word with the elderly captain.

Standing upon the creaking wooden pier, he pointed toward the rows of gaunt folk behind the stone wall and pleaded earnestly:

"Kind-hearted captain, the people of Myra are starving in dire straits. Please take pity and spare us a share of wheat from each ship to help our old people and little children survive this hardship."

The weathered captain sighed, stroked his graying beard, and shook his head with regret:

"Bishop, seeing your townsfolk like this breaks my heart as well. Yet every sack below deck was strictly weighed down to the ounce by Egyptian officials and sealed in the ledgers. When we reach Constantinople, the imperial warehouse master will dump every sack onto the great scales. Should even one handful of grain be missing, my whole crew and I will answer with our lives!"

A heavy gloom fell over the pier. Yet Nicholas looked straight into the captain's anxious eyes, his voice calm and firm:

"Open your holds and save these starving people. I pledge my honor and my whole faith that when you reach the capital, the emperor's wheat will not be short by a single ounce."

The steady gaze of the red-cloaked Bishop melted the fear out of the old captain's heart. Turning round, he swung his hand decisively:

"Open the hatches! Haul wheat ashore to help the townsfolk of Myra!"

Cheers erupted all along the quay. Strong sailors rolled up their sleeves and carried sack after sack of golden wheat down the gangplanks, passing them from hand to hand to the townspeople. That grain fed all of Myra through two bitter winters, with baskets of plump seed-wheat still left over for spring planting besides.

A few days later, fair winds returned, and the fleet weighed anchor toward Constantinople. All along the voyage, many young sailors still worried in secret about the moment they would stand before the imperial warehouse master.

The day the ships docked at the capital, the entire crew stood lined up along the stone quay, holding their breath as officials poured sack after sack of wheat into the massive bronze-and-oak weighing pans. The whole wharf fell dead silent. Every eye stayed fixed on the trembling needle of the balance beam.

One second... two seconds... then the iron needle stopped dead still upon the exact center mark of the scale, balanced so flawlessly it seemed not a single grain had ever left the ship's holds!

The elderly captain dropped his cloth cap onto the stones, gazed southward toward little Myra resting beside the sea, and wept for joy behind a radiant smile.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Famine at the opening:** Read the cracked fields, watery gruel, and silent children sitting against the walls in a slow, slightly dry tone.
- **Unshakable pledge:** Speak Nicholas's promise to the captain clearly and distinctly, conveying complete trustworthiness.
- **The scales at Constantinople:** Slow right down at the moment the pointer needle sways and settles dead still on the mark, pausing a light beat before the old captain's tears of relief.

### Chapter 6: Hands Staying the Sword and Three Innocent Men

In the peaceful outskirts west of Myra lived three gentle men who tended small olive orchards and grew barley along the hillside all year round. Every morning they drove donkey carts carrying baskets of fresh greens and pressed oil into the early market, returning each evening to their wives and children beneath red-tiled roofs.

Yet those thriving green orchards caught the greedy eye of the local magistrate. Wanting to seize the honest men's fields without paying a penny, he pocketed a purse of silver as a bribe from a wicked schemer and falsely accused all three men of treason against the crown. Without letting them utter a single word in defense, guards bound all three tightly with hemp rope and marched them straight out to the dusty open ground used as an execution place outside the city gate.

That morning, inside the shady churchyard, Bishop Nicholas had rolled up the sleeves of his dark-red cloak and was ladling hot bean porridge into wooden bowls for the city's frail elderly. Suddenly, from down the lane, a small boy burst breathlessly into the yard, bare feet dusty, chest heaving for air. The boy clutched tightly at Nicholas's cloak hem, pointed toward the city gate, and cried out between gasps:

"Bishop! Hurry... hurry out to the west gate! The wicked magistrate has bound the three olive growers! The executioner is about to swing his sword!"

The wooden ladle froze in Nicholas's hand. Without a word, he set the ladle straight onto the rim of the pot, gathered both sides of his dark-red cloak up in his hands, and broke into a swift run out through the courtyard gate. He sprinted through the bustling morning market loud with haggling voices, weaving past cloth stalls, baskets of fresh fish, and rows of earthenware jars stacked close together, his rapid footsteps drumming across the cobblestones. Seeing the red-cloaked Bishop racing urgently toward the west gate, the townsfolk abandoned their market baskets and surged along behind him.

Out on the dusty execution ground, a tight ring of soldiers clutching spears stood packed round. The three innocent men, blindfolded with rough cloth, knelt silently upon the dry earth, their necks bared to the chilly wind. Seated in an armchair beneath a canopy, the corrupt magistrate folded his arms and gave a cold nod. A broad-shouldered executioner stepped forward, gripped the hilt of a heavy iron sword with both hands, and lifted it straight overhead, the steel blade flashing blindingly in the morning sun.

At the very instant the blade was about to fall, a thunderous cry tore through the silence:

"Stay your hand!"

Bishop Nicholas parted the wall of soldiers' spears and strode straight into the center of the ring. Without flinching before the cold steel, he stepped right up beside the executioner, reached out both strong hands—the very hands that had hauled sail lines in sea storms and carried grain sacks to feed the starving—clamped firmly onto the back of the iron blade, and pressed it hard down to the ground!

The entire execution ground froze dead silent. Trembling from head to foot, the executioner let go of the sword hilt and dropped to one knee in the dust. The spear-bearing soldiers glanced at one another in bewilderment, not one daring to step forward and touch the Bishop's red cloak.

Still keeping his hands upon the blade, Nicholas turned straight toward the canopy where the corrupt magistrate sat stiff as stone. His voice rang out calm yet clear as a bronze bell across a crowded square:

"My lord magistrate, before all these citizens of Myra standing here, state clearly what treason these three gentle gardeners have committed! Or is it their olive orchards and the bag of silver hidden beneath your roof that accuse them in their stead?"

Hearing his secret bribe of silver named right out loud, the magistrate's face turned pale as hearth ash. Looking round, he saw hundreds of angry eyes from the people of Myra fixed hard upon him; his lips quivered without forming a single word of denial. His legs shook violently, and all at once he sprang to his feet, stammered out before the crowd a full confession of taking a bribe to frame the innocent, and hurriedly ordered the guards to unbind all three men immediately.

Cheers broke out across the open ground outside the city gate. Freed from their blindfolds, the three men clasped their wives and children in tears of joy and relief. When everyone turned round to bow in gratitude, Bishop Nicholas was already quietly brushing dust from the sleeves of his red cloak, stepping back through the crowd toward the churchyard and his still-warm pot of bean porridge.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Urgent pace through the marketplace:** Tell the boy's breathless arrival and Nicholas gathering up his cloak to run through the market briskly and decisively, letting listeners feel every second counting down.
- **The moment the sword is stayed:** Pause for a full beat right after the shout *"Stay your hand!"*, then speak firmly and strongly as Nicholas grips the iron blade and forces it down to the earth.
- **Calm moral courage:** Keep Nicholas's voice when confronting the corrupt magistrate clear and steady as stone—never shouting in anger—then soften lightly as he returns to the pot of porridge.

### Chapter 7: Little Shoes by the Hearth on a Cold Wind Night

Autumn in Myra passed quickly. Winds blowing down from the northern sea carried a sharp chill threading through the winding cobbled alleys. By evening, olive-wood smoke rose from low chimneys, while behind tightly closed wooden doors shone only the dim flicker of small oil lamps.

On those biting late-autumn and early-winter nights, inside the warm kitchen of the church, Bishop Nicholas often prepared with his own hands a wide-mouthed woven wicker basket. Carefully he layered ripe red apples picked from mountain orchards at the bottom of the basket, wrapped handfuls of freshly roasted, fragrant chestnuts still warm in clean linen cloths, and tucked in golden honey-glazed cakes baked with the help of neighborhood widows. At the very top of the basket, he arranged neatly several pairs of tiny leather shoes stitched with sturdy thread—gifts the cobbler down by the harbor had quietly sewn especially for poor children.

Waiting until lamps had been lit along the streets and the town had settled into quiet sleep, Nicholas put on his thick dark-red cloak, lifted the heavy wicker basket, and stepped out into the chilly wind. He did not walk along the wide avenues, turning instead down damp thatched-roof lanes where families of sailors lost at sea and widowed mothers scraped by to raise young children.

His footsteps upon the damp stones fell as softly as dry leaves touching the earth. Pausing before a cottage where a child coughed softly in sleep, he stood listening a moment, then gently laid a pair of little leather shoes, a red apple, a honey cake, and a few roasted chestnuts right beside the doorstep—or slipped the gifts deftly inside a child's worn old shoe left out on the porch—and walked quietly on to the neighboring cottage.

One frosty night when salt mist hung thick, the sailmaker's little boy could not sleep for the wind whistling through cracks in the wooden boards. Hearing the faintest rustle out on the porch, he crept softly out of bed and pressed his eye to a narrow gap between two planks of the door.

Through pale silver mist drifting up from the sea, he saw the broad back of someone wearing a dark-red cloak kneeling beside the stone step of his own home. Just after setting a honey cake and a pair of new shoes beside the boy's old sandals, the figure paused on spotting a scrawny stray kitten curled shivering against the cold corner wall. The red-cloaked walker gently unwound his own woolen scarf, wrapped it tenderly around the little kitten's back, and only then stood up to glide softly into the night mist and disappear at the head of the lane.

Next morning, hugging his new shoes still fragrant with honey and chestnuts, the boy ran down to the wooden pier to whisper the tale to all his friends in the neighborhood. From that night on, children across Myra told one another that whenever quiet footsteps drifted past outside on bitter winter nights, even without ever seeing the walker's face, every child's heart felt strangely warm inside.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Sensory winter details:** Read slowly and warmly when describing Nicholas packing ripe apples, roasted chestnuts, honey cakes, and tiny leather shoes so listeners almost smell the warmth of the kitchen.
- **Footsteps in the mist:** Drop your voice softly like a whisper as Nicholas moves down the thatched lanes and as the boy peeks through the crack in the wooden door.
- **The moment with the stray kitten:** Keep a tender, affectionate tone for the gesture of wrapping the woolen scarf round the kitten before fading into the white mist.

### Chapter 8: Three Generals in the Capital's Dungeon

A few years earlier, three young imperial generals from the capital of Constantinople had stopped at Myra harbor for several days while sailing on official duty. During their stay in that seaside city, all three had witnessed with their own eyes that extraordinary morning outside the west gate when Bishop Nicholas stepped forward to stay the executioner's sword and clear three innocent gardeners. The image of the red-cloaked elder standing calm amid a ring of soldiers stayed carved deep inside their memory for many years afterward.

Yet when they returned to serve inside the grand palace at Constantinople, those three upright officers unwittingly offended several greedy nobles. Envying their honesty, the wicked courtiers invented false tales accusing the three generals before Emperor Constantine of secretly plotting treason. Without taking time to investigate thoroughly, the furious emperor stripped them of rank, threw all three friends into the deepest, darkest stone dungeon beneath the earth, and set their execution for the following morning.

On their final night inside the freezing dungeon, there burned not a single torch—only the steady drip of water down moss-grown stone walls and wind howling through iron bars high overhead. With not one kinsman or friend in the capital daring to speak in their defense, the three generals sat leaning back to back in thick darkness. Amid their despair, the youngest suddenly remembered the clear, bright eyes of the Bishop in that distant seaside city. Together in the dark, the three clasped their hands and softly prayed that justice might not be buried behind those heavy stone walls.

That very night, inside his splendid palace chamber above ground, Emperor Constantine tossed restlessly upon his brocade-covered bed. Just as he drifted into uneasy sleep, a soft golden light bloomed right beside the head of his bed. In a dream as clear as broad daylight, the emperor saw a silver-haired old man wrapped in a warm dark-red cloak, holding a brightly burning candle in one hand and looking straight into his eyes.

"Emperor, why have you so heartlessly imprisoned and condemned to death three honest men who have committed no crime?" the old man's warm yet commanding voice echoed through the dream. "Awake this very night and examine that case again with fairness, or else justice and conscience themselves shall speak judgment against you!"

Emperor Constantine started wide awake in the middle of the night, brow drenched in cold sweat, the vision of the red-cloaked elder holding the candle still shining clear before his eyes. Too shaken to wait for daybreak, the emperor quickly threw on his royal robes and ordered guards with blazing torches down into the stone dungeon to bring the three generals straight up to the great hall for questioning.

Through half the night by flickering torchlight, every straightforward answer from the three generals matched down to the smallest detail, exposing the entire shameful plot of the lying courtiers. Seeing how nearly he had committed a terrible wrong, Emperor Constantine hurriedly stood up to restore freedom, honor, and rank to all three men. Hearing them speak with deep emotion of the Bishop of Myra in their midnight prayer, the emperor felt profound reverence and commanded servants to prepare gold-embroidered ceremonial robes and precious vessels for the three generals to carry back to Myra in gratitude.

A few weeks later, when their ship docked at familiar Myra harbor, the three generals walked to the church steps and knelt to place the emperor's magnificent gifts into Bishop Nicholas's hands. The silver-haired elder merely smiled kindly as he raised them to their feet, accepted the costly treasures, and promptly sold or gave away every single item that very day to buy warm blankets, medicine, and bread for sick elders and poor families all along the harbor.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Contrast between two levels:** Read the cold, damp darkness of the stone dungeon slowly and quietly; when shifting to the dream inside the palace, adopt a solemn, resonant voice for the red-cloaked elder holding the candle.
- **The emperor's alarm:** Pick up the pace slightly when the emperor wakes in fright at midnight and sends torchbearers down into the dungeon.
- **A quiet ending in Myra:** Read calmly and matter-of-factly how Nicholas receives the royal treasures and gives every piece away that very day, without adding a single word of commentary.

### Chapter 9: A Peaceful Evening at Life's End in Myra

Springs blooming with olive blossoms and winters swept by sea winds followed one another gently across Myra. Quietly time crowned the Bishop of Myra's head with hair silver-white like frost upon distant mountain peaks, and his long beard now shone soft and smooth as pure sheep's wool. Though his feet could no longer run as swiftly as on that morning long ago outside the west gate, old Nicholas's eyes kept the same clear, warm light as when he was a boy watching seagulls at Patara harbor.

During the crisp early days of December, when pale golden afternoon sunshine spread across the stone steps before the church porch, the old man often came out to sit upon his familiar wooden chair. Once lessons in net-mending or helping mothers kindle kitchen fires were done, children from all across Myra flocked to sit gathered round his knees—one resting a head against his lap, another folding arms and gazing up eagerly to listen. He told them stories of ships weathering storms upon starry seas and of a winter star in a far-off land, pausing now and then to reach into the cloth pouch at his side and share out handfuls of fragrant roasted chestnuts to every small palm.

Then came an afternoon on the sixth day of December when the kindhearted old father felt his strength gently lowering, like a sail that has finished a long voyage folding quietly down as the ship glides into harbor. He lay resting peacefully upon a simple wooden bed placed beside an open window facing the deep blue sea.

Gathered round his bed were the young friends who had learned from him how to share bread with the hungry, weathered sailors with long beards and sun-darkened skin, mothers who had once received tiny shoes upon their porches, and even little children standing on tiptoe at the doorway. Nicholas reached out his thin yet still-warm hand to clasp each person's hand in turn, his voice soft as a breeze drifting through olive leaves yet every word crystal clear:

"Do not be sad when I travel far away. True light does not live inside locked chests of gold, nor in noisy words of praise. It lives always inside hands ready to share silently whenever you see someone standing out in the cold."

Outside the window, the afternoon sun slowly touched the calm surface of the sea, painting a wide band of the far horizon rose pink. At the exact moment the bronze bell atop the high tower began tolling its long, warm evening chime across Myra bay, old Nicholas smiled gently, closed his eyes, and entered peaceful rest.

That evening, as night mist drifted down to cloak the narrow alleys, the whole seaside city lay hushed without a single sound. Then from wooden cottages beside the pier to stone-roofed houses high upon the hillsides, every household struck a light and set a small candle inside its half-open window. Hundreds and thousands of warm golden points of light shimmered softly across the dark waters of the bay, quietly seeing Myra's gentle father home to rest.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Slow and warm as late afternoon sunlight:** Read the scene of the old bishop sitting on the wooden porch telling stories and handing out chestnuts in a truly unhurried, kindly tone.
- **Parting words:** Read Nicholas's final message from the wooden bed very slowly, softly, yet clearly word for word, keeping calm serenity free of sorrowfulness.
- **Night of a thousand candles:** Soften your voice gently as the evening bell tolls on December 6, and pause for two or three seconds after the image of candles glowing along the dark bay.

## Part II: The Winter Legend Journey Around the World

### Chapter 10: From the Red Cloak of Myra to Santa Claus on Christmas Eve

After that peaceful afternoon on the sixth day of December, the story of the dark-red-cloaked Bishop of Myra never closed behind the stone walls of his seaside town. Every year when early winter winds began blowing cold, merchant vessels sailing from the Mediterranean lifted their sails and carried that memory across narrow straits northward into lands covered with snow.

Sitting round glowing braziers upon freezing European harbors, sailors told wide-eyed children the story of the gentle saint who had dropped three bags of gold into stockings hanging beside the hearth and kept wheat holds from losing a single grain. That warm tale traveled through snow-heavy pine forests and lingered long in the green land of the Netherlands, with its tranquil canals and rows of windmills turning steadily in the winter mist.

There, children lovingly softened the name *Nicholas* into a tender, joyful name: *Sinterklaas*. In the innocent imagination of Dutch children every early December, silver-bearded Sinterklaas still wore his long red cloak arriving from the sea by ship, then rode a snow-white horse stepping lightly across steep snow-covered tiled roofs. Before going to bed, little ones arranged their clean wooden shoes neatly beside the warm fireplace, tucking a handful of fragrant hay and a crisp carrot inside to refresh the white horse after his long journey. Waking at daybreak, hay and carrot were gone, replaced by spiced gingerbread fragrant with cinnamon, sweet chestnuts, and charming little wooden toys.

Then centuries rolled onward. Tall sailing ships carrying Dutch families left old harbors, braving fierce Atlantic waves toward a new land around the port of New Amsterdam. On winter nights when thick snow fell heavily outside the windowpanes, children from everywhere gathered round brick hearths and spoke the name *Sinterklaas* in a softer, merrier new tongue: *Santa Claus*. And from every corner of the world right into our own homes today, children everywhere still fondly call him by that beloved name—Santa Claus.

Through a journey of nearly two thousand years across blue seas and snowy forests, the deep crimson cloak of the Bishop of Myra gained a trim of snow-white fur; his beard grew long and silver-white like morning clouds on high mountains; his shepherd's staff transformed into a heavy sack brimming with warm surprises. That image stayed woven forever into the twenty-fourth night of December—when green fir trees shine with candlelight and woolen stockings are hung neatly once again in expectation of wonder.

And so the flame that first kindled at the Beginning of Creation, traveled through the bright star in the sky above Bethlehem, through the wood-shaving-scented carpenter's workshop in Nazareth, to a half-open midnight window in Patara and the merry chime of bells across white snow, has never once gone out. For wherever on this earth a quiet hand places joy into someone else's life without asking for a name, without waiting for praise, there the red-cloaked Bishop of long ago—our beloved Santa Claus—is still walking softly through the warm winter night.

#### Storytelling Notes
- **Shifting from sea to snow:** Tell how sailors carry the story northward in a gradually livelier voice, evoking a long voyage across centuries.
- **Playful warmth in the Dutch wooden-shoe scene:** Lightly bring out children's eagerness as they tuck hay and carrots into wooden shoes and awake next morning to gingerbread and toys.
- **Closing the entire epic:** Beginning at *"And so the flame that first kindled at the Beginning of Creation..."*, read very slowly, solemnly, and warmly, bringing all five books seamlessly home into the listener's heart today.
